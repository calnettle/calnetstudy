# Topic 4 — The Capitalisation Method

Weeks 4 and 5, plus the Week 3 tutorial's retail exercise. The unit's
second learning outcome puts this topic in capital letters —
*UNDERSTAND AND APPLY THE CAPITALISATION METHOD* — and it is the largest
single block of the mid-semester exam.

The method is two lines long:

```
NOI   = Gross Income − Operating Expenses

Value = NOI / Capitalisation rate
```

Everything else is the argument about what belongs in each of those two
numbers.

## The income approach

IVSC 2025: "The income approach provides an indication of value by
converting projected cash flows to a single current value. Under the
income approach, the value of an asset is determined by reference to the
value of income, cash flow or cost savings generated by the asset."

All property purchased on the basis of the return received from net rental
or net profit income is valued by an income method. The main one is
**capitalisation of net rents (profit)**. The idea is to convert the
anticipated benefits of ownership into a capital sum.

Two halves feed the estimate:

| Income stream | Value estimates |
|---|---|
| Likely market rent | Valuation method |
| Lease terms and conditions | Suitable market evidence |
| Future profitability | Underlying valuation factors |
| Risk–return | Current market issues |

Three background frameworks the deck lists and the exam likes as short
questions:

| Factors affecting value | Forces of value change | Depreciation |
|---|---|---|
| Demand | Political forces | Physical obsolescence |
| Utility / usefulness | Economic forces | Functional obsolescence |
| Scarcity | Supply forces | Economic obsolescence |
| Transferability | Demographic forces | Technological obsolescence |

## The NOI ladder

Memorise this in order. Every capitalisation question is a request to fill
it in.

```
    Estimate    Rental Income
    Add         Other Income Sources
    Deduct      Vacancy Allowances
                ─────────────────────────
                Gross Operating Income
    Deduct      Operating Expenses
                ─────────────────────────
                Net Operating Income (NOI)
    Determine   Appropriate Capitalisation Rate
    Calculate   Market Value (Capitalise NOI)
```

### Rental income

Per Storey (2015), rental income is the maximum amount of income a
property would produce **when 100% of the rental space is leased** in any
given market. It is based on current market rent or contract/lease rent.
Also called *potential gross revenue*, *potential gross income*, or
*gross potential income*.

```
Rental Income = rent per m² × net lettable area (NLA)
```

**Example.** A 13,000 m² office block in Brisbane CBD, market rent
$800/m² p.a.

```
Rental Income = 800 × 13,000
              = $10,400,000 p.a.
```

Factors to take into account when applying market rent: terms and
conditions of the lease, vacancies, permitted use, and valuation date.
Explicitly **excluded** from market rent: goodwill, the tenant's fit-out,
and sub-tenancy arrangements.

> **Do not value the tenant's own fit-out.** A tenant who spent
> $1.2m fitting out a trading floor has not made the landlord's building
> worth $1.2m more, and the rent the landlord can charge the *next* tenant
> is unaffected. Goodwill and sub-tenancy income belong to the occupier,
> not the reversion.

### Face rent

The quoted base rental rate **before** taking any rent increases or
incentives into account. It may — and more likely may not — be market
rent. It is stated in the lease as either gross or net.

### Gross rent versus net rent

| | **Gross rent** | **Net rent** |
|---|---|---|
| Lessee pays | Rent, and $0 outgoings in addition | Base rent plus part or all of outgoings |
| Lessor pays | Operational costs, taxes, insurance, structural repairs | Whatever the lease does not push to the tenant |

Net leases come in tiers:

| | Base rent | Operational costs | Taxes | Insurance | Structural repairs |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Net (single-net)** | ✓ | ✓ | ✓ | | |
| **NN (double-net)** | ✓ | ✓ | ✓ | ✓ | |
| **NNN (triple-net)** | ✓ | ✓ | ✓ | ✓ | ✓ |

The Week 2 tutorial's framing is the clearest one: under a gross lease the
**landlord bears any increase in outgoings**; under a net lease the
**tenant bears cost fluctuations**, which gives the landlord more
predictable income. On a $100 gross deal the landlord keeps whatever is
left after outgoings rise; on a $60 net deal with $40 estimated outgoings
the landlord is guaranteed their $60.

**Gross rental income example.** Tank Stream Labs sub-leased 1,024 m² on
level 7 of Wynyard Green for three years at a gross rate of about
$895/m².

```
Gross Rental Income = 895 × 1,024
                    = $916,480 p.a.
```

**The question you cannot answer without an assumption.** *A lessor
receives a rent of $150,000 p.a.; outgoings are $25,000 p.a. What is the
net rent?* The deck's own hint: you must make an assumption, because you
cannot answer without knowing who pays the outgoings.

```
If the LESSOR pays outgoings:
    the $150,000 is a GROSS rent
    → net rent = 150,000 − 25,000 = $125,000

If the LESSEE pays outgoings:
    the $150,000 is the base rent and the tenant pays $25,000 on top
    → net rent  = base rent + outgoings recovered
                = 150,000 + 25,000
                = $175,000
```

> **The deck only gives the second branch, and it is the counter-intuitive
> one.** Adding outgoings to get a *net* rent looks backwards until you see
> that the "net" here means net **to the landlord** — the landlord banks
> $150,000 of rent *and* $25,000 of recovered outgoings, and has no
> outgoings expense. State your assumption in one line before you
> calculate and you get the mark whichever branch the marker wanted.

### Other income sources

Income not generated directly from rental payments: billboard and signage
fees, parking fees, late fees and penalties, licence fees from forfeited
rent deposits, vending machines, ATMs, pop-up stores, aerial towers.

> **Other income is capitalised differently, and the deck says so in one
> easily-missed sentence.** These "may well be substantial amounts, so
> don't overlook them, but they should be treated as **terminable income,
> being of the nature of chattels**." An ATM licence is not a lease and
> does not run with the land. Capitalising a $40,000 vending contract at
> the building's 7% cap rate books $571,000 of value for an agreement that
> can be cancelled on thirty days' notice.

### Vacancy allowances

Vacant lettable space. You reflect churn by looking at the tenancy
schedule to determine the frequency of tenancy change in the building —
always case by case. **Excluded** from the vacancy allowance: space under
refurbishment, and space that fails Occupational Health & Safety
standards.

The **vacancy rate** is the percentage of NLA in a property that is vacant
or unoccupied at the valuation date. It can be based on the property's
historical performance, competing properties, or natural vacancy.

```
Method 1 — Vacancy rate
    Vacancy rate = unoccupied space / lettable area × 100

Method 2 — Loss of rent
    Loss of rent = gross income × vacancy rate
```

**Worked example — Milton SP.** The property generates $535,500 a year in
base rent (gross) when fully let. Estimate the revenue loss from vacancy
using comparable market information.

| Comparable | NLA (m²) | Vacant (m²) | Vacancy rate |
|---|---|---|---|
| Milton A | 730 | 50 | 6.8% |
| Milton B | 500 | 35 | 7.0% |
| Milton C | 800 | 55 | 6.9% |
| Milton D | 425 | 30 | 7.1% |
| **Market vacancy rate** | | | **6.9% → adopt 7%** |

```
50/730 = 6.849%    35/500 = 7.000%
55/800 = 6.875%    30/425 = 7.059%

Unweighted mean = 6.946%  →  6.9%  →  adopt 7%

Loss of rent = 535,500 × 7%
             = $37,485
```

The deck's instruction alongside the table — "think about the trend, range
and pattern of property transactions" — is what separates a valuer from a
spreadsheet. Four comparables in a 6.8%–7.1% band is a tight, coherent
pattern, which is why rounding to 7% is defensible.

**Uncollectable rent** is a separate allowance, for tenant defaults, late
payment and non-payment. Do not fold it into the vacancy rate.

### Gross Operating Income

```
        Gross Rental Income
Add     Other Income Sources
Less    Vacancy Allowances
        ───────────────────
        Gross Operating Income
```

**Worked example — Milton SP.** Base rent derived from gross market rent;
7% average vacancy; $20,000 earned from signage.

| Level | Rent ($/m²) | Area (m²) | Gross rent p.a. | Car bays | Car parking p.a. |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Ground | $720 | 200 | $144,000 | 5 | $38,500 |
| Level 1 | $700 | 200 | $140,000 | 5 | $38,500 |
| Level 2 | $680 | 200 | $136,000 | 5 | $38,500 |
| **Total** | | **600** | **$420,000** | **15** | **$115,500** |

```
Base rent                    $ 420,000
Add: Car Parking             $ 115,500
                             ─────────
                             $ 535,500
Less: Vacancy @ 7%           $  37,485
Add: Signage                 $  20,000
                             ─────────
Gross Operating Income       $ 518,015
```

All of it reconciles: `200 × 720 = 144,000`, `200 × 700 = 140,000`,
`200 × 680 = 136,000`, summing to $420,000; car parking is
`3 × 38,500 = $115,500`, which is $7,700 per bay per year or $641.67 per
bay per month.

> **The order of those lines changes the answer, and the deck's own
> template disagrees with its own worked example.** The template says
> `Gross Rental + Other Income − Vacancy`. The worked example applies the
> 7% vacancy to `rent + parking` **only**, then adds the $20,000 signage
> afterwards, giving $518,015. Apply the vacancy to the full $555,500 —
> which is what the template literally says — and you get
> `555,500 × 0.93 = $516,615`, **$1,400 lower**. Capitalised at 7%, that is
> $20,000 of value. The worked example's order is the defensible one:
> signage income does not disappear because a tenancy is empty. Follow the
> worked example, and write one line saying why.

### Operating expenses

Outgoings are necessary to maintain the functionality and competitive
advantage of the property, and are deducted from gross operating income.

| **Fixed** — do not vary with occupancy in the short run | **Variable** — vary with occupancy | **Other** — large or multi-tenanted properties |
|---|---|---|
| Land tax | Utilities: water, electricity, fire protection | Promotion fees |
| Property tax | HVAC, lifts and escalators | Legal fees on leases |
| Council rates | Repairs and maintenance | Valuation fees |
| Government charges: water, sewerage, garbage | Suppliers: pest control, cleaning, security, landscaping, sanitary disposal | Management fees |
| Insurance premium | Miscellaneous | Postage and sundries |

The first four fixed items are **statutory outgoings** — the ones a retail
lease is allowed to recover under the Retail Shop Leases Act. That
distinction (statutory versus general outgoings) appears in the lease
synopsis in Topic 3 and matters for what you can add back.

## The capitalisation rate

The property-specific term for the annual return on capital invested in a
property at a given date. It is a **measure of yield derived from the
analysis of comparable sales transactions**, and it reflects the ratio of
income to value.

```
Cap rate = NOI / Value
Value    = NOI / Cap rate
NOI      = Cap rate × Value
```

The three-way arrangement is worth writing out in an exam because it shows
the marker you know which variable is being solved for.

**What the cap rate actually measures: risk.** "The higher the risk of
maintaining the current level of net rent or profit, then the higher the
capitalisation rate." Cap rate and capital value are on a seesaw — higher
yield, lower capital value.

Risk decomposes two ways:

| | Type | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Micro** | Unsystematic / specific risk | Business risk, financial risk, operational risk |
| **Macro** | Systematic / market risk | Interest rate risk, foreign exchange risk, sovereign risk |

Levels of risk and uncertainty are reflected in the **all-risks yield
(ARY)**, the market yield and the capitalisation rate — one number
incorporating all the risks, uncertainties and potential benefits.
Participants show three attitudes to risk: **risk aversion, risk
neutrality, risk acceptance**.

**Worked example.** An industrial building bought for $6.5 million with
NOI of $590,000.

```
Cap rate = 590,000 / 6,500,000
         = 9.077%  →  9.08%
```

Same building, same income, sold at a 6.2% cap rate:

```
Value = 590,000 / 0.062
      = $9,516,129
```

```
NOI      = $590,000        NOI      = $590,000
Cap Rate = 9.08%           Cap Rate = 6.20%
Value    = $6,500,000      Value    = $9,516,129
```

"The more stable the income stream, the lower the risk, then the lower the
capitalisation rate." A 288-basis-point compression adds **$3,016,129** —
**46.4%** — to the value of an income that has not changed by a cent.

> **The cap rate is the most sensitive input in the entire unit, and
> nothing else comes close.** Differentiate `V = NOI/r` and the elasticity
> of value with respect to `r` is exactly −1: a 1% *relative* change in the
> cap rate moves value by 1% in the opposite direction. But cap rates are
> quoted in quarter-percent steps, and a quarter of a percent on a 6% rate
> is a **4.2% relative** move. One notch of judgement — 6.00% versus
> 6.25% — is worth 4% of the valuation. That is why the tutorial insists
> you justify the rate from sales evidence rather than asserting it.

### Choosing a rate — the convention

> **"When choosing a cap rate always go the nearest quarter i.e. 8.0%,
> 8.25%, 8.5% or 8.75%."** The Week 3 tutorial says this twice, on two
> separate slides, which is as close to an exam instruction as a slide
> deck gets. An answer of "8.37%" is wrong even if your arithmetic is
> perfect. And there is a matching convention on the value: "for rounding
> in commercial, it is usually common practice to round **$25,000**
> (similar to the 25 basis points in the capitalisation rate)."

## Year's Purchase

The multiplier form of the same relationship. YP reflects **the number of
years the net rent will take to repay the purchase price**.

```
                    Capital value (CV)
Year's Purchase = ----------------------
                    Net income (NI)

Capital value   = Net income × Year's Purchase

YP in perpetuity = 1 / cap rate
```

The five-step Year's Purchase approach:

1. Determine the current net annual rent as at the date of sale, and check
   the rent at date of sale was actually market-based.
2. Collect evidence of comparable rents and sales, and analyse them to
   establish a pattern of both net rents and cap rates/yields.
3. Calculate the YP (multiplier) for each comparable.
4. Estimate the return the client would require on a similar property,
   using YP in perpetuity for a freehold interest.
5. Calculate the subject's value using the market capitalisation rate.

**Worked example.** Market research identifies four comparables for
Milton SP, selected on use, location, utility value (age, condition,
design), quality of tenant and lease term.

| Property | Net income | Sale price | YP (multiplier) | Cap rate |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Milton A | $118,260 | $1,478,250 | 12.50 | 8.00% |
| Milton B | $97,500 | $1,147,050 | 11.76 | 8.50% |
| Milton C | $148,800 | $1,814,630 | 12.20 | 8.20% |
| Milton D | $88,825 | $1,009,378 | 11.36 | 8.80% |
| **Milton SP** | **$112,788** | **??** | | |

Every YP and cap rate reproduces:
`1,478,250 / 118,260 = 12.500` and `118,260 / 1,478,250 = 8.000%`;
`1,147,050 / 97,500 = 11.765`; `1,814,630 / 148,800 = 12.196`;
`1,009,378 / 88,825 = 11.363`. And `1/0.08 = 12.5` ✓ — YP in perpetuity
and cap rate are reciprocals.

Applying three risk-profile rates to Milton SP's $112,788:

| | Core | Core Plus | Opportunistic |
|---|---|---|---|
| NOI p.a. | $112,788 | $112,788 | $112,788 |
| Capitalisation rate | 8.00% | 8.50% | 8.80% |
| Capital value | **$1,409,850** | $1,326,918 | $1,281,682 |
| Year's Purchase | 12.50 | 11.76 | 11.36 |

> **The deck's Core figure is $1,409,840; the correct figure is
> $1,409,850.** `112,788 / 0.08 = 1,409,850` and `112,788 × 12.5 =
> 1,409,850` — both routes agree, so the deck has a $10 transcription
> error. The other two columns are right to the dollar
> (`112,788/0.085 = 1,326,917.65` and `112,788/0.088 = 1,281,681.82`). It
> is a trivial error, but it is worth reproducing the multiplication in an
> exam so you can show the marker your two routes agree.

"Cap rates and price/earnings multiples are inversely related. As the cap
rate goes up, the valuation multiple goes down." The Core-to-Opportunistic
spread of 80 basis points costs **$128,168**, or **9.1%** of the Core
value, on income that never changes.

## Lease incentives

A bonus or discount offered to a tenant in consideration of their entry
into a lease. Forms:

- Free fit-out expenses and/or plant
- **Rent-free periods** — upfront from lease commencement
- **Rental abatement** — a discount spread equally over the lease term
- Surrender / take-back premises or break clauses
- Capping or removal of the service charge
- Others: relocation within the same building, advertisements, signage

Motives for granting them: secure a tenant in the face of competition;
take advantage of increased capital value; cash-flow advantage for the
tenant; taxation; impact on a company's balance sheet.

That second motive is the one worth understanding. A landlord who lets at
a **face** rent of $500/m² with a 30% incentive is economically letting at
$350/m², but the *face* rent is what a naive purchaser capitalises. Face
rent inflates the apparent value; the incentive is the hidden liability.

### Impact on capital value

The deck gives a formula and a worked table that do not produce the same
answer, and you need to be able to handle both.

```
Formula:  Total incentive = Lease term × annual rental × lease incentive %
```

**Example.** Five-year lease, NLA 200 m², face rent $500/m² p.a.,
incentive 25%, effective rent $375/m², cap rate 8%.

```
Face annual income      = 200 × 500 = $100,000
Effective annual income = 200 × 375 = $ 75,000

Route A — capitalise the EFFECTIVE rent
    Value = 75,000 / 0.08
          = $937,500

Route B — capitalise the FACE rent, deduct the incentive below the line
    Capitalised face  = 100,000 / 0.08          = $1,250,000
    Total incentive   = 5 × 100,000 × 25%       = $  125,000
    Value             = 1,250,000 − 125,000     = $1,125,000
```

> **Route A and Route B differ by $187,500 — 20% of the answer — and the
> deck does not reconcile them.** The reason is that Route A capitalises
> the discount **in perpetuity**, as though every future tenant will also
> get 25% off, while Route B charges the incentive **once**, over the
> five-year term it actually applies to. Route B is the correct treatment
> for a below-the-line adjustment on a single lease; Route A is
> appropriate only when the incentive level *is* the market norm and will
> recur at every reletting. The deck says "refer to the Excel
> spreadsheet", and that spreadsheet is not in the unit folder, so the
> intended answer cannot be confirmed from the material. Show both, name
> the assumption behind each, and ask your tutor which the exam wants.

**Rent-free example.** A property has recently been let on a five-year
lease at $50,000 p.a. with a **six-month rent-free period** at
commencement. What is the present value to the landlord of the rental
income over five years at an all-risks yield of 6%?

```
PV of the full five years, annually in arrears
    = 50,000 × (1 − 1.06⁻⁵) / 0.06
    = 50,000 × 4.212364
    = $210,618.19

With Year 1 reduced to $25,000 (six months rent-free)
    Year 1  25,000 / 1.06      = $ 23,584.91
    Year 2  50,000 / 1.06²     = $ 44,499.82
    Year 3  50,000 / 1.06³     = $ 41,980.96
    Year 4  50,000 / 1.06⁴     = $ 39,604.68
    Year 5  50,000 / 1.06⁵     = $ 37,362.91
                                 ────────────
                                 $187,033.28

Cost of the incentive = 210,618.19 − 187,033.28 = $23,584.91
```

The cost of the incentive is exactly the discounted value of the six
months forgone — which is the sanity check that tells you the model is
right. On a monthly-in-arrears basis (rent nil for months 1–6, then
$4,166.67 for 54 months, discounted at the effective monthly equivalent of
6%) the PV is **$191,769.70**; the annual approximation understates the
landlord's position by $4,736 because it defers the whole first year's
rent to year-end. Say which convention you used.

## Below-the-line adjustments

Where appropriate, the indicative capitalised value is adjusted to reflect:

- **Immediate capital expenditure (capex)**, if required to sustain the
  current income
- **Adequate allowance for "letting up"** to fully lease the property
- **The value of the reversionary income** — the difference between
  passing rental and market rental

These sit *below* the capitalisation line because they are one-off capital
sums, not perpetual income. Capitalising a one-off cost is the single
biggest structural error a student makes in this topic.

## Over-rented and under-rented property

### Over rent

The amount by which passing rent exceeds market rent in a leasehold
interest (API). From the tenant's point of view it is **profit rent**;
from the lessor's it is a **reversionary interest**. Profit rent
terminates at a market rent review or at the end of the lease, and may
occasionally be capitalised.

**Four steps:**

1. Determine the amount of excess rent.
2. Adjust (PV) the excess until lease expiry or market rent review,
   assuming the rent reverts to market at expiry.
3. Capitalise the **market** net income.
4. Add the PV of the excess rent to the indicative value.

**Worked example.** Current net income $250,000 p.a.; market net income
$150,000 p.a.; cap rate 8%; lease expires in 12 months.

```
Current Net Income          $ 250,000.00  pa
Less: Market Net Income     $ 150,000.00  pa
                            ─────────────
Over Market Rent            $ 100,000.00  pa
                            = $8,333.33 monthly

Interest rate               0.67% monthly  (8% / 12)
Period                      12 months
PV of Over Market Rent      $  95,798.18

Market Net Income           $ 150,000.00
Divide: Cap rate                     8%
                            ─────────────
Indicative Value            $1,875,000.00
Add: PV Over Market Rent    $   95,798.18
                            ─────────────
Market Value                $1,970,798.18
```

### Under rent (rental shortfall)

The amount by which passing rent is less than market rent (API). Same four
steps, with step 4 **deducting** the shortfall.

**Worked example.** Current net income $100,000 p.a.; market $150,000;
cap rate 8%; lease expires in 12 months.

```
Under Market Rent          −$ 50,000.00  pa  = −$4,166.67 monthly
PV of Under Market Rent    −$ 47,899.09

Indicative Value            $1,875,000.00
Less: PV Under Market Rent −$    47,899.09
                            ─────────────
Market Value                $1,827,100.91
```

The two PVs are exactly proportional — $47,899.09 is precisely half of
$95,798.18, because $50,000 is half of $100,000 — which is a useful
internal check on the deck's figures.

### The summary that ties it together

| Assessment | Under-rented | Passing = Market | Over-rented |
|---|---|---|---|
| Indicative Value | $1,875,000.00 | $1,875,000.00 | $1,875,000.00 |
| Adjustment | −$47,899.09 | – | +$95,798.18 |
| **Market Value** | **$1,827,100.91** | **$1,875,000.00** | **$1,970,798.18** |

> **Capitalise the market income, then adjust — never capitalise the
> passing income.** The deck's "impacts of potential error" slide shows
> what happens if you get this wrong. Capitalise the under-rented
> property's own $100,000 and you get **$1,250,000** instead of
> $1,827,101 — a **31.6% undervaluation**. Capitalise the over-rented
> property's $250,000 and you get **$3,125,000** instead of $1,970,798 —
> a **58.6% overvaluation**. Both errors come from the same single mistake:
> treating a temporary rent as a perpetual one.

Behind all of it is the law of demand and supply: if supply is low and
demand high, higher rent can be obtained; if supply is high and demand
low, passing rents are made competitive to attract tenants.

## A complete worked valuation

The Week 3 tutorial's retail exercise, end to end. This is the shape of a
full-mark exam answer.

```
Gross Income                                              $228,000
Less: Outgoings                                           $ 62,000
Less: Permanent vacancy allowance (3%)                    $  6,840
                                                          ─────────
Net income                                                $159,160

Capitalised net income at 8.5%                            $1,872,471

Capital adjustments:
    Leasing up vacant shop          $10,000
    Incentive allowance             $ 4,000
    Agents commission               $ 2,500
                                    ───────
    Total capital adjustments                             $  16,500
                                                          ─────────
Capitalised value                                         $1,855,971

Rounded                                                   $1,850,000
```

Checks: `228,000 × 3% = $6,840` ✓; `228,000 − 62,000 − 6,840 = $159,160` ✓;
`159,160 / 0.085 = $1,872,470.59` ✓; `1,872,470.59 − 16,500 =
$1,855,970.59` ✓; rounded to the nearest $25,000 gives **$1,850,000** ✓.

The tutorial's own commentary on why 8.5% was chosen is the part worth
copying:

> "In this example I made the cap rate slightly higher than the analysed
> yield in the sale because the subject property had a vacant shop and a
> lower WALE."

That is one sentence, and it does three things: it names the comparable,
it names the two differences, and it explains the direction of the
adjustment. Write that sentence in every valuation you produce.

> **Be consistent between how you analysed the sale and how you apply the
> rate.** The tutorial's rule: "if you analyse the sales by deducting
> outgoings and adding a vacancy allowance then you need to be consistent
> and treat the valuation the same way, i.e. use the analysed yield to
> determine the capitalisation rate." An analysed yield built on
> market income net of vacancy must be applied to a subject NOI built the
> same way. Mixing an initial yield from the sale with a market NOI on the
> subject is the most common way to be 20% wrong while showing perfect
> arithmetic.

## Summary

- `NOI = Gross Income − Operating Expenses`;
  `Value = NOI / Cap rate`; `YP = CV / NI = 1 / cap rate`.
- The ladder: rental income → add other income → deduct vacancy → gross
  operating income → deduct operating expenses → NOI → apply cap rate →
  market value.
- Gross rent means the landlord pays outgoings; net rent means the tenant
  does. You cannot compute a net rent without knowing which — state the
  assumption.
- Vacancy comes from comparables and is applied to rent and parking, not
  to signage. Uncollectable rent is a separate allowance.
- The cap rate is a measure of risk derived from sales, quoted to the
  nearest quarter percent. A quarter percent is worth roughly 4% of value.
- Incentives can be handled as an effective rent (capitalised in
  perpetuity) or as a below-the-line deduction (charged once). They are
  not equivalent — the deck's own example differs by 20%.
- Over- and under-rented property: capitalise the **market** income, then
  add or deduct the PV of the difference until expiry. Never capitalise
  the passing rent.
- Round the value to the nearest $25,000.

<details><summary>Checkpoint — nine questions</summary>

1. A property has gross rental income of $640,000, other income of
   $35,000, a vacancy rate of 5.5% (applied to rent only) and operating
   expenses of $172,000. Compute the NOI.
2. Apply a 7.5% cap rate to that NOI and round it commercially.
3. A landlord receives $220,000 p.a. and outgoings are $48,000. What is
   the net rent? Answer both branches.
4. Four comparables show vacancies of 42/620, 30/450, 61/880 and 25/390.
   What market vacancy rate do you adopt?
5. A sale at $2,880,000 on a net income of $237,600. What are the cap rate
   and the YP?
6. A property is over-rented: passing $340,000, market $295,000, cap rate
   6.75%, lease expiring in 18 months, rent monthly in arrears. What is
   the market value?
7. What would that property be worth if you wrongly capitalised the
   passing rent, and by what percentage would you be out?
8. Face rent $620/m² on 850 m², a 5-year lease, 30% incentive, cap rate
   7%. Value it both ways.
9. Why is a vending-machine licence not capitalised at the building cap
   rate?

---

**Answers**

1. `Vacancy = 640,000 × 5.5% = $35,200`.
   `GOI = 640,000 − 35,200 + 35,000 = $639,800`.
   `NOI = 639,800 − 172,000 = $467,800`
2. `467,800 / 0.075 = $6,237,333.33` → round to the nearest $25,000 →
   **$6,225,000**
3. If the **lessor** pays outgoings, $220,000 is gross and the net rent is
   `220,000 − 48,000 = $172,000`. If the **lessee** pays them, $220,000 is
   base rent and the net rent to the landlord is
   `220,000 + 48,000 = $268,000`.
4. `42/620 = 6.774%`; `30/450 = 6.667%`; `61/880 = 6.932%`;
   `25/390 = 6.410%`. Mean `= 6.696%` → adopt **6.7%**, or 7% if the
   deck's rounding habit is being followed. The 6.41% outlier is worth a
   sentence — a 390 m² comparable is the smallest and least reliable.
5. `Cap rate = 237,600 / 2,880,000 = 8.25%`.
   `YP = 2,880,000 / 237,600 = 12.121`, and `1 / 0.0825 = 12.121` ✓
6. `Excess = 340,000 − 295,000 = $45,000 p.a. = $3,750/month`.
   `Monthly rate = 0.0675/12 = 0.5625%`, `n = 18`.
   `PV = 3,750 × (1 − 1.005625⁻¹⁸)/0.005625 = 3,750 × 17.073156 =
   $64,024.33`.
   `Indicative value = 295,000 / 0.0675 = $4,370,370.37`.
   `Market value = 4,370,370.37 + 64,024.33 = $4,434,394.70` → round to
   **$4,425,000**.
7. `340,000 / 0.0675 = $5,037,037.04`, against the correct $4,434,395.
   That is **13.6% too high** — `5,037,037 / 4,434,395 − 1 = 0.1359`.
8. `Face income = 850 × 620 = $527,000`.
   **Route A**: effective rent `= 620 × 0.70 = $434/m²`;
   `850 × 434 = $368,900`; `368,900 / 0.07 = $5,270,000`.
   **Route B**: `527,000 / 0.07 = $7,528,571.43`;
   `total incentive = 5 × 527,000 × 30% = $790,500`;
   `7,528,571.43 − 790,500 = $6,738,071.43`.
   The two differ by **$1,468,071**, 21.8% of the Route B figure. Name
   your assumption.
9. Because it is **terminable income of the nature of a chattel** — it is
   a licence, not a lease, it does not run with the land, and it can be
   cancelled at short notice. Capitalising it in perpetuity books value
   for an income stream that has no perpetual right to exist. Treat it as
   a short-term cash flow, or exclude it and note it.
