# Topic 1 — Foundations of Investment Valuation

Week 1, plus the front half of the Week 4 deck and the Week 2 tutorial.
This is the vocabulary topic. Nothing here is hard, but every term is used
as a load-bearing word later — if you are fuzzy about the difference
between *yield* and *cap rate* in Week 1, you will lose marks in Week 5
when the exam asks you to analyse a sale and then apply the result.

## Why anyone buys property

There are exactly three motives for acquiring an interest in real
property, and the deck expects you to name all three.

| Motive | What the buyer wants | Test |
|---|---|---|
| **Investment** | A return on capital funds — growth on the invested sum so it becomes larger with time | Is the property producing income for someone other than the owner? |
| **Occupation** | Owner or tenant use, residential or business | Is the owner in the building? |
| **Speculation** | A hope of profit on capital invested, taking a calculated risk on the presumption that a considerably larger sum will be recouped in future | Is the buyer relying on a future event (rezoning, cycle turn) rather than current income? |

USB144 is about the first one. The whole unit assumes an
**income-producing asset** — something a third party pays rent to occupy.

## The four ratios

Everything in the unit is one of four ratios. Learn them as a set, because
the exam will hand you three of the four inputs and ask for the missing
one.

```
NOI      = Gross Income − Operating Expenses

Cap rate = NOI / Property Value

Value    = NOI / Cap rate

Yield    = NOI / Capital Value        (the annual income return, as a %
                                       of market value)

ROI      = (Current value of investment − Cost of investment)
           ------------------------------------------------
                        Cost of investment
```

**Gross income** includes rental income, other income (parking, vending,
signage) and is stated *before* vacancy loss. **Operating expenses** are
rates, land tax, insurance, maintenance, utilities and management.

### Worked example — all four in one property

An industrial building is bought for **$6,500,000**. It produces gross
income of **$720,000 p.a.** and incurs operating expenses of
**$130,000 p.a.** Two years later it is worth $7,400,000.

```
NOI      = 720,000 − 130,000
         = $590,000 p.a.

Cap rate = 590,000 / 6,500,000
         = 0.090769
         = 9.08%

Value at a 6.2% market cap rate
         = 590,000 / 0.062
         = $9,516,129

ROI (on the two-year capital movement)
         = (7,400,000 − 6,500,000) / 6,500,000
         = 900,000 / 6,500,000
         = 13.85%
```

Those first three numbers are the Week 5 deck's own example, and they
reconcile: 9.08% and 6.2% applied to the same $590,000 NOI give
$6.5m and $9.52m respectively. That $3m gap on identical income is the
single most important idea in the unit — **the cap rate does most of the
work in a valuation, not the income.**

> **Yield and cap rate are the same arithmetic used in opposite
> directions, and the unit will penalise you for mixing them up.** You
> *derive* a **yield** from a sale — you know the price, you divide the
> income by it. You *apply* a **cap rate** to a subject property — you
> know the income, you divide by the rate to get value. The Week 3
> tutorial says it plainly: "you analyse your sales to determine a yield
> and when you apply that yield to the capitalisation calculation, it is
> called a capitalisation rate." Same number, different job. Write the
> word that matches the direction you are working in.

The Week 1 deck adds one nuance about yield that is easy to miss: yield
measures income against **cost**, cap rate measures income against
**value**. "At the time of purchase, these could be the same, but over
time they will drift apart." The day you buy, price = value. Five years
later your yield-on-cost is fixed while the market cap rate has moved.

## What a valuation report actually is

Four properties of a valuation report, straight from the deck:

1. **A legal document.** Signed by the valuer as at a point in time, and
   built on historical sales transactions.
2. **For a specific purpose** — mortgage security, corporate reporting,
   property investment, divestment, evidence to a court.
3. **A risk assessment** for lending against the asset as security.
4. **Full of assumptions and qualifications**, which are part of the
   document, not decoration.

**Market value** (IVSC definition, which you should be able to reproduce
close to verbatim):

> the estimated amount for which an asset or liability should exchange on
> the valuation date between a willing buyer and a willing seller in an
> arm's length transaction, after proper marketing and where the parties
> had each acted knowledgeably, prudently and without compulsion.

The **market rent** definition (IVSC / API / RICS) is the same sentence
with "leased" swapped for "exchange", "lessor/lessee" for "buyer/seller",
and the extra phrase **"on appropriate lease terms"**:

> the estimated amount for which an interest in real property should be
> leased on the date of valuation between a willing lessor and a willing
> lessee on appropriate lease terms in an arm's length transaction, after
> proper marketing and where the parties had each acted knowledgeably,
> prudently and without compulsion.

> **"On appropriate lease terms" is the phrase that earns the mark.** It
> is the only substantive difference between the two definitions and it is
> the whole basis of Topic 3 — a rent is only comparable evidence if the
> lease terms behind it are comparable. A $200/m² net rent on a ten-year
> lease with a 4% fixed review is not evidence for a $200/m² gross rent on
> a three-year lease.

### Purposes of a valuation

| Sale or purchase | Statutory | Other |
|---|---|---|
| Private treaty | Compulsory acquisition / resumption | Leasing and rent review |
| Public auction | Council rates | Mortgage |
| Sealed auction | Local taxes (property tax) | For (re)development |
| Tenders | State taxes (land tax) | Company accounts / reporting |
| | Commonwealth taxes (GST, CGT) | Insurance |

## The profession and its regulation

| Body | What it produces |
|---|---|
| **IVSC** — International Valuation Standards Council | The international framework of valuation standards and rules |
| **RICS** — Royal Institution of Chartered Surveyors | RICS Valuation Global Standards, the *Red Book* |
| **API** — Australian Property Institute | Rules of Professional Conduct / Professional Practice |

**Queensland** is the jurisdiction you sit exams in:

- **Valuers Registration Act 1992 (Qld)** — you must be registered under
  the Act to value land in Queensland.
- Valuers are subject to the API Rules of Professional Practice and the
  Code of Conduct contained in the **Valuers Registration Regulations
  2013 (Qld)**.
- The **Valuers Registration Board**, established under the Act, handles
  disciplinary measures against registered valuers.

Western Australia is the deck's contrast case: regulation sits with the
Department of Energy, Mines, Industry Regulation and Safety, covering
licensing, registration and code of conduct.

## Asset classes

The unit covers seven land-use types. Three of them — office, retail and
industrial — are grouped as **commercial real estate (CRE)** and get most
of the airtime.

```
1. Residential
2. Office        ┐
3. Retail        ├─ Commercial Real Estate (CRE)
4. Industrial    ┘
5. Hotel / Leisure / Tourism
6. Rural
7. Mixed Use
```

Alongside these sit **special-purpose** assets (government institutions,
places of worship, hospitals, education, transport precincts, retirement
villages) and the rural sub-types (dryland and irrigated farming, grazing,
horticulture, intensive agriculture, intensive livestock, forestry/carbon).

### Office — seven characteristics

Location; location within location (precinct); quality; age; construction
and building services; size / floor plate configuration and the location
of the core; environmental and energy efficiency.

**Location** in a major city runs CBD → CBD fringe → regional centre →
suburban/metropolitan → business parks → office space within shopping
centres. In a major town it collapses to a two-to-four-block CBD plus
suburban, with smaller towns having a mixed office/retail main street.

**Location in location** is the precinct structure inside a large CBD.
Brisbane's, per the deck: financial ("Brisbane's Golden Triangle"), retail
(Queen Street Mall and Edward Street), legal (North Quarter), government
(George Street), cultural/entertainment (Southbank). The finance precinct
carries a higher proportion of Prime accommodation; the government sector
is generally A and B grade.

**Grade** is a voluntary standard set by the Property Council of Australia
under its *Guide to Office Building Quality*.

| Grade | Definition |
|---|---|
| **Premium** | A landmark building in a major CBD market that is a trendsetter in establishing rents. Expansive views and outlook, ample natural light, prestige access from an attractive street setting, prestige lobby and lift finishes, premium lift ride, premium amenities, premium presentation and maintenance |
| **Grade A** | High-quality building: high-quality views/outlook/natural light, access, lobby and lift finishes, lift ride, amenities, presentation and maintenance |
| **Grade B** | Good quality office building with a good standard of finish and maintenance |
| **Grade C** | Adequate quality office space |

Read the grade descriptions as a single sentence repeated at falling
intensity: *prestige/premium* → *high quality* → *good* → *adequate*. That
is genuinely all that separates them in the PCA wording.

**Size / floor plate**: high rise (predominantly CBD or major regional,
35–100 m tall, more than 12 floors); medium rise (4–12 floors, often
restricted by zoning); low rise (up to 3 floors); single level. Newer
business parks provide larger campus-style floor plates with fewer floors,
more parking and green space.

**Environmental**: owners, investors and tenants seek Greenstar and NABERS
ratings. Government legislation requires disclosure of a **Building Energy
Efficiency Certificate (BEEC)** for office property offered for sale or
lease — and, to a lesser extent, for retail and industrial.

### Retail — five characteristics

Location; size and tenancy mix; ownership; age; services.

The size hierarchy is examinable as a ladder, because the anchor tenancy
defines the tier:

```
Single shop (1–2 storeys)
Group shops (up to 5 or 6)
High street retail
Neighbourhood centre         — supermarket anchor
Sub-regional centre          — supermarket + discount department store (DDS)
Regional centre              — supermarket + DDS + 2 department stores
Super regional               — supermarket + DDS + at least 2 department stores
Bulky goods retail
```

> **The deck's own regional / super-regional definitions overlap and
> cannot both be right as written.** "Regional (Supermarket + DDS + 2
> Department Store)" and "Super Regional (Supermarket + DDS + at least 2
> Department Stores)" describe the same tenancy mix. In PCA practice the
> separation is by gross lettable area retail (GLAR) and by the *number*
> of DDS and department stores, not by the anchor list alone. If a
> multiple-choice question turns on the distinction, answer by size, and
> confirm the threshold with your tutor.

Ownership runs owner-occupier (small retail) → private investors →
property companies → investment institutions. On age, smaller single shops
have the greatest age range; larger centres may date from the 1970s but
after renovation generally present as less than 10 years old. Services
scale with centre size: tenant mix, public transport access, car parking,
security.

### Industrial — five characteristics

Industrial use; location/zoning; size; construction; age.

| Use | Location | Size | Construction |
|---|---|---|---|
| Heavy manufacturing | Fringe CBD | Small strata units 200–1,000 m² | Older brick and column |
| Medium manufacturing | Industrial estates | Medium 1,000–4,000 m² | Tilt slab |
| Light manufacturing | Town / regional city outskirts | Large > 4,000 m² | Portal frame |
| High-tech manufacturing | Business parks | | Purpose built |
| Warehousing | Designated industrial zones | | |
| Distribution centres | | | |
| Combined office/industrial | | | |

The age issue specific to industrial is **technological obsolescence** — a
1980s warehouse with 5 m clearance and no container-height roller doors is
not a modern logistics facility no matter how well maintained.

## The rent hierarchy

The Week 1 summary slide is a gift of an exam answer. "The valuation of
commercial property is based on income and risk."

| Sector | Highest rent | Lowest rent |
|---|---|---|
| Office | Prime CBD | Main street, small town |
| Retail | Super regional | Small town single shop |
| Industrial | Purpose built, multinational tenant | Small strata title unit, non-designated industrial area |

Notice that the industrial row is not about the building — it is about the
**tenant covenant** and the **zoning**. That is the Week 4 idea (risk sets
the cap rate) smuggled into Week 1.

## Property as an asset class

From the front of the Week 4 deck. Six characteristics of property
investment:

- **Low correlation with traditional investments** (equities, bonds).
- **Typically illiquid.**
- **Current market value is problematic to determine**, because property
  is not frequently traded in a public marketplace.
- **High transaction costs and taxes** on sale and purchase.
- **Requires maintenance and specific management skills.**
- **Limited historical risk and return data**, because individual
  properties are heterogeneous.

The correlation point is the one with an argument attached: direct
property returns are relatively stable over the long term compared with
bonds and equities, and no strong positive relationship exists between
direct property and other assets. Weak correlation makes direct property
desirable under **modern portfolio theory**, which emphasises balance —
the lower or more negatively correlated two asset classes are, the greater
the diversification benefit of holding both.

### Investor styles

The risk ladder that generates the cap rates you will use in Topic 4:

| Investment characteristics | Investor style |
|---|---|
| Low risk, high quality assets, stable income return over the long term, low leverage | **Core** funds; insurance |
| Income and yield focus, moderate leverage | **Syndicates** |
| Leasing risk, redevelopment and refurbishment, higher-and-better use, up-zoning | **Core-Plus / Value-Add** |
| High risk / high return, high leverage | **Opportunistic** |

Market structure sits on two axes: **active vs passive** investment, and
**individual asset vs diversified portfolio**, with direct property at the
active/individual corner, fund-of-funds at the passive/diversified corner,
and securitised property in between.

## The six drivers of asset value

From the Week 2 tutorial. This is the list you write down when an exam
asks "what drives the value of an income-producing asset?"

1. **Location** — for retail, proximity to amenities and transport, and
   foot traffic in CBD locations. For industrial, supply-chain efficiency
   and access to rail and motorways, which drive logistics cost.
2. **Improvements** — quality of building services (HVAC, lifts),
   accessibility including disabled-friendly facilities, interior fit-out
   quality, and end-of-trip facilities (bike racks, showers, communal
   space, connectivity).
3. **Capex / sustainability** — NABERS-style ratings covering energy
   efficiency, waste management and indoor air quality. Capex planning
   splits into short-term needs (a foyer refurbishment in two years),
   long-term sustainability (HVAC replacement), and aligning capex with
   lease terms and expiries.
4. **WALE** — weighted average lease expiry. Covered in Topic 3.
5. **Leasing** — how much rent, for how long, and how much incentive.
6. **Incentives** — rent-free periods, rent rebates, fit-out
   contributions. Can reach 30% of the total rental agreement.

The tutorial puts a number on the WALE driver that is worth memorising as
an order-of-magnitude anchor:

> A building with a **10-year WALE** might attract a cap rate around
> **5.0%**, against **5.75%** for an otherwise identical building with a
> **5-year WALE**, *ceteris paribus*.

> **A long WALE is not automatically better, and the tutorial gives you
> the counter-example to prove it.** Sydney CBD 2016–2018: office
> buildings were being acquired and demolished for the metro line,
> residential and hotel development. Tenants had to find space fast,
> effective rental growth hit **+40%**, and incentives fell to 10% and
> under. A landlord locked into a long WALE at 2015 rents captured none of
> that. If an exam question describes a rapidly tightening market, "short
> WALE = higher risk" is the wrong answer.

Run the 75 basis points through the capitalisation formula and you can see
why WALE gets its own tutorial. On an NOI of $500,000:

```
10-year WALE:   500,000 / 0.0500  = $10,000,000
 5-year WALE:   500,000 / 0.0575  =  $8,695,652
                                     -----------
Value difference                     $1,304,348   (13.0% of the higher figure)
```

## Summary

- Three motives to acquire: investment, occupation, speculation. USB144 is
  about investment — income-producing assets.
- `NOI = Gross Income − Operating Expenses`. `Value = NOI / Cap rate`.
  Everything else in the unit is an argument about one of those two
  inputs.
- Yield is derived from a sale; a cap rate is applied to a subject. Same
  arithmetic, opposite direction, and the word you choose signals which
  you are doing.
- Market value and market rent share a definition; market rent adds "on
  appropriate lease terms."
- Queensland: **Valuers Registration Act 1992 (Qld)**; standards from
  IVSC, RICS Red Book and the API.
- Office grades are PCA-voluntary and step down premium → high → good →
  adequate. Retail tiers are set by the anchor. Industrial is defined by
  use, zoning, size, construction and technological obsolescence.
- Property is illiquid, costly to transact, heterogeneous and weakly
  correlated with equities and bonds — which is exactly why institutions
  hold it.
- Six drivers of asset value: location, improvements, capex/sustainability,
  WALE, leasing, incentives.
