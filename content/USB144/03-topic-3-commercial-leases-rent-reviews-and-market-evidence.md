## What a lease is

Moore et al. (2016:663) define a lease as an "agreement entered into by a
landlord [or lessor] and a tenant [or lessee]." A **leasehold interest is
created from a freehold interest and is therefore a lesser interest** —
the freeholder cannot give away more than they hold.

Five elements have to be present:

```
1. Lessor
2. Lessee
3. Interest granted for exclusive possession of a property
4. For a fixed or determinable term
5. Regular payment
```

**Exclusive possession** is the element that turns a licence into a lease.
It means quiet enjoyment, the ability to install an internal fit-out,
access at agreed times, and provision of required services. Cases:
*Radaich v Smith* (1959) 101 CLR 109 and *Lewis v Bell* [1985] 1 NSWLR
731.

**Fixed or determinable term** needs a commencement, a continuance and an
end. *Lace v Chantler* [1944] KB 368 held that a lease "for the duration
of the war" is insufficient, because the duration is not ascertainable.

> **Exclusive possession is what makes it a lease, not the label on the
> document.** Call a document a licence all you like — if the occupier has
> exclusive possession for a determinable term at a rent, the courts treat
> it as a lease with all the statutory protection that attaches. That
> matters to a valuer because a "licence" over a car park or a signage
> position may in substance be terminable income, or may in substance be
> a lease you have to capitalise.

### Legal interests and their documents

| Legal interest | Document | Parties |
|---|---|---|
| Freehold | Certificate of title | Freeholder |
| Leasehold | Lease and counterpart of lease | Lessor and lessee/tenant |
| Sub-lease | Sub-lease and counterpart | Sub-lessor (original tenant) and sub-lessee |
| Assignment | Deed of assignment of lease | Assignor (original tenant) and assignee (replacement tenant) |
| Licence | Licence agreement | Licensor (owner) and licensee |

## The legislation

Leases are contracts governed by both common law and statute. Three
statutory layers, and the deck's table across all jurisdictions collapses
to this for Queensland:

| Layer | Queensland |
|---|---|
| Lease-specific | **Retail Shop Leases Act 1994 (Qld)** |
| General property law | **Property Law Act 2023 (Qld)** |
| Titling and land titles | **Land Titles Act 1994 (Qld)** |

The other jurisdictions worth being able to name: Retail Leases Act 1994
(NSW), Retail and Commercial Leases Act 1995 (SA), Retail Leases Act 2003
(Vic), Commercial Tenancy (Retail Shops) Agreements Act 1985 (WA),
Business Tenancies (Fair Dealings) Act 2003 (NT), Leases (Commercial and
Retail) Act 2001 (ACT), Code of Practice for Retail Tenancies (Tas).

> **Legislation can void a clause the parties actually agreed to, and that
> changes the cash flow you are valuing.** The deck's example: **ratchet
> clauses on rent reviews are deemed void under retail leasing legislation
> in Australia.** A ratchet clause says a market review can never reduce
> the rent. Strike it out and a retail tenant's rent can fall at review —
> so the "upward-only" assumption you would happily make for an office
> lease is unsafe for a shop. Check which Act governs before you model the
> review.

Behind the synopsis sit the full terms and conditions. The examinable set:

| Clause | What it settles |
|---|---|
| **Lessor / Lessee** | The legal entities, not the trading names |
| **Premises** | Area of exclusive use, measured per the **Property Council Method of Measurement** |
| **Lease term** | Length, commencement and expiry dates |
| **Rent** | How much (annual and $/m²); how often (monthly, quarterly, semi-annual, annual); gross or net; face or effective; initial or current; incentives, when and how |
| **Rent review** | Fixed, CPI, market, ratchet clause |
| **Repairing liability** | Who repairs and maintains, who pays what and on what basis |
| **Outgoings recovery** | Definition (statutory vs general), outgoings % or $/m², common-area cleaning, tenancy cleaning |
| **Lease renewal and options** | On what terms, when to activate |
| **Use clause** | What activities the lessee may undertake |
| **Purchase option** | Call or put option over the property |
| **Termination** | By expiry or default |
| **Surrender / assignment / sub-lease** | Free to assign or sub-let, or prohibited |
| **Make good** | Fair wear and tear definitions; terms for repairing fixtures, fittings and improvements |
| **Demolition clause** | Lets the landlord terminate to demolish or redevelop |
| **Notices, registration, penalties, security, car parking** | Administrative but valued |

### Reading a clause from both sides

The deck's worked lease review is a good template for the qualitative
half of an exam answer:

| Clause | Terms | Lessor's view | Lessee's view |
|---|---|---|---|
| Lease term | 10 years | Low-risk investment, low churn rate | Secure tenancy |
| Option | 2 × 10 | Advantage: lease buy-out. Disadvantage in a market shock: must maintain cash flow by meeting tenant demand | Advantage: exercise the option if the offer is favourable. Disadvantage: lack of flexibility |
| Rent and outgoings | $423,100 p.a. + outgoings + GST | Exposure to default, but creates additional income | If tenancy costs are high, the tenant can assign or sublet |
| Annual rent review | CPI | Slow rent growth and low return; does not account for property demand or value | Affordability — rent reflects economic growth |
| Incentives | 20% | Balances tenancy risk against financial returns | Early exit means the incentive is paid back |

> **A demolition clause is a valuation issue, not a legal footnote.** It
> converts a ten-year lease into a lease the landlord can end at will. For
> WALE, for the cap rate, and for the terminal value in a DCF, a lease
> with a demolition clause is not a ten-year covenant. If the tenancy
> schedule shows a long WALE and the leases carry demolition clauses, the
> long WALE is fiction.

## Passing rent versus market rent

The valuer must assess **two separate things**:

1. The **passing rent** based on the actual lease, or a notional lease if
   the property is owner-occupied.
2. The **current market rent** for that property sector.

**Passing (or contract) rent**: the rent being paid by the lessee as
specified by the terms of the lease or tenancy agreement (API, APC,
IVSC). It may equal market rent. In practice the two often differ
substantially, particularly for older leases with fixed rental terms.

Rent reviews exist to close that gap. Per McKenzie (2018) they are
conducted so the net income adds value to the CRE investment, and they
respond to:

- **Resets** — microeconomic factors
- **Deflation and inflation** — macroeconomic factors
- **Length of leases and the rarity of annuity-style leases**

## The five types of rent review

```
1. Fixed percentage increase (typically 2%–5% p.a.)
2. Consumer Price Index (CPI)
3. Fixed amount
4. Market rental review — often at the commencement of an option term
5. Combination, e.g. CPI + 2%
   (plus turnover rent provisions, for retail)
```

### 1. Fixed percentage

Rent increases by stated increments. Compound, not simple.

*Three tenants of a small West End office building:*

| Tenant | Term | Review | Passing rent | Yr 1 | Yr 2 | Yr 3 | Yr 4 | Yr 5 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 3 | 3% | $32,000 | $32,960 | $33,949 | $34,967 | | |
| 2 | 5 | 2% | $38,000 | $38,760 | $39,535 | $40,326 | $41,132 | $41,955 |
| 3 | 3 | 3% | $24,300 | $25,029 | $25,780 | $26,553 | | |

```
Tenant 1, Year 3 = 32,000 × (1.03)³
                 = 32,000 × 1.092727
                 = $34,967.26
```

> **The column headed "Year 1" is already the reviewed rent, not the
> starting rent.** $32,000 is the passing rent *before* the first review;
> the Year 1 column shows $32,960, which is one review applied. So a
> three-year term shows three columns, and the exponent equals the column
> number, not the column number minus one. Read the deck's own table
> before you assume off-by-one either way.

### 2. CPI

```
                              Current Index − Base Index
CPI Rent Review = Rent × --------------------------------
                                   Base Index
```

The deck also defines the CPI review verbally as "dividing a current
quarter index figure by the index figure for that quarter in a previous
year."

**Worked example.** Tenant A, office, lease start 1 July 2024, NLA 25 m².

```
Base index    (Sep-24)  =  139.4
Current index (Jun-25)  =  144.1

Multiplier = (144.1 − 139.4) / 139.4
           = 4.7 / 139.4
           = 0.033716  =  3.3716%
```

Applied to a base rent of **$1,000 p.a.**:

```
CPI rent review = 1,000 × 0.033716  =  $33.72
Passing rent    = 1,000 + 33.72     =  $1,033.72
```

> **This slide contradicts itself, twice, and you should say so if it
> comes up.** *(1)* The question sets the base rent at **$10,000 p.a.**;
> the worked table then uses **$1,000 p.a.** On $10,000 the review is
> **$337.16** and the passing rent is **$10,337.16**. The $10,000 figure
> is the internally consistent one — the slide also gives NLA as 25 m²,
> and `10,000 / 25 = $400/m²`, a plausible small-office rate, while
> `1,000 / 25 = $40/m²` is not. *(2)* The deck's own definition says to
> compare *the same quarter* a year apart, but the worked example compares
> **Jun-25 against Sep-24** — nine months, not twelve. A true June-on-June
> or September-on-September comparison would give a different multiplier.
> Use whichever pair the exam question hands you, name the quarters
> explicitly, and confirm the convention with your tutor.

### 3. Fixed amount

The rent rises by a stated dollar figure on each anniversary. Arithmetic,
not geometric — the increase does not compound.

```
Rent at review n = Initial rent + (n × fixed amount)
```

### 4. Market review

"A rent determined according to the value of the premises in a prescribed
market, determined according to comparable properties." Commercial leases
are explicit about what may and may not be taken into account, both for
the subject premises and for the properties used as comparable evidence.

**Worked example.** An office lease: initial rent $500/m² net, NLA 600 m²,
fixed increases of 5% p.a., five-year term, market rent review on exercise
of the option.

| | Subject ($/m²) | Market growth | Market ($/m²) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Initial | 500 | | 500 |
| Year 1 | 500 | −3% | 500 |
| Year 2 | 525 | 0% | 485 |
| Year 3 | 551 | +1% | 485 |
| Year 4 | 579 | +2% | 490 |
| Year 5 | 608 | | **500** ← passing rent after exercising the option |

```
Subject, Year 5 = 500 × (1.05)⁴ = $607.75  →  $608
Market:  500 × 0.97 = 485;  485 × 1.00 = 485;
         485 × 1.01 = 489.85 → 490;  490 × 1.02 = 499.80 → 500
```

> **The growth-rate column is offset by one row, and reading it in line
> costs you the whole table.** The −3% sits on the Year 1 line but produces
> the **Year 2** market rent of $485. Year 1's market rent is still $500.
> Check any growth-rate table by reproducing one cell before you trust the
> rest of it.

The commercial point is brutal: five years of 5% fixed increases take the
tenant from $500 to $608/m² while the market went from $500 to $500. At
the option the market review resets the rent to **$500**, wiping out
$108/m² — a **17.8% cut** on 600 m², or **$64,800 a year** of income the
landlord had been booking.

### 5. Combination

CPI plus a fixed margin. The tutorial workbook's template applies them in
sequence — CPI first to get the CPI-adjusted rent, then the margin on top
of that adjusted figure. See Topic 6 for the worked version and for the
alternative additive reading.

### Turnover rent (retail)

A percentage of business turnover (gross sales) that the tenant pays on
top of base rent.

**Worked example.** A vintage clothing store in a regional centre pays
base rent of $45,000 plus 6% of average gross turnover. Turnover last
financial year was $500,000.

```
Step 1 — turnover rent
    = 500,000 × 6%
    = $30,000

Step 2 — passing rent
    = Base rent + Turnover rent
    = 45,000 + 30,000
    = $75,000
```

The turnover component lifts the rent **66.7% above** the base, which is
why the base rent alone is never the comparable for a
turnover-rent shop.

### Projecting future rental value

*A property has a current rental value of $25,000 p.a. It has just been
let on a 15-year lease with 5-yearly upward-only reviews. Anticipated
rental growth is 2.5% p.a.*

```
1st review (year 5)   FV = 25,000 × (1.025)⁵  = $28,285.21
2nd review (year 10)  FV = 25,000 × (1.025)¹⁰ = $32,002.11
```

> **A 15-year lease with 5-yearly reviews has two reviews, not three.**
> The review at year 15 does not happen, because year 15 is the expiry.
> Count reviews as `(term ÷ review interval) − 1`. The same logic catches
> out the Week 5 tutorial's 20-year / 10-yearly question, where strictly
> only the year-10 review falls within the term.

## The five-step market rent review

This is the Assessment 2 skeleton.

```
Step 1   Review the terms of the subject lease
Step 2   Inspect the subject premises, noting value drivers
Step 3   Identify and analyse the rental evidence
Step 4   Determine the market rental rate
Step 5   Prepare the market rent review report
```

### Step 1 — the subject lease

Five questions to answer:

- What is the current rent, if the tenant is in place?
- What is the remaining lease term?
- When is the next rent review due?
- Is the tenant likely to remain (possible vacancy factor and letting-up
  issues)?
- Have the terms and conditions of the lease been met?

The output is a **tenancy schedule**. The deck's hypothetical, for a
four-level Milton office building:

| Tenancy | Level | NLA (m²) | % NLA | Start | Expiry | Term | Options | Review | Base rent p.a. | $/m² | Car spaces | Car rent p.a. |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| QUT Bookshop | 1 | 705 | 22% | Owner occ | | | | | $0 | – | 30 | – |
| Open Book Pty | 2 | 232 | 7% | 1-Oct-24 | 30-Sep-28 | 4 | 5 | CPI | $96,512 | $416 | 5 | $15,780 |
| Closed Book Pty | 3 | 581 | 18% | 1-May-23 | 30-Apr-30 | 7 | 5 | 3% | $278,880 | $480 | 5 | $15,000 |
| Happy Brothers Ltd | 4 | 846 | 26% | 1-May-22 | 30-Apr-30 | 8 | 5 | 4% | $488,912 | $578 | 20 | $75,840 |
| Asset Management Ltd | 5 | 876 | 27% | 1-Oct-21 | 30-Sep-30 | 9 | 5 | 4% | $479,605 | $547 | 25 | $74,700 |
| **Total** | | **3,240** | **100%** | | | | | | **$1,343,909** | | **85** | **$181,320** |

Both totals reconcile exactly: `705+232+581+846+876 = 3,240` and
`96,512 + 278,880 + 488,912 + 479,605 = 1,343,909`.

> **Do not rebuild the rent from the $/m² column — it is rounded.**
> `846 × 578 = $488,988`, but the schedule says **$488,912**; the true
> rate is $577.91/m². `876 × 547 = $479,172` against a stated $479,605;
> the true rate is $547.49/m². The rents are the primary data and the
> rates are derived and rounded. If you recompute from the rate you will
> be out by hundreds of dollars per tenancy, and the schedule will stop
> footing.

The deck's property description for 299 Coronation Drive, Milton is the

### Step 3 — analyse the rental evidence

Seven tests for whether a letting is comparable:

- Recent lease date
- Similar location
- Similar use and size
- Similar tenure
- Similar lease terms and conditions
- Similar construction and condition
- Open market transaction

Lease evidence itself comes from three sources: **open market lettings**,
**lease renewals**, and **rent reviews**.

The `A2 Resources` folder in the unit is a real evidence pack of Cushman &
Wakefield lease-analysis sheets, and it is the cleanest example of
Step 3 you will get. Every sheet reduces the deal to a single
**rate per m² of GLA**:

| Property | Tenant | Commenced | Term | Review | Area (m²) | Net rental p.a. | $/m² GLA |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1/29 Armada Place, **Banyo** | Evolution Group | Feb 2023 | 5 yrs + 5 + 5 | 3.5% or CPI | 1,650 | $280,500 | **$170** |
| 23 Huntington Place, **Banyo** | iMile Delivery & Logistics | Dec 2024 | 3 yrs | 3% | 1,140 | $216,600 | **$190** |
| 1/231 Holt Street, Pinkenba | Charcoal Clothing | Jun 2025 | 3 yrs + 1×3 | 4% | 3,008 | $630,000 | **$209** |
| 6–12 French Avenue, Brendale | Dirty Laundry | Aug 2025 | 10 yrs + 1×5 | CPI or 4% | 3,312 | $596,160 | **$180** |
| 405 Newman Road, Geebung (9 tenancies) | various | 2023–24 | 3–10 yrs | 3%–3.5% | 1,571–4,605 | $290,635–$759,825 | **$160–$200** |

Check one before you trust the set: `1,650 × 170 = $280,500` ✓ and
`1,140 × 190 = $216,600` ✓. Holt Street is `630,000 / 3,008 = $209.44` →
$209 ✓. French Avenue is `596,160 / 3,312 = $180.00` ✓ exactly. The
Geebung sheets also reconcile — `3,328 × 160 = $532,480` ✓,
`4,605 × 165 = $759,825` ✓, `2,035 × 200 = $407,000` ✓.

Two things the brochures teach that no slide does:

1. **The agent's comment is evidence about the transaction, not
   decoration.** Huntington Place is "leased to an international company
   with **minimal incentive**" — so its $190 is close to an effective
   rent. Four of the Geebung tenancies were "agreed 18–24 months before
   PC" (practical completion), which means those rates are 2021 decisions
   printed on 2023 leases and should be adjusted upward or discarded.
2. **Rate per m² of GLA is the unit of comparison, and it must be
   like-for-like on the net/gross axis.** Every sheet above is **net**.
   Comparing a net $190 to a gross $190 overstates the subject's rent by
   the whole outgoings load.

> **The Geebung range spans $160 to $200 — a 25% spread — and the reason
> is timing, not quality.** The two lowest rates ($160, $165) carry the
> comment "deal agreed 18–24 months before PC"; the two highest ($200,
> $195) are "agreed before PC" in Feb–Mar 2024 and "leased after PC". Rank
> your evidence by *when the deal was struck*, not by the lease
> commencement date printed on the sheet. A valuer who averages that range
> without adjusting for timing has produced a number with no defence.

### Step 4 — determine the market rate

Factors: location, condition of improvements, and leasing market
condition. The deck's Milton conclusion:

```
Gross face rent range        $430/m² to $485/m² of NLA p.a.
Effective gross rent range   $331/m² to $373/m² of NLA p.a.
Incentive range              23% to 39%
```

Sense-check the two ranges against each other:

```
331 / 430 = 0.770  →  a 23.0% incentive
373 / 485 = 0.769  →  a 23.1% incentive
```

Both ends of the effective range correspond to the *bottom* of the stated
23%–39% incentive range. At 39% the effective rents would be $262 and
$296, not $331 and $373. The three ranges are internally consistent only
at the 23% end — quote them as given, but do not derive one from another.

The subject-by-subject adjudication:

|---|---|---|---|---|
| QUT Property Economics | 705 | owner occ | $480 | Gross rent $480 with a 35% incentive |
| Open Book Pty | 232 | $416 | $480 | Leased **under** market parameters |
| Closed Book Pty | 581 | $480 | $480 | Passing rent **within** market parameters |
| Happy Brothers Ltd | 846 | $578 | $480 | Passing rents are **above** market; a rent reduction is applied given current economic conditions |
| Asset Management Ltd | 876 | $547 | $480 | As above |
| **Total** | **3,240** | | **$480** | Adopt **$480/m² p.a.** gross for the NLA |

### Step 5 — the report

The concluding paragraph is worth learning as a sentence pattern, because
it is the shape of the mark scheme:

> Given legal (strength of lease terms and conditions) and physical
> aspects (location, quality of tenant, size and scale) and in
> consideration of the micro and macro economic factors of the Brisbane
> Fringe Office Market, the rental rates adopted for the subject property
> are **$480/m² p.a. for NLA, 35% incentives, $350 for secured basement

Legal factors, physical factors, market factors, then the adopted numbers.

## Analysis of sales evidence

Most investment involves giving up a large capital sum now in return for
an income. Investors assess quality by the **yield** — the annual income
return expressed as a percentage of market value.

```
Yield = Net Operating Income (NOI) ÷ Capital Value
```

**Example.** Rental $85,000 net p.a., purchase price $1,300,000.

```
85,000 / 1,300,000 = 6.538%  →  6.54%
```

### The four yields

This table is examinable verbatim and the distinctions are worth real
marks.

| Yield | Numerator | Denominator |
|---|---|---|
| **Initial (passing) yield** | Passing net income | Purchase price |
| **Effective yield** | Current net income **after adjusting for rent incentives or impending vacancies** | Value or price |
| **Analysed / equated market yield** | Net **market** income (not passing income) | Sale price **plus any capital adjustments** to the core value |
| **Reversionary yield** | A **future** net income | Current value or price — normally quoted with the date from which it applies |

> **Initial and analysed yield differ in *both* the numerator and the
> denominator, and getting one right while ignoring the other is the
> classic half-mark answer.** Analysed yield swaps passing income for
> market income *and* adds capital adjustments (incentive allowance,
> letting-up, agency fees) to the price. The Week 3 tutorial's Sale 1
> shows how far apart they land: initial **6.53%**, analysed **8.11%** —
> 158 basis points. Capitalise the same $253,500 at each and the values
> are `253,500 / 0.0653 = $3,882,083` against
> `253,500 / 0.0811 = $3,125,771`, a **$756,312** gap. Pick the wrong
> yield and you are a quarter of the way out.

**Sale 1, worked.** Sale date 20 February 2023; price $3,125,000; GLAR
804 m²; net income $204,000 p.a.

```
Initial yield  = (204,000 / 3,125,000) × 100
               = 6.528%  →  6.53%

Market assessment of rent      $260,000
Permanent vacancy allowance    2.5%
Adjusted net rent              260,000 × 0.975 = $253,500

Analysed yield = (253,500 / 3,125,000) × 100
               = 8.112%  →  8.11%

Sale price per m² = 3,125,000 / 804 = $3,887/m²
```

### Writing the sales commentary

The deck's Milton conclusion is the template. Note the structure: state
the range, explain the constraint, then justify each end.

- The sales evidence shows an analysed market yield range of **6.99% to
  9.84%** and a sale rate of **$2,397 to $6,394/m²**.
- There are limited transactions of investment properties in the Fringe
  CBD areas.
- **Sale 1 sets the upper end** of the yield range because of inferior
  occupancy (by income) and inferior location.
- **Sale 3 sets the lower end** — although it transacted at a higher price
  point (passing rent above market parameters), the property is inferior
  in physical condition, WALE, occupancy and location.
- Conclusion: **adopt 8.25%–8.75%**, the range having softened due to
  diminishing WALE and stagnant conditions in the Milton sub-market.
- On rate per m²: Sale 1 is a good guide given comparable location and
  size; **adopt a slightly inferior $3,000–$3,400/m²**.

> **Higher price does not mean lower yield when the income is
> over-market.** Sale 3 transacted at a higher price *because* its passing
> rent was above market — the buyer paid for income that will fall at the
> next review. That is why it sets the *low* end of the analysed yield
> range: on **market** income rather than passing income, the yield
> collapses. This is the single most useful sentence in the deck for
> Assessment 3.

## Weighted Average Lease Expiry

WALE measures the average time in which all leases in a property will
expire. It indicates when the property, or parts of it, are likely to fall
vacant, and it can be weighted **by rental income** or **by area**.

```
                Σ ( weightᵢ × days remainingᵢ )
WALE (days)  =  ------------------------------- , summed over all tenancies
                        Σ weightᵢ

where weightᵢ = tenancy rent  (for WALE by income)
              = tenancy area  (for WALE by area)

WALE (years) = WALE (days) / 365
```

In practice you compute each tenancy's contribution as
`(rentᵢ ÷ total rent) × daysᵢ`, then sum.

### Worked example

The Milton schedule, valued as at **7 August 2025**. Total rent
$1,343,909; total area 3,240 m².

| Tenancy | Area | Rent | Expiry | Days remaining | WALE (income) | WALE (area) |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| QUT Bookshop | 705 | $0 | owner occ | 0 | 0.00 | 0.00 |
| Open Book Pty | 232 | $96,512 | 30-Sep-28 | 1,150 | 82.59 | 82.35 |

> **The owner-occupied floor is in the denominator for area WALE but
> contributes nothing, and that is what drives the two WALEs apart.** QUT
> Bookshop is 22% of the area at $0 rent. It drags the *area* WALE down by
> a full 22% while leaving the *income* WALE untouched — 4.77 years by
> income against 3.70 by area, a 29% difference on the same building.
> Always say which basis you used. "WALE of 3.7 years" is not an answer;
> "WALE of 3.70 years by area, 4.77 years by income" is.

### What WALE tells you

| WALE ≥ 5 years | WALE < 5 years |
|---|---|
| Large tenants | Smaller tenants |
| Low churn rate | High churn rate |
| Hard to negotiate rents | Growth in property income |

Short WALE (typically 1–3 years) carries uncertainty in cash flow and
valuation, potential vacancy at expiry, costs at expiry (leasing fees,
legal, refurbishment) and reduced liquidity — against the opportunity to
reset below-market rents and to redevelop. Long WALE (5+ years) carries
the risk of under-renting if fixed rents fall below future market rates
and difficulty adjusting to market trends — against financial stability,
leverage options from predictable cash flow, and the value support of
long-term certainty.

## Summary

- A lease needs lessor, lessee, exclusive possession, a fixed or
  determinable term, and regular payment. Exclusive possession is the
  distinguishing element (*Radaich v Smith*); an unascertainable term
  fails (*Lace v Chantler*).
- Queensland: Retail Shop Leases Act 1994, Property Law Act 2023, Land
  Titles Act 1994. Ratchet clauses are void in retail leases.
- Passing rent is what the lease says; market rent is what the evidence
  says. The valuer assesses both.
- Five review types: fixed %, CPI, fixed amount, market, combination —
  plus turnover rent for retail.
- The five-step market rent review is the Assessment 2 structure: review
  the lease, inspect, analyse the evidence, determine the rate, report.
- Lease evidence is reduced to **$/m² of GLA or NLA**, on a consistent
  net-or-gross basis, and adjusted for incentives and for when the deal
  was struck.
- Four yields: initial (passing income ÷ price), effective (after
  incentives and vacancy), analysed/equated (market income ÷ adjusted
  price), reversionary (future income ÷ current price).
- WALE weights days-to-expiry by income or by area. Always name the basis.
