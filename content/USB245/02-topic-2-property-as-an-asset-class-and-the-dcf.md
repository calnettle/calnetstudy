# TOPIC 2 — Property as an Asset Class and the DCF

Week 2 lecture. Two halves: what makes property different from every other
asset class, and the five-step decision process that ends in a discounted
cash flow. This is the week the assignment actually starts.

## 2.1 Scale of the Australian Property Market

Property is one of the most widely held investments in Australia.

| Measure | Figure |
|---|---|
| Residential property market value | **over $12.5 trillion** |
| Taxpayers declaring rental property income | 14% |
| Households renting | 30% |
| Australians holding units in listed/unlisted property funds | est. 1.5 million |
| Australians with property exposure via superannuation | over 16 million |
| Typical super fund allocation to property | ~8–11% of portfolio |

On the indirect side, the ASX provides access to an A-REIT sector with
**over $100 billion** in funds under management across **50 trusts**:

- **Industrial trusts** — warehouses, factories, industrial parks
- **Office trusts** — medium to large office buildings in and around major
  cities
- **Hotel and leisure trusts** — hotels, cinemas, theme parks
- **Retail trusts** — shopping centres and similar
- **Diversified trusts** — a mixture of the above

> **The $12.5 trillion is residential; the $100 billion is listed.** They
> are not comparable and quoting them side by side without saying so is a
> mistake. One is the gross value of every dwelling in the country; the
> other is the market capitalisation of a sector of the share market. Your
> AREIT in the assignment sits in the $100 billion pool and is bidding for
> a commercial asset — neither number is a benchmark for it.

### Why investors own property

The lecture's list, and it doubles as a checklist for A2's "investment
objectives" section:

- **Recurrent income** — like interest-bearing securities, property pays
  reliable rent.
- **Superannuation substitute** — an income stream after retirement.
- **Long-term capital growth**.
- **Tax benefit** — interest on debt financing is deductible against income.
- **Belief of an inherently safe asset** — note the wording. A *belief*, not
  a fact.
- **Diversification** — reducing volatility by spreading across assets, so
  less exposure to any one asset's risk.

## 2.2 What Makes Property Different

Traditional asset classes are cash (T-bills), stocks, bonds and real estate.
Property is like the others in producing income and capital gain, but it has
**a better risk-adjusted performance than other expected income streams** —
a moderate risk and return profile.

The comparison table, verbatim from the deck:

| Investment concern | Stocks | Property* | Long-term bonds | Cash (T-bill) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Risk** | High | Moderate to Low** | Moderate to Low | Lowest |
| **Total return** | High | Moderate to High | Moderate | Lowest |
| **Growth** | High | Moderate to High | Low / None | None |
| **Inflation protection** | Good | Good | Bad | Best (if reinvested) |

\* Unlevered institutional-quality commercial property.
\*\* Low risk for investors with long horizons and much money, so they can
hold assets to maturity. **Moderate** risk for investors exposed to asset
market price volatility.

> **Read the second footnote before you quote the table.** Property's risk
> rating is *conditional on the investor*, not a property of the building.
> The same asset is low-risk to a sovereign fund that can hold through a
> cycle and moderate-risk to a leveraged buyer facing a refinance in three
> years. In A2 this is how you justify your discount rate: state the
> investor's position, then price the risk *they* bear.

Four more distinguishing features:

**Diversification.** Property offers diversification benefits to investors
who already hold shares and interest-bearing securities, because property
returns **do not mirror** returns of other asset classes. Low correlation is
the mechanism; that is why super funds hold 8–11%.

**Inflation hedging.** Rents are periodically reviewed — often to CPI or a
fixed percentage — so income tracks prices in a way a fixed bond coupon
cannot.

**Illiquidity.** Properties cannot be sold quickly. Units in property funds
and REIT shares overcome this — *but* listed property units are volatile in
price, and unlisted property units generally have **no reliable secondary
market**. You trade one problem for another.

**Heterogeneity.** Every unit of property is unique in location, building
quality and financing. Markets segment by property type (residential,
commercial, industrial, tourism, health) and geographically.

**Cyclicality.** Property markets have larger price moves up and down over
the years — boom, peak, bust. Peak-to-peak cycle lengths vary across markets
and countries: 20–25 years, or 7–10 years, or something else entirely. The
deck leaves that with a question mark, deliberately.

> **A 7–10 year holding period is one cycle if you are lucky and half of one
> if you are not.** The AREIT's stated horizon spans at least one turn in
> rents and yields. That is why the terminal cap rate assumption dominates
> the answer — see note 05, §4.5 and §4.7.

## 2.3 Direct vs Indirect

| | **Direct** | **Indirect** |
|---|---|---|
| **What** | Investors own the physical asset | Investment through a fund; investors own securities giving rights to income from the properties. Traded publicly or privately |
| **Why** | Control of the asset · no fee leakage · prices quoted | Small investment parcels · liquidity · access to alternate sectors · "expert management" · diversification · set and forget · small transaction costs |
| **But** | Large transaction costs · illiquid · requires management | Volatile markets · illiquid if unlisted · returns not always reflective of property fundamentals, especially in falling markets |

Note the phrase **"expert management"** appears in scare quotes in the deck.
That is the fee-versus-value question, and it is a fair thing to raise in
A2 given your AREIT is the "expert manager" here.

## 2.4 Property Market Efficiency — Or Lack Of It

Property is an **imperfect** or **inefficient** market. Eight reasons:

```
1. Lack of transparency
2. Physical differences / lack of uniformity
3. Indivisibility
4. High transaction costs
5. Emotive buying
6. Limited ability for short selling
7. Infrequent sales
8. Slow reaction times to information
```

Compare with equities: uniform units, continuous pricing, near-zero
transaction cost, short selling available, information priced in minutes.

> **Inefficiency is why this unit exists.** In an efficient market, analysis
> earns nothing — the price already reflects everything knowable. In an
> inefficient one, the analyst who builds the better cashflow model finds
> the mispriced asset. It is also why the brief bans off-the-shelf DCF
> software: the modelling *is* the skill being assessed.

## 2.5 The Decision-Making Process

Buy? Sell? Refurbish? Refinance? Five steps, in order:

```
AMOUNT  →  OBJECTIVES  →  RETURN  →  CASHFLOW  →  RISK
```

Structure your A2 report in this order and it will read like the lecture.

## 2.6 Amount — How Much Can We Afford?

Driven by equity and debt, access to funds, and credit market conditions.

```
           E
P = ───────────────
     1 + T − M

P = price of the property
E = equity available to invest
T = acquisition costs, as a % of price
M = % of price to be borrowed
```

### Exercise 1

*Deposit of $72,000, eligible for an 80% loan, acquisition costs estimated
at 4%. What price range?*

```
P = 72,000 / (1 + 0.04 − 0.80)
  = 72,000 / 0.24
  = $300,000
```

Check it back: price $300,000, loan 80% = $240,000, acquisition costs 4% =
$12,000. Cash required = 300,000 − 240,000 + 12,000 = **$72,000** ✓.

*What if I can get a 95% loan?*

```
P = 72,000 / (1 + 0.04 − 0.95)
  = 72,000 / 0.09
  = $800,000
```

The same $72,000 buys a property **2.67 times** larger. That is leverage,
and the exercise exists to make you feel how violently the denominator
responds: moving M from 80% to 95% shrinks `1 + T − M` from 0.24 to 0.09.

> **The formula is highly sensitive because the denominator is a small
> difference between large numbers.** At M = 96% with T = 4%, the
> denominator is 0.08 and P = $900,000. At M = 104% it is negative and the
> formula is meaningless. Sanity-check every answer by reconstructing the
> cash: `price − loan + costs` must equal your equity.

### Applying it to 41 Park Road

The AREIT's LVR is 65%. At a $8,000,000 price with acquisition costs of
5.5%:

```
Loan            = 0.65 × 8,000,000  = $5,200,000
Acquisition cost= 0.055 × 8,000,000 = $  440,000
Total outlay    =                     $8,440,000
Equity required = 8,440,000 − 5,200,000 = $3,240,000
```

Or straight from the formula: `E = P × (1 + T − M) = 8,000,000 × 0.405 =
$3,240,000` ✓.

> **LVR is measured against VALUE, not against total outlay.** The lender
> lends 65% of the property's value; the acquisition costs are yours. Every
> extra dollar of stamp duty is a dollar of equity, not a dollar of debt.
> Model the loan off price and the costs off equity, or your month-0
> cashflow will be wrong by the full acquisition cost.

## 2.8 Return — Which One Are You Talking About?

The distinction that decides how you use the DCF:

| | **Required return** | **Expected return** |
|---|---|---|
| **Input (fixed)** | Expected income; required return | Expected income; asking or market price |
| **Calculate (variable)** | **Investment value** | **Expected return** |
| **Purpose** | To compare with asking or market price | To compare with required return |

In words: **required return** discounts net income to a present value, which
is the price the investor can pay *in order to earn* that rate. **Expected
return** is the rate earned *if* the property is bought at the asking price
and the expected income is received. (Rowland [2.60].)

> **This is the difference between an NPV question and an IRR question, and
> A1 asks both.** Fix the discount rate at your required return and solve for
> value → NPV, which you compare to $8m. Fix the price at $8m and solve for
> the rate → IRR, which you compare to the AREIT's 7.0% target. Same
> cashflows, two questions. Answering only one is half the assignment.

### The three decompositions of total return

```
Rt = Ri + Rc      total return = income return + capital return

1.  r  = y + g    TOTAL RETURN = current yield + growth
2.  rt = rf + rp  TOTAL RETURN = risk-free rate + risk premium
3.  r  = ρ + π    TOTAL RETURN = real return + inflation
```

Each does a different job in the report:

- **`r = y + g`** links the cap rate to the total return. Used to test
  whether a price is consistent with a growth story.
- **`rt = rf + rp`** builds a discount rate from the bond rate up. This is
  the build-up method the Week 4 tutorial will formalise alongside WACC.
- **`r = ρ + π`** keeps you honest about whether your cashflow is nominal or
  real.

### Worked example — the AREIT's 7% target under `r = y + g`

41 Park Road's net rental income is **$370,259.88** p.a. (see note 06 for
the reconciliation). At the quoted **$8,000,000**:

```
y = 370,259.88 / 8,000,000 = 4.628%

r = y + g   →   7.0% = 4.628% + g   →   g = 2.372% p.a.
```

So the asking price only clears the AREIT's hurdle if income and value grow
at **2.37% p.a. or better**, every year, for the whole hold — before any
allowance for downtime, incentives or capex. Drop the price and the required
growth falls fast:

| Price | Initial net yield | Growth needed for r ≥ 7% |
|---|---|---|
| $8,000,000 | 4.628% | 2.372% p.a. |
| $7,000,000 | 5.289% | 1.711% p.a. |
| $6,500,000 | 5.696% | 1.304% p.a. |
| $6,000,000 | 6.171% | 0.829% p.a. |

> **`r = y + g` is an approximation, and the exam wants you to know it.** It
> assumes income and capital value grow at the same constant rate forever
> and that nothing interrupts the income. 41 Park Road has 80% of its income
> expiring inside twelve months (note 06, §5.7), so the "g" here is doing
> work it cannot do. Use `r = y + g` as a *screen* — a fast test of whether
> a price is plausible — and the DCF for the answer.

## 2.9 Concept of Value → The DCF

> Investments are worth the present value of their estimated/expected
> future income streams.

Hence the capitalisation method, `CV = NI / Y`. **But that equation assumes
year-1 net income is received in perpetuity.** That is an oversimplification.
What if:

- a 25% market increase is forecast next year?
- the major tenant's lease expires the year after?
- you have refurbishment plans?

Overcome this with **discounted cash flow analysis**.

### What a DCF does

- Allows detailed analysis of all property-specific factors.
- Incorporates all forecast income and expenditure over the holding period,
  **uneven**.
- Is the sum of the PVs of future net income streams over that period.
- Things to consider: term, frequency, **net** income, required return.

Used to calculate **either**:

- the price you would pay for the right to receive the future income
  streams; **or**
- the return you would expect from those streams, given a fixed purchase
  price.

```
        n     CFt
P  =    Σ   ───────
       t=1  (1 + r)^t

P    = price
CFt  = cashflow in each period t
n    = number of periods
r    = required rate of return, or discount rate
```

### The 7 steps

Memorise these. The A1 workbook is literally this list turned into
worksheets.

```
1. Set up cashflows over period/time
2. Determine the Net Income for each period
3. Calculate the discount factor for each period    DF = 1/(1+r)^t
4. Calculate the PV of each period                  PV = CF × DF
5. Add up all the PVs
6. Adjust for any acquisition costs
7. Answer = NPV
```

### The lecture's own worked DCF

Exercise 2: your grandmother leaves you money for your education. Five years
at university, $600 per week, an investment paying 6% p.a.

**(a) Static — even cashflows.**

```
PMT = $600/week × 52 = $31,200 p.a.
n   = 5,  r = 6%

PV = 31,200 × (1 − 1.06^-5) / 0.06
   = 31,200 × 4.21236
   = $131,425.75   →  slide gives $131,426  ✓
```

**(b) Dynamic — uneven cashflows.** Same budget, escalated at 2.5%
inflation, plus $5,000 for legal and accounting fees.

```
 t     CFt          DF = 1/(1.06)^t       PV
──────────────────────────────────────────────────
 1   $31,200.00        0.9434         $29,433.96
 2   $31,980.00        0.8900         $28,462.09
 3   $32,779.50        0.8396         $27,522.30
 4   $33,598.99        0.7921         $26,613.55
 5   $34,438.96        0.7473         $25,734.80
                                 ─────────────────
                     (5) Σ PVs =      $137,766.69
                   (6) Acq costs =      $5,000.00
        (7) Investment required =     $142,766.69
```

Both figures match the slide to the dollar ($137,767 and $142,767).

> **The slide labels that final line "NPV". It is not an NPV.** It is the
> **gross present value of the outflows plus the fees** — the amount you must
> invest today. A net present value nets an inflow against an outflow:
> `NPV = Σ PV − outlay`, which for a property purchase means **subtracting**
> the price and acquisition costs, not adding them. The step-6 wording
> "adjust for any acquisition costs" is doing a lot of work — in this
> savings problem the adjustment is **+$5,000**, and in the property problem
> it is **−(price + costs)**. Get the sign backwards in A1 and your NPV is
> out by roughly twice the acquisition cost. The FICTIONAL teaching workbook
> does it correctly: `NPV = Sum of PVs − Total acquisition outlay` (note 05,
> §4.5).

## 2.10 Risk — Actual ≠ Forecast

Risk in a DCF has one definition: **the forecast is wrong**. The deck splits
sources into two families.

| Systemic / External | Non-systemic / Internal |
|---|---|
| Legislative | Income |
| Market | Bankruptcy |
| Inflation | Customer |
| Competition | Technology |
| Finance | Plant & equipment |
| Supply and demand | Contractor |
| Economy | Obsolescence |

Systemic risk cannot be diversified away — every property in the portfolio
is exposed to an interest-rate move. Non-systemic risk can be: one tenant's
insolvency is survivable across twenty buildings.

> **Which family a risk belongs to determines what you can do about it, and
> that is the marked part.** Tenant default is non-systemic → diversify the
> rent roll, take bank guarantees, stagger expiries. A yield expansion is
> systemic → you cannot diversify it, so you price it into the discount rate
> and test it in sensitivity. Listing risks without saying which lever
> addresses each is a descriptive answer where an analytical one was asked
> for.

### The three drivers of income risk

The income stream of an income-producing property is influenced by three
things:

```
LOCATION   →   BUILDING   →   TENANT / LEASE
```

**Location** (Rowland pp. 24–25), by sector:

| Sector | Consider |
|---|---|
| Residential | House or units, distance to work, public transport, schools, infrastructure, demographics, view, aspect, socio-economic factors |
| Office | CBD or suburban, economic growth, vacancy, employment rate, public transport, zoning |
| Retail | Neighbourhood / regional / strip / specialty, disposable income, catchment demographics, supply, zoning, access, visibility |
| Industrial | Warehouse, distribution, road networks, workforce, office component, showroom |
| Tourist / leisure | Beachside, accessibility, airports, other demand generators |
| Development site | Zoning, infrastructure, other supply |

**Building** (Rowland p. 27) — features that attract and retain tenants:

| Sector | Features |
|---|---|
| Residential | Bedrooms, garage spaces, yard size, functionality, condition, fittings |
| Office | Floor plate (size and columns), natural light, views, technology, finishes, foyer, car parking, a/c quality, condition/maintenance, green rating |
| Retail | Shop frontage, finishes, lighting, climate control, position relative to majors, signage, visibility, age |
| Industrial | Floor-to-ceiling height, floor loading, turning bays, showroom/office, roller doors |

**Obsolescence is a feature specific to property as an investment class.**
Types: functional, technological, design, style, environmental — arguably all
of the above.

> **Obsolescence is the reason a property DCF needs a capex line and a share
> portfolio does not.** A share does not wear out. A building's air
> conditioning, lifts and services do, and the tenant who tolerates them
> today will not renew on them in five years. If your A1 model has no
> recurring capex allowance and no upgrade provision, you have modelled a
> bond, not a building — and the "minimal capex required" line in the AREIT's
> objectives is an assertion you are supposed to *test*, not accept.

**Tenants and leases** (Rowland p. 28). The lease is the contract by which
the owner gets the right to receive income. What matters:

- **Lease term and options** — options are the tenant's right, not yours
- **Tenant quality** — financial strength, growth and vacancy potential
- **Rent review mechanisms and frequency** — including the **ratchet clause**
  (a market review that can only go up, never down)
- **Responsibility for outgoings** — gross vs net lease
- **Vacancy** — incentives, leasing commissions, rent-free periods, and the
  forecast long-term vacancy rate

> **An option is exercisable at the tenant's discretion, which makes it
> worth nothing to you as certainty of income.** A tenant with a 2 × 3-year
> option will exercise it if the market rent exceeds the option rent, and
> walk if it does not — so options protect the downside for the *tenant*.
> WALE is therefore conventionally measured to **expiry**, not to the end of
> the option period. Extending WALE with options in the assignment is a
> presentational choice that must be labelled if you make it.

## Summary

- Australian residential property is worth over $12.5 trillion; the listed
  A-REIT sector holds over $100 billion across 50 trusts.
- Property is a growth asset with a moderate risk/return profile,
  diversification benefits, inflation hedging — and illiquidity,
  heterogeneity and cyclicality.
- Direct = control, no fee leakage, but illiquid and management-intensive.
  Indirect = liquidity and small parcels, but volatile or unlisted-illiquid.
- Property markets are **inefficient**: opaque, non-uniform, indivisible,
  costly to transact, emotive, un-shortable, infrequently traded and slow to
  price information.
- Decision process: **Amount → Objectives → Return → Cashflow → Risk**.
- `P = E/(1 + T − M)`. Check every answer by rebuilding the cash.
- Required return → solve for value. Expected return → solve for the rate.
- `r = y + g`, `rt = rf + rp`, `r = ρ + π`.
- `CV = NI/Y` assumes year-1 income in perpetuity. The DCF removes that
  assumption; the 7 steps are the method, and step 6's sign depends on
  whether you are *investing* or *buying*.
- Risk = actual ≠ forecast, split systemic/non-systemic, driven by
  **location, building, tenant/lease**.
