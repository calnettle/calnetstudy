# TOPIC 1 — Investment Principles

Week 1 lecture, Part 2. The definitions, the risk/return trade-off, the six
things every investor must decide, the asset-class map, and the assumed
knowledge the unit expects you to already have from USB142 and USB144:
net operating income, the capitalisation approach, and time value of money.

## 1.1 What Is an Investment?

> **Investment** — the commitment of capital to an asset in expectation of
> obtaining additional income, profit or some future benefit.

In finance the benefit is called a **return**. Property returns have exactly
two components and every measure in this unit is built from them:

```
1. INCOME return   — future income (rent)
2. CAPITAL return  — capital appreciation (price growth)
```

The word *investment* carries an implication: **the safety of the principal
matters**. That is what separates it from the two things it is often
confused with.

| | Principal | Return expectation |
|---|---|---|
| **Investment** | Safety of principal matters — risk is *managed* | Risk-adjusted return |
| **Speculation** | Risking principal is acceptable | Above-market return sought |
| **Gambling** | Risking principal is the point | Negative expected value |

Expected returns are a product of three things:

```
Expected return = time value of money
                + expected inflation
                + future uncertainty (risk)
```

Which is why investors seek a **risk-adjusted return**, not simply the
biggest number.

## 1.2 Risk and Return

Two propositions carry the whole unit:

```
1. There is a reward for bearing risk.
2. The greater the risk, the greater the POTENTIAL return.
```

Together those are the **risk/return trade-off**.

The lecture then asks two questions that look rhetorical and are not.

**Q: Does risk = return?** No. Risk buys you a *higher expected* return —
it does not deliver one. If risk guaranteed return it would not be risk.
The relationship is between risk and the *distribution* of outcomes: higher
risk widens the distribution in both directions.

**Q: If all investors want the maximum return, will all investors accept a
high-risk investment?** No — and that is what **risk tolerance** is for.

> **This distinction is exam bait.** "Higher risk means higher return" is
> wrong and is marked wrong. "Higher risk means investors *require* a higher
> expected return as compensation" is right. The required return is the
> price of bearing the risk, set before the fact; the realised return is
> whatever actually happens.

### Risk tolerance

> **Risk tolerance** — the level of uncertainty and potential loss an
> investor is *willing and able* to accept in pursuit of returns.

Willing *and* able. An investor may be psychologically comfortable with
volatility but unable to survive it — a fund with monthly redemption
obligations cannot hold an illiquid development site regardless of appetite.

Why it matters: it drives investment selection and portfolio structure, it
balances expected return against potential loss, and it keeps investments
aligned with the investor's actual financial circumstances.

The property examples from the deck:

| Risk level | Property example |
|---|---|
| Low | Prime commercial property; diversified A-REITs |
| Moderate | Established residential investment properties |
| High | Property development projects; speculative land |

## 1.3 Returns — Percentage, Not Dollars

It is more useful to think in **percentage** returns than dollar returns,
because percentages are comparable across investments of different size.

```
Income return   Ri = income / purchase price
Capital return  Rc = (sale price − purchase price) / purchase price
Total return    Rt = Ri + Rc
```

### Worked example — why dollars mislead

Investment A: buy a residential property for $1m; one year later the total
profit (income plus appreciation) is $150,000.
Investment B: buy a small commercial property for $5m; one year later the
total profit is $500,000.

```
A:  150,000 / 1,000,000  = 15.0%
B:  500,000 / 5,000,000  = 10.0%
```

B earns **3.3 times the dollars** and is the **worse** investment per dollar
committed. A wins by 5 percentage points. Dollar returns tell you nothing
until you name the capital that produced them.

### Worked example — total return decomposed

You bought a property for $35m, received income of $1.25m, and it is now
selling for $40m.

```
Income return   = 1.25 / 35        = 3.571%   →  3.57%
Capital return  = (40 − 35) / 35   = 14.286%  → 14.29%
Total return    = 3.57 + 14.29                = 17.86%
```

> **The denominator is always the PURCHASE price, not the sale price.**
> `(40 − 35)/40` gives 12.5%, which is the profit as a share of what the
> asset is now worth — a different and much less useful quantity. Every
> return in this unit is measured against the capital you committed.

The lecture's follow-up: *what if you'd held it for five years?* 17.86% is
then a **holding-period return**, not an annual one. Converting it needs the
geometric mean, not division:

```
Annualised = (1 + HPR)^(1/n) − 1
           = (1.178571)^(1/5) − 1
           = 3.341%  →  3.34% p.a.
```

Dividing 17.86 by 5 gives 3.57% — wrong, and wrong in the flattering
direction, because it ignores compounding.

> **Never divide a multi-year return by the number of years.** Simple
> division always overstates the annual rate for a positive return. The gap
> is small over five years (3.57% vs 3.34%) and large over ten. Use the
> geometric mean, or an IRR.

## 1.4 Investment Objectives

Six factors. Every one of them shows up in the AREIT scenario for the
assignment, so learn them as a checklist you can run over any brief.

| Objective | The question it answers | 41 Park Road link |
|---|---|---|
| **Return preferences** | Income now, or growth later, or both? | "Target total return exceeding 7.0%" — a *total* return, so both |
| **Risk tolerance** | How much uncertainty can be borne? | "Minimal capex required" — low tolerance for capital risk |
| **Investment horizon** | How long is the money committed? | "7–10 year holding period" |
| **Liquidity** | How fast can it be converted to cash without loss? | Direct property is illiquid; a listed AREIT has unitholders who are not |
| **Amount available** | What is the opportunity set? | LVR 65% — so equity of roughly 40% of price once costs are added |
| **Tax considerations** | What survives to the after-tax line? | 30% company rate, no CGT discount |

Two of these are worth expanding.

**Liquidity.** Direct property is relatively illiquid: selling takes time and
incurs substantial transaction costs. An investor must retain enough liquidity
elsewhere to meet unforeseen expenses. Note the tension in the assignment: an
AREIT is a *listed* vehicle whose units are liquid, holding *illiquid* direct
assets. That mismatch is why AREITs trade at premiums and discounts to net
tangible assets.

**Amount available.** Capital determines both the opportunity set and the
achievable diversification. Limited funds restrict options; larger capital
buys access to bigger assets, broader diversification, and therefore lower
portfolio risk for the same expected return.

> **"Total return exceeding 7.0%" is not a 7% cap rate.** A cap rate is an
> income yield at a point in time; a total return includes growth. Under
> `r = y + g`, an asset bought on a 4.6% initial yield needs about 2.4% p.a.
> of income-and-value growth just to reach a 7% total return. Confusing the
> two turns an unacceptable deal into an acceptable one on paper — which is
> exactly the error the A2 rubric is looking for.

### Where investment objectives are disclosed

You will need to cite real objectives in A2, so know where they live.

| Direct property | Indirect (REITs / A-REITs) |
|---|---|
| Property investment prospectuses | Product Disclosure Statements (PDS) |
| **Information memoranda (IMs)** | Annual reports |
| Development project documents | ASX announcements |
| Agency marketing material | Fund manager websites |
| Feasibility studies, valuation reports | |

Example from the deck: an IM for a student accommodation development may
state an objective of annual income distributions of **6–7%** and long-term
capital growth over a **10-year** holding period. That is return preference,
return level and horizon in one sentence — the exact shape your A2
"investment objectives" section should take.

### The Australian disclosure regime

Three regimes, each producing a document you can cite.

**Product Disclosure Statement (PDS).** Under the *Corporations Act 2001*
(Cth), managed investment schemes and financial products offered to retail
investors generally require a PDS setting out investment objectives,
strategy, risks, fees and costs, expected returns and investor suitability.
ASIC oversees this and expects disclosure to be **clear, concise and
effective**, and not misleading or deceptive.

**Target Market Determination (TMD).** Under the Design and Distribution
Obligations regime, issuers must prepare a TMD identifying the type of
investor the product suits, the appropriate risk tolerance, investment
horizon and liquidity needs. Property example: an illiquid unlisted property
trust specifying suitability for investors with a **7+ year horizon and low
liquidity requirements**.

**Listed property securities.** A-REITs on the ASX must comply with
continuous disclosure, the ASX Listing Rules, and annual and half-year
reporting obligations.

> **A PDS and a TMD are not the same document and are not interchangeable
> in a reference list.** The PDS tells you what the product *is*; the TMD
> tells you who it is *for*. If A2 asks you to evidence an investor's risk
> tolerance and horizon, the TMD is the stronger citation.

## 1.5 Asset Classes

What could you invest in? Bonds, stocks, deposits, antiques, direct property,
indirect property, managed funds, art and jewellery, gold, futures, wine,
options, coins, mortgages, mutual funds, savings accounts — and on it goes.

Choice depends on **investment objectives** and the **characteristics of the
asset class**. Classes are distinguished by type (shares, property, bonds),
by whether they deliver income or growth, and by risk/return profile. They
sort into two families:

```
DEFENSIVE  — lower risk, lower return.  Cash, fixed interest.
             Suits short-horizon investors or those reducing volatility.

GROWTH     — potential for long-term capital gains, higher risk.
             Australian shares, international shares, property.
             Suits long-horizon investors who can wear short-to-medium
             term volatility.
```

The deck's classification grid — the shaded cells are the ones the lecture
filled in, the rest were set as homework:

| | Cash | Fixed interest | Shares | Direct property | Listed property |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Classification | Defensive | Defensive | Growth | Growth | Growth |
| Optimal horizon | Short–medium (1 to >5 yrs) | | | Long-term (5 yrs+) | |
| Risk | | Low to moderate | | Moderate to high | |
| Growth | None (unless reinvested) | Low | High | Moderate (high) | |

> **Direct and listed property are the same underlying asset with different
> risk.** Both are growth assets holding buildings. Listed property adds
> equity-market volatility and daily pricing; direct property adds
> illiquidity and lumpy transaction costs. Week 2 pushes this further — do
> not treat "property" as one row in a table.

## 1.6 Assumed Knowledge — Net Operating Income

From here the lecture recaps what USB144 taught, because A1 cannot be built
without it.

> **Net Operating Income (NOI)** — the income generated by a property after
> deducting all operating expenses, but **before interest, taxes and
> depreciation**.

```
NOI = Gross Rental Income − Operating Expenses
```

### Worked example

A commercial property generates $120,000 p.a. in rental income. Annual
operating expenses:

```
Council rates          $ 8,000
Insurance              $ 2,000
Property management    $ 6,000
Maintenance            $ 4,000
                       ───────
Total outgoings        $20,000

NOI = 120,000 − 20,000 = $100,000 p.a.
```

> **"Before interest, taxes and depreciation" is the whole definition.**
> Interest depends on how *you* financed it, not on the property; tax
> depends on who *owns* it. Deduct either one and you are no longer measuring
> the asset — you are measuring your position in it. This is exactly why the
> brief splits A1 (property cashflow) from A2 (equity and after-tax
> cashflows). Putting interest in the A1 NOI line loses marks twice: once for
> the wrong number, once for the wrong concept.

## 1.7 Assumed Knowledge — The Capitalisation Approach

```
Capital Value = Net Income / Capitalisation rate

CV = NI / Y
```

Two terms you need for the DCF:

- **Initial yield** — at the beginning, year 0, when you buy it.
- **Terminal yield** — at the end of the holding period, year *n*, when you
  sell it. Also called the exit or reversionary yield.

### Exercise 1a

*What is the property in §1.6 worth at a 7.5% cap rate?*

```
CV = 100,000 / 0.075 = $1,333,333
```

### Exercise 1b — reading a market transaction

> "GIC … purchased a 27,670 sqm site, featuring a 14,543 sqm concrete tilt
> panel office/warehouse, for $20m on a cap rate or yield of 8%."

Two questions: net income per sqm of building, and the site coverage ratio.

```
Net income   = CV × Y = 20,000,000 × 0.08 = $1,600,000 p.a.

Per sqm of BUILDING = 1,600,000 / 14,543 = $110.02/sqm
Per sqm of SITE     = 1,600,000 / 27,670 =  $57.82/sqm

Site coverage ratio = building area / site area
                    = 14,543 / 27,670 = 52.56%
```

Why site coverage matters: at 52.6% the site is roughly half built out.
The unbuilt half is hardstand, truck manoeuvring and parking — which an
industrial tenant needs — but it is also **latent development capacity**.
A low coverage ratio is either an operational feature or an expansion
opportunity, and which one it is changes the value.

> **Name the denominator every single time.** $110.02/sqm and $57.82/sqm
> are the same income measured against different areas. Rent is quoted on
> *lettable* area, land value on *site* area, and construction cost on
> *gross floor* area. A number quoted without its denominator is not an
> answer.

### Exercise 2 — building the net income first

*What would you expect to pay for a 5,486 sqm prime industrial property,
fully leased at $175/sqm gross, outgoings $62/sqm, cap rate 8%?*

```
Gross income  = 5,486 × 175 = $  960,050
Outgoings     = 5,486 ×  62 = $  340,132
Net income    =               $  619,918   (= 5,486 × $113/sqm)

Capital value = 619,918 / 0.08 = $7,748,975
```

> **`CV = NI / Y` takes NET income. Feeding it gross gives $12,000,625 —
> 55% too high.** The slide sets this up deliberately: it hands you a gross
> rate and an outgoings rate and makes you do the subtraction. In the
> assignment, the IM hands you a "Gross Rental Income" line first and a "Net
> Rental Income" line second; using the wrong one moves the answer by a
> million dollars a time.

## 1.8 Assumed Knowledge — Time Value of Money

Three forms, three exercises. All three were verified to the cent.

### Present value of a level annuity — Exercise 3

*A property returns a net rent of $16,200 p.a. for the remaining 12 years of
the lease. At 6.75%, what is that income stream worth today?*

```
              1 − 1/(1+r)^N
PV = PMT  ×  ───────────────
                    r

              1 − 1/(1.0675)^12
   = 16,200 × ─────────────────
                    0.0675

   = 16,200 × 8.0496       =  $130,403.51
```

Note what this *is*: the price someone would pay up front for the right to
receive $16,200 a year for twelve years, given a 6.75% required return. That
is a DCF with a flat cashflow — the whole of this unit is that idea with the
flat assumption removed.

### Present value of a single future sum — Exercise 4

*A block of units must be repainted in 7 years at a cost of $125,000. What
lump sum invested today at 6.45% covers it?*

```
            1               125,000
PV = FV × ───────    =    ──────────    =   $80,703.13
          (1+r)^n         (1.0645)^7
```

### Sinking fund payment — Exercise 5

*Same repaint. What equal annual payment, invested at 6.45%, accumulates to
$125,000 in 7 years?*

```
            (1+r)^n − 1                    FV × r          125,000 × 0.0645
FV = PMT × ─────────────    →    PMT =  ─────────────  =  ──────────────────
                 r                       (1+r)^n − 1        (1.0645)^7 − 1

                                                       =  $14,688.82
```

Sanity check: 7 × $14,688.82 = $102,821.74 of contributions, and the interest
earned makes up the remaining $22,178. If your PMT × n exceeds the FV, you
have inverted the formula.

> **PV and FV annuity formulas are one keystroke apart and answer opposite
> questions.** "What is a stream worth today" uses `1 − (1+r)^-n` over `r`.
> "What stream accumulates to a target" uses `(1+r)^n − 1` over `r`. On the
> Sharp EL-738XTB, the giveaway is which key you solve for: PV vs PMT with
> FV loaded. Write down which one you are solving for *before* you touch the
> calculator.

## 1.9 Net Present Value — The Bridge to Everything Else

Level annuities are fine until the cashflow stops being level. Then:

```
        n     CFt
NPV =   Σ   ───────
       t=1  (1+r)^t
```

### Exercise 6 — the five-year rental stream

An investor expects net rental income of **$20,800** in year 1, after
allowing for vacancy, management fees, maintenance, insurance and council
rates. Rents grow at **2.5% p.a.**; the required return is **9%**. What are
the next five years of rental income worth today?

```
 t    CFt          DF = 1/(1.09)^t      PV
───────────────────────────────────────────────
 1   $20,800          0.9174        $19,082.57
 2   $21,320          0.8417        $17,944.62
 3   $21,853          0.7722        $16,874.53
 4   $22,399          0.7084        $15,868.25
 5   $22,959          0.6499        $14,921.97
                              ─────────────────
                       Σ PVs =      $84,691.93
```

The lecture's rounded table gives $84,692 — it agrees to the dollar.

**Q: What does this tell you?** It tells you the *income only* is worth
$84,692 — no sale proceeds are included. It is not what the property is
worth. That distinction is the single most common mis-read in this material,
and it recurs in Week 2 in a worse form (see note 02, §2.9).

> **Growth of 2.5% and a discount rate of 9% are not netted off.** A
> tempting shortcut is to discount a flat $20,800 at `9% − 2.5% = 6.5%`.
> That gives $86,438 — close enough to look right, wrong by $1,746, and it
> collapses the moment growth varies year to year or a lease expires. Build
> the cashflow line, then discount it.

## Summary

- Investment is the commitment of capital expecting a return; return is
  **income + capital**, and *investment* implies the principal matters.
- There is a reward for bearing risk, so higher risk carries a higher
  **required** return — not a higher realised one.
- Percentage returns are comparable; dollar returns are not. Always divide
  by the **purchase price**, and annualise with the geometric mean.
- Six investment objectives: return preference, risk tolerance, horizon,
  liquidity, amount, tax. They are disclosed in PDSs, TMDs, IMs and annual
  reports.
- Defensive (cash, fixed interest) vs growth (shares, property).
- `NOI = gross income − operating expenses`, before interest, tax and
  depreciation.
- `CV = NI / Y` — always net income; name the denominator of every $/sqm.
- PV of an annuity, PV of a lump sum, and a sinking-fund PMT are the three
  time-value forms. NPV generalises all three to uneven cashflows, and that
  is the whole assignment.
