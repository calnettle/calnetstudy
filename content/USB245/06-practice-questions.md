## Section A — Investment Principles

### A1

Define investment, and explain how it differs from speculation and gambling.

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**Investment** is the commitment of capital to an asset in expectation of
obtaining additional income, profit or some future benefit. That benefit is
the **return**, and it has two components — **income return** (rent) and
**capital return** (appreciation).

The distinguishing feature is that *the safety of the principal matters*.
Investors seek a **risk-adjusted** return, priced off the time value of
money, expected inflation and future uncertainty.

**Speculation** and **gambling** both accept that risking the principal is
part of the exercise: speculation seeks an above-market return by taking on
risk deliberately, and gambling has a negative expected value. The line is
drawn by the treatment of principal, not by the size of the return sought.
</details>

### A2

An investor buys a property for $4.2m, receives net income of $231,000 over
the year, and the property is revalued at $4.62m at year end. Calculate the
income, capital and total returns. If the same total return had taken four
years, what is the annual return?

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

```
Income return   = 231,000 / 4,200,000                = 5.50%
Capital return  = (4,620,000 − 4,200,000)/4,200,000  = 10.00%
Total return    = 5.50 + 10.00                       = 15.50%
```

Over four years, 15.50% is a **holding-period** return. Annualise it
geometrically:

```
(1.1550)^(1/4) − 1 = 3.668%  →  3.67% p.a.
```

**Not** `15.50 / 4 = 3.875%`. Simple division always overstates the annual
rate for a positive return because it ignores compounding.
</details>

### A3

An investor says: "I want the highest return available, so I'll take the
highest-risk investment." Critique that statement in three sentences.

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Higher risk buys a higher **expected** return, not a higher realised one —
it widens the distribution of outcomes in both directions, which is what
makes it risk. Whether the investor *should* accept it depends on their
**risk tolerance**: the uncertainty and potential loss they are both
*willing* and *able* to bear, which is constrained by their liquidity needs,
horizon and capital. Rational investors therefore select on **risk-adjusted**
return, not on the headline number, which is why not every investor holds
development sites.
</details>

### A5

Where would you find an investor's stated objectives for (a) a direct
property investment and (b) an A-REIT? Name the regulator and the two
disclosure documents that apply to retail-offered products.

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**(a) Direct:** property investment prospectuses, **information memoranda**,
development project documents, agency marketing material, feasibility studies
and valuation reports.

**(b) Indirect / A-REIT:** Product Disclosure Statements, annual reports,
ASX announcements, fund manager websites.

**Regulator: ASIC**, under the *Corporations Act 2001* (Cth). The two
documents are the **Product Disclosure Statement (PDS)** — objectives,
strategy, risks, fees, expected returns, investor suitability — and the
**Target Market Determination (TMD)** under the Design and Distribution
Obligations regime, which identifies the investor type, appropriate risk
tolerance, horizon and liquidity needs. ASIC expects disclosure to be clear,
concise and effective, and not misleading or deceptive.
</details>

## Section B — Capitalisation and Affordability

### B1

A commercial property produces gross income of $850,000 p.a. Outgoings are:
council rates $62,000, land tax $41,000, insurance $55,000, management
$38,000, repairs $29,000. Calculate NOI and value the property at a 6.75%
capitalisation rate. What is the outgoings ratio, and is it plausible?

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

```
Outgoings = 62,000 + 41,000 + 55,000 + 38,000 + 29,000 = $225,000
NOI       = 850,000 − 225,000                          = $625,000

CV = 625,000 / 0.0675 = $9,259,259
```

Outgoings ratio `225,000 / 850,000 = 26.5%` of gross — comfortably inside
the normal 15–35% band for Australian commercial property, so the schedule
looks complete.

Feeding the **gross** income into `CV = NI/Y` gives $12,592,593 — 36% too
high.
</details>

### B3

A property is offered on a 5.25% initial yield. Your required total return is
7.25%. What annual growth does that price imply, and name two reasons the
relationship might not hold.

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

```
r = y + g   →   7.25% = 5.25% + g   →   g = 2.00% p.a.
```

Both income and capital value would need to grow at 2.00% p.a. for the
holding period.

Two reasons it might not hold:

1. **`r = y + g` assumes uninterrupted income in perpetuity.** Any lease
   expiry brings downtime, incentives and re-letting costs that break the
   smooth growth assumption — a property with a short WALE cannot deliver
   constant g.
2. **It assumes income growth and capital growth are the same number.** They
   diverge whenever the exit cap rate differs from the entry cap rate. Yield
   expansion of 50 basis points can wipe out several years of income growth.

Also acceptable: the formula ignores capex, which is a real cash drain that
never appears in y or g.
</details>

</details>

A building will need a $450,000 façade replacement in 9 years. At 4.8%,

### C3

Your required return is 8.25% p.a. effective. What monthly discount rate do

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

```
Correct:  (1.0825)^(1/12) − 1 = 0.662797%  per month
Check:    (1.00662797)^12 − 1 = 8.2500%    ✓
```

### C4

A property produces net income of $64,000 in year 1, growing at 2.8% p.a.

### C5

State the seven steps of a DCF, and explain why step 6 has a different sign
in the Week 2 grandmother exercise than it does in a property purchase.

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

```
1. Set up cashflows over period/time
2. Determine the Net Income for each period
3. Calculate the discount factor for each period
4. Calculate the PV of each period
5. Add up all the PVs
6. Adjust for any acquisition costs
7. Answer = NPV
```

In the grandmother exercise you are working out **how much you must invest**
to fund a stream of *outflows*. The $5,000 of legal and accounting fees is
an additional amount you must find, so step 6 **adds** it: `137,767 + 5,000 =
$142,767`. That figure is a gross present value, despite the slide labelling
it "NPV".

In a property purchase the cashflows are *inflows* and the price is an
outflow, so step 6 **subtracts** the total acquisition outlay:
`NPV = Σ PVs − price × (1 + acquisition cost %)`. Getting the sign backwards
in A1 puts your NPV out by roughly twice the acquisition cost.
</details>

## Section D — The Information Memorandum

### D1

An outgoings schedule shows a quarterly column and an annual column. Five of
six rows multiply by 4 to their annual figure exactly; the sixth shows
$2,458.33 quarterly against $29,500 annual. Diagnose the error and state
which column you would model from.

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

`2,458.33 × 4 = $9,833.32 ≠ $29,500`. But `29,500 / 12 = $2,458.33` exactly —
so the entry is a **monthly** figure sitting in a column headed "per
quarter".

You know it is the *quarterly* entry that is wrong, not the annual one,
because (a) the other five rows in the same column reconcile exactly, which
pins the inputs, and (b) the annual column sums to the stated annual total.

**Model from the annual column** and derive monthly as `annual ÷ 12`. Taking
the quarterly figure at face value understates annual outgoings by $19,667,
which at a 6.5% cap rate is roughly $302,000 of overstated value.
</details>

### D2

Four tenants pay $120,000, $80,000, $60,000 and $40,000 with 0.5, 3.0, 1.5
and 5.0 years remaining. Calculate the WALE by income. Why is it lower than
the simple average of the terms?

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

```
Total income = $300,000

WALE = (120,000×0.5 + 80,000×3.0 + 60,000×1.5 + 40,000×5.0) / 300,000
     = (60,000 + 240,000 + 90,000 + 200,000) / 300,000
     = 590,000 / 300,000
     = 1.967 years
```

The simple average of the four terms is `(0.5+3.0+1.5+5.0)/4 = 2.5 years`.
The income-weighted figure is lower because the **largest** tenant has the
**shortest** term — 40% of income expires in six months. WALE by income is
the right measure precisely because it captures that concentration; an
unweighted average hides it.
</details>

### D3

A tenancy schedule shows eight tenants and the IM says the income is "hedged
with the number of tenants and lease terms". One tenant is 43.5% of income;
80.5% of income expires within twelve months. Assess the claim.

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

The claim is **half true and materially misleading**.

Eight tenants is genuine diversification of **covenant** — no single
insolvency destroys the income, and the loss of any one small tenant is
survivable. That is the part the IM is entitled to say.

It is **not** diversification of **expiry**, and expiry is the risk that
matters over a 7-year hold. With 43.5% of income in one tenant and 80.5%
expiring inside a year, an incoming owner faces near-simultaneous re-letting
of most of the building: downtime on several suites at once, incentives,
leasing commissions and possible fitout contributions, all concentrated in
the first two years. The correct treatment in a DCF is to model each expiry
with its own downtime and incentive rather than assuming renewal.

The right sentence for a report: *"The IM characterises the income as hedged
by tenant number and lease term. Tenant diversification is real; expiry
diversification is not — 80.5% of gross income expires within twelve months
of the assessment date and a single tenant represents 43.5% of income."*
</details>

### D4

An IM states a gross floor area of 1,209 m² and gross rental income of
$454,237.52. A colleague reports "the building is rented at $376/m²". What
is wrong with that, and what would you report instead?

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

`454,237.52 / 1,209 = $375.71/m²`, so the arithmetic is right and the
**denominator is wrong**. GFA includes stairs, lift shafts, plant rooms,
lobbies and common area — none of which is leased. Rent is paid on

On a plausible 80–90% building efficiency the true gross rate is between
**$417/m² and $470/m²** — a spread of 25% against the naive figure. Since
the IM discloses no lettable area and no tenancy areas, the correct report is:

*"Gross income equates to $375.71/m² of GFA. Lettable area is not disclosed;
assuming 85% efficiency gives approximately 1,028 m² NLA and $442/m² gross,
which is the basis on which market comparison has been made."*

State the assumption, or the comparison to market evidence is meaningless.
</details>

### D5

An IM's outgoings show $97,364 of total outgoings and $13,387 of recovered
outgoings. Your DCF template assumes net leases with full recovery from
occupied area. What do you do, and what is it worth?

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**Override the template.** Recovery is `13,387 / 97,364 = 13.75%` of
outgoings — these are effectively **gross** leases, where the owner bears
the outgoings.

Applying the template's full-recovery assumption would add roughly
`97,364 − 13,387 = $83,978` p.a. of phantom recoveries to NOI. At a 6.5%
capitalisation rate that is about **$1.29 million** of overstated capital
value, on a building quoted at $8m — a 16% error.

Model recoveries at the disclosed level, escalate them, and state the
assumption. Note also that the recovery in this case is electricity from a
solar installation on-charged to tenants, not a conventional outgoings
recovery, so it should be grown with the electricity line rather than with
total outgoings.
</details>

## Section E — Risk and the Model

### E1

Distinguish systemic from non-systemic risk with two property examples of
each, and say what an investor can do about each family.

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**Systemic / external** risk affects every asset and cannot be diversified
away: legislative change, market movements, inflation, competition, finance
availability, supply and demand, the economy.
*Examples:* an interest-rate rise that expands cap rates across the market;
a planning-scheme amendment altering permitted uses.
*Response:* it cannot be diversified, so it is **priced** — into the discount
rate and the risk premium — and **tested** in sensitivity analysis.

**Non-systemic / internal** risk is specific to the asset or the tenant:
income, bankruptcy, customer, technology, plant and equipment, contractor,
obsolescence.
*Examples:* the largest tenant becoming insolvent; the lifts reaching end of
life.
*Response:* it **can** be diversified and managed — spread the rent roll,
stagger expiries, take bank guarantees, hold a capex sinking allowance,
diversify across buildings and sectors.

The marked distinction is that naming a risk is descriptive; naming the
**lever that addresses it** is analytical.
</details>

### E2

Name the three drivers of income risk for an income-producing property, and
for the tenant/lease driver list five things that matter.

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**Location → Building → Tenant/Lease.**

For tenants and leases (Rowland p. 28):

1. **Lease term and options** — noting an option is the tenant's right, not
   the landlord's, so it does not secure income
2. **Tenant quality** — financial strength, growth and vacancy potential
3. **Rent review mechanisms and frequency** — including the ratchet clause
   (a market review that can only go up)
4. **Responsibility for outgoings** — gross vs net lease
5. **Vacancy** — incentives, leasing commissions, rent-free periods, and the
   forecast long-term vacancy rate
</details>

### E3

Explain why obsolescence is described as "a feature specific to property as
an investment class", and what it implies for a DCF.

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

A share, a bond or a cash deposit does not physically deteriorate; a building
does, and it also becomes **functionally, technologically, stylistically and
environmentally** outdated even when perfectly maintained. Floor plates,
services, air conditioning, technology capacity and green ratings all age
relative to what tenants will accept.

Implications for the DCF:

- A **recurring capex allowance** is required, or NOI is overstated every
  year. It is not an operating expense — it sits below NOI in the property
  cashflow.
- A **one-off upgrade provision** may be required within the holding period,
  which is what makes the cashflow genuinely uneven.
- The **terminal cap rate** should usually be set above the initial yield,
  because you are selling a building that is seven years older.
- A DCF with no capex line has modelled a bond, not a building.
</details>

### E4

A model reports a monthly IRR of 0.68%. A team member writes "annual IRR
8.16%". Correct it, and state the decision consequence if the hurdle rate is
8.0%.

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

```
Wrong:    0.68% × 12          = 8.16%
Right:    (1.0068)^12 − 1     = 8.472%
```

The correct figure is **8.47%**, 31 basis points higher.

Consequence: both figures clear an 8.0% hurdle, so the recommendation is
unchanged *here* — but the error is systematic and always understates the
return, so on a marginal deal it would produce a false rejection. Annualise
by compounding: `(1 + monthly IRR)^12 − 1`.
</details>

### E5

Your annual sensitivity grid shows a base-case NPV of $620,209; your monthly
DCF reports $721,237 on identical assumptions. Explain, and say what you
write in the report.

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Nothing is broken. The grid discounts **annual** cashflows received at year
end; the monthly model receives cash **monthly** and discounts 84 times.
Money received earlier is worth more, so the monthly model produces the
higher present value. The $101,028 gap is a **timing-granularity**
difference, not an error.

In the report: quote the **monthly** NPV as the headline result — it is the
model the brief asked for — and footnote the grid as being computed on
annual cashflows for tractability, noting that the *relative* movements
across the grid are what matter, not the absolute cell values. Two
unexplained NPVs for one property reads as an error to a marker.
</details>

### E6

Your AREIT's objective is a "target total return exceeding 7.0%". Your model
reports a property IRR of 6.4% and an equity IRR of 9.8% at 65% LVR. What do
you recommend, and what must you disclose?

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

You **cannot answer** until you resolve whether the 7.0% target is ungeared
or geared — and you must say so explicitly rather than picking the
convenient reading.

- Read as **ungeared**, the property IRR of 6.4% **fails** the objective and
  the deal should be rejected or repriced.
- Read as **geared**, the equity IRR of 9.8% **passes**.

Disclose: (a) which reading you have adopted and why; (b) **both** figures,
so the board can see the difference; and (c) that the equity return is only
higher because debt at its current cost is below the property return — if
the property IRR falls below the interest rate, leverage would *reduce* the
equity return and amplify the loss. The gearing is adding return **and**
risk, and reporting only the flattering number is the failure mode the
rubric is watching for.

Then price it: solve for the purchase price at which the *property* IRR
reaches 7.0%, and recommend bidding at or below that number.
</details>

## Section F — Long-Form Practice

### F1

You are an investment analyst for a listed AREIT. A suburban mixed-use
building is offered at $8.0m with net rental income of $370,260 p.a., a WALE
by income of 0.98 years, 80% of income expiring within twelve months, and
only 13.75% of outgoings recovered from tenants. Your AREIT targets a total
return above 7.0% over 7–10 years with minimal capex, at 65% LVR.

Write the structure of your recommendation, with the three numbers that
drive it.

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**Structure** — follow the lecture's decision process:

```
Amount      →  Objectives  →  Return  →  Cashflow  →  Risk
```

1. **Amount.** At $8m with 5.5% acquisition costs, total outlay $8.44m; debt
   at 65% of price is $5.2m; **equity required $3.24m**.
2. **Objectives.** Restate the five AREIT criteria and score the asset
   against each. Location passes; capex, return and holding-period risk are
   in question.
3. **Return.** Net initial yield `370,260 / 8,000,000 =` **4.63%**. Under
   `r = y + g`, the price requires **2.37% p.a.** growth in income and value
   just to reach the 7.0% total return — before any allowance for downtime,
   incentives or capex.
4. **Cashflow.** Build the monthly DCF with each expiry modelled at its own
   downtime and incentive, recoveries at the disclosed 13.75%, and a capex
   allowance. Report NPV at the required return and IRR at $8m.
5. **Risk.** WALE of **0.98 years** with 80% of income expiring in year 1 is
   the dominant risk; a single tenant at 43.5% of income is the second.
   Sensitivity on discount rate × terminal cap rate; state the price at
   which NPV turns negative.

**The three numbers:** the **4.63% initial yield** (the price is aggressive
against passing income), the **0.98-year WALE** (the income is not secure),
and the **13.75% outgoings recovery** (the owner wears $83,978 p.a. of
outgoings). Together they say the quoted price prices the asset as though the
income were durable and net, and it is neither.

**Likely recommendation:** do not bid at $8m. Solve the DCF for the price at
which the property IRR reaches 7.0% and recommend bidding at or below it,
with the re-letting programme costed explicitly.
</details>

### F2

Explain, with a worked illustration, why capitalisation and DCF can give
materially different answers for the same property, and when you would trust
each.

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

`CV = NI / Y` assumes **year-1 net income is received in perpetuity**. A DCF
makes no such assumption — it models each period's actual cashflow.

Worked illustration. Take net income of $370,260 and a 6.5% cap rate:

```
Capitalisation:  370,260 / 0.065 = $5,696,306
```

Now suppose 80% of that income expires within twelve months, each expiry
carries 4–6 months of downtime and 3 months of rent-free incentive, and the
re-let rents are at market rather than the escalated passing rent. Year 2's
cashflow might be 40–50% below year 1's, and years 1–3 carry leasing
commissions and fitout contributions. Every one of those effects reduces the
present value, and **none of them appears in the capitalisation** — the
capitalisation values a $370,260 perpetuity that does not exist.

**Trust the capitalisation** when income is stable and long-dated, the
building is fully leased on market rents, and you want a fast cross-check
against comparable sales evidence. It is the market's language and the way
sales are quoted.

**Trust the DCF** when income is uneven — expiries, vacancy, incentives,
staged capex, a known market shift — which is exactly the situation the Week
2 lecture lists as the reason DCF exists: a 25% market movement forecast, a
major expiry, or refurbishment plans.

Best practice is to run both and explain the gap. If the DCF value sits well
below the capitalisation, the difference *is* your risk story, quantified.
</details>
