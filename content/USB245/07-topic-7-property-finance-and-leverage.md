# TOPIC 7 — Property Finance and Leverage

Everything up to Week 6 was an **unlevered** cashflow: the property's own
income and the property's own return, as if you paid cash. Week 7 adds
**debt**. The cashflow stops being about the property and starts being
about the **equity** — what *your* money earns, once the lender has been
paid.

That single change is what makes the rest of the unit work. Interest is
the largest deduction in Week 8's tax calculation, and the Equity IRR is
what the A2 board recommendation actually turns on.

Reading: Rowland Chapters 3, 4 and 6.

## 14.1 The Capital Stack

The lecture opens with the **funding silo** (or capital stack): where the
money to fill the silo comes from, and what each layer costs.

| Layer | Share of total cost | Indicative return | Security |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Equity** | top ~20% (above 80% LVR) | 25%+ | Unsecured — **lowest** priority of repayment |
| **Mezzanine** | 60–80% | ~15%+ | Subordinated |
| **Senior debt** | 0–60% | ~8% | Secured — **highest** priority of repayment |

Read it as a risk ladder. The senior lender is paid first and accepts ~8%;
the equity holder is paid last and demands 25%+ for standing behind
everyone else. The lecture also flags that **development lending and
investment lending are different animals** — this unit is about investment
lending, secured against an income-producing asset.

## 14.2 What Financial Leverage Is

> **Leverage = the use of borrowed money.** Lending or borrowing against
> the value of, and the cash flow generated by, a property.

Loans in this market are generally **secured against the property** by way
of a mortgage, and **serviced from the rental income** of the property.

Why borrow at all? The lecture gives four reasons:

- Investors need funding to buy new properties.
- Investors have insufficient equity.
- Investors need to get a return on funds.
- The potential benefits of debt funding are a strong incentive on their own.

### The two ratios

```
Leverage ratio        LR = V/E = (D + E)/E = D/E + 1
Loan-to-value ratio   LVR = Outstanding loan balance / Property value
```

Where `V` = total property value, `E` = value of the equity investment
(`E = V − D`), and `D` = the loan or debt value.

**Worked example.** An investor buys a $10,000,000 property using
$4,000,000 of their own money and $6,000,000 of debt.

```
LR   = $10,000,000 / $4,000,000  =  2.5
     = D/E + 1 = 6/4 + 1         =  2.5    ✓ both forms agree
LVR  = $6,000,000 / $10,000,000  =  60%
```

Leverage of 2.5 means the investor controls an asset **2.5 times** the
money they put in.

> **LR and LVR are not the same number and are not interchangeable.** LR is
> value ÷ equity and runs from 1 upwards (1 = no debt). LVR is debt ÷ value
> and runs from 0 to 1 (0 = no debt). A 60% LVR is an LR of 2.5. Exam
> questions switch between them inside a single scenario.

## 14.3 Positive and Negative Leverage

This is the core idea of the week. Derived from the WACC formula:

```
rE = rD + LR(rP − rD)
```

| Symbol | Meaning |
|---|---|
| `rE` | Return on equity |
| `rD` | Return on debt — i.e. the loan interest rate |
| `rP` | Return on the property |
| `LR` | Leverage ratio (`D/E + 1`) |

Everything hangs on the **spread**, `(rP − rD)`:

| Condition | Name | Effect of more debt |
|---|---|---|
| `rP > rD` | **Positive leverage** | Increases return on equity |
| `rP < rD` | **Negative leverage** | **Decreases** return on equity |
| `rP = rD` | Neutral | No effect — `rE = rD` whatever the gearing |

> **Leverage does not create return. It magnifies a spread that already
> exists — in both directions.** If the property earns more than the debt
> costs, borrowing lifts the equity return. If it earns less, borrowing
> *destroys* equity return faster than it would have declined unlevered.
> The lecture's phrasing: "when the cost of borrowing exceeds the rate of
> return on assets, leverage is unfavourable."

### Exercise 1 — leverage when the spread is positive

Property cost $1,000,000; net income $100,000 (so `rP` = 10%); interest-only
mortgage at **8%**.

| | a | b | c |
|---|---|---|---|
| Property cost | $1,000,000 | $1,000,000 | $1,000,000 |
| LVR | 80% | 50% | 0% |
| Loan | $800,000 | $500,000 | $0 |
| Equity | $200,000 | $500,000 | $1,000,000 |
| Net income before finance | $100,000 | $100,000 | $100,000 |
| Interest payment | $64,000 | $40,000 | $0 |
| Net income after finance | $36,000 | $60,000 | $100,000 |
| **Return on equity** | **18%** | **12%** | **10%** |

```
ROE = $ return after finance / equity invested
    = 36,000 / 200,000 = 18%
```

All three verified independently. `rP` = 10% > `rD` = 8%, so the spread is
positive and **more debt gives more return**: 18% > 12% > 10%.

Check it against the formula. At 80% LVR, `LR` = 1,000,000/200,000 = 5:

```
rE = 8% + 5 × (10% − 8%) = 8% + 10% = 18%   ✓
```

### Exercise 2 — the same property when the spread goes negative

Identical property, interest rate now **18%**:

| | a | b | c |
|---|---|---|---|
| LVR | 80% | 50% | 0% |
| Interest payment | $144,000 | $90,000 | $0 |
| Net income after finance | **−$44,000** | $10,000 | $100,000 |
| **Return on equity** | **−22%** | **2%** | **10%** |

The ranking **inverts completely**. `rP` = 10% < `rD` = 18%, so the
geared position is now the worst one, and the 80% LVR case does not merely
underperform — it loses money in cash terms. As the lecture puts it: here
"it pays to maximise the cash put into the property and minimise the amount
of borrowed money."

> **The tutorial workbook runs this same exercise at 12%, not 18%, and
> gets 2% / 8% / 10%.** Both are internally correct — they are just
> different interest rates. Verified: at 12%, ROE = 2%, 8%, 10%; at 18%,
> ROE = −22%, 2%, 10%. Don't try to reconcile the deck's table to the
> spreadsheet's; note instead that 12% is the *break-even-ish* case where
> the spread has just gone negative, and 18% is the disaster case. If an
> exam question quotes one of these tables, read the interest rate off the
> question, not off memory.

### The capital-allocation point

The lecture runs three scenarios and draws a deliberately
counter-intuitive pair of conclusions:

- **As equity increases, the *profit* increases** — you pay less interest,
  so more dollars land in your pocket.
- **As equity increases, the *return* plummets** — profit on cost rises
  slightly, but equity IRR falls off a cliff.

> **Percentage return is the better measure, not dollars.** More of your
> own money in a deal will almost always produce more dollars of profit and
> a worse return on those dollars. The question is never "did I make
> money?" but "is this the best allocation of *this* capital?"

## 14.4 How Much Can You Borrow?

> The degree of leverage available is largely determined by **NOI**.

Lenders use two tests. Note which one is the primary one — it is not the
one students expect.

**1. Debt service coverage ratio (DCR)** — an **income-based** test:

```
DCR = NOI / Debt Service
```

where debt service = the loan payment (interest, or interest + principal).
It is not a 1:1 test — lenders want a buffer, typically **DCR > 1.2**, set
by risk appetite and where the market cycle is. Dividing NOI by the minimum
acceptable DCR gives the annual debt service the property must be able to
meet, and from that the maximum loan.

**2. Loan-to-value ratio (LVR)** — a **value-based** test. Lenders specify
a maximum, typically **60–65% for commercial investments**.

> **DCR is the primary decision factor, not LVR.** The lecture is explicit:
> the question the lender asks first is *"can the loan be serviced from the
> NOI of the property?"* — not *"how much is it worth?"* Valuations move;
> rent rolls pay the interest. Students routinely answer "how much can I
> borrow" with LVR alone and lose the marks for the DCR test.

How much leverage is enough? The lecture declines to give a number: the
benefits are easily overstated, more borrowing makes the investment
increasingly risky, and increasing borrowing relative to equity may itself
**drive up the cost of borrowing** (pushing `rD` toward `rP` and killing
the spread that made gearing attractive). Case-by-case analysis.

### Why gear at all, given the risk?

The lecture's list, worth knowing as exam bullet points:

- Buy a more expensive property than otherwise possible.
- Acquire **more separate** properties — which can *reduce* overall risk
  through diversification.
- Increase returns to equity (with correspondingly higher potential losses).
- Control a larger investment, so a given percentage capital gain is
  realised on a larger base.
- As long as the **debt service constant** (annual debt service as a % of
  the amount borrowed) is lower than the return on total assets, more
  leverage increases the equity return.
- **Tax.** Interest is deductible, so the after-tax cost of borrowing is
  lower than the headline rate — and the higher the borrower's marginal tax
  rate, the lower that after-tax cost. Depreciation allowances are
  deductible even on assets bought with borrowed money. (This is the hinge
  into Topic 8.)

## 14.5 What a Mortgage Actually Is

A nice piece of etymology the lecture uses to make the point stick:
*mortgage* comes from **mort** (death) + **gage** (pledge) — a "dying
pledge". The pledge dies one of two ways:

- **Repayment** — the debt dies because you paid it off in full.
- **Default** — the borrower's property rights die, and the lender takes
  the property.

Mechanically: a loan to buy or refinance property, **secured by a mortgage
over that property**, which becomes an interest in the land to the benefit
of the lender. The land is **encumbered**. The lender's rights are
**registered on the title** (e.g. first registered mortgage). When the loan
is repaid the lender provides a **Discharge of Mortgage**, which is
registered to cancel the encumbrance; the property is then
**unencumbered**.

A mortgage agreement is **two documents**, and the exam likes this
distinction:

| | 1. The loan agreement | 2. The mortgage document |
|---|---|---|
| **What it does** | Creates the debt; specifies terms and conditions | Pledges the property as security for that debt |
| **Contains** | Loan amount, term, fees, interest rates, time and method of payment, default penalties, acceleration and prepayment clauses | The covenants: pay the debt, insure the property, pay rates and taxes, maintain the property |
| **Other clauses** | — | Lender may collect reserves for taxes/insurance; rent assigned to lender on default; lender must release the mortgage promptly on repayment; lender may assign the mortgage on |
| **Why both?** | Enforceable at law but **of little value without security** | The security instrument that protects the mortgagee against the mortgagor defaulting |

**Mortgagee** = the lender. **Mortgagor** = the borrower. Getting these the
wrong way round in a written answer is a cheap mark to lose.

## 14.6 Mortgage Mathematics — the Notation

| Symbol | Meaning |
|---|---|
| `PMT` | Total regular mortgage payment |
| `INT` (`IPMT`) | Interest component of that payment |
| `AMORT` (`PPMT`) | Principal amortised — i.e. paid down |
| `r` or `i` | Mortgage interest rate, **per period** |
| `t` | Any periodical payment through the term |
| `T` | Mortgage term (life of the contract) |
| `OB` | Outstanding loan balance; `OB₀` = the original loan amount |

Two rules govern every mortgage question in this unit:

```
PMTt   = INTt + AMORTt
INTt   = OBt−1 × rt
```

Read the second one carefully: **interest accrues on last period's closing
balance**, not this period's. That one-period lag is what makes an
amortisation schedule a schedule rather than a formula.

## 14.7 Structure 1 — Interest-Only Mortgages

```
PMTt = INTt              where INTt = OBt−1 × rt
If AMORTt = 0  then  OBt = Loan  for all t
PMTT = INTT + OB₀        the final payment repays the whole principal
```

Characteristics:

- The outstanding balance is **constant** for the life of the loan; the
  entire original principal is repaid as a **lump sum at maturity**.
- Payments are purely interest — fixed and level if the rate is fixed.
- Normally a relatively **short term, 3 to 5 years**.
- **Most common loan type for commercial property**, and used extensively
  for taxation purposes.

**Advantages:** straightforward; low, regular, unchanging payments (easy to
budget and administer); payments may be tax deductible.

**Disadvantages:** the **balloon payment** at maturity requires refinancing
or a sale; because principal is never paid down it **maximises total
interest paid** over the holding period; can carry slightly higher rates;
lack of paydown increases default risk; equity builds more slowly.

## 14.8 Structure 2 — Fully Amortising (Constant Payment) Mortgages

```
PMTt = PMT, constant for all t
```

Fully amortising means the payments **fully repay the loan by the end of
the term** — no balloon. `PMT` stays constant at a fixed rate, but its
`INT` and `AMORT` components shift over time: interest-heavy early,
principal-heavy late.

Terms of **15 to 30 years** are common. **Most common loan type for
residential mortgages, not commercial property** — the mirror image of
interest-only.

The payment comes from the annuity formula:

```
              Loan × r
PMT = ─────────────────────
                    1
         1 −  ───────────
              (1 + r)^T
```

which is just `PV = PMT × [1 − (1+r)^−n] / r` rearranged.

**Worked example — $1,000,000 at 12%, 30 years, monthly:**

```
r = 0.12/12 = 0.01,  T = 360

PMT = 1,000,000 × 0.01 / (1 − 1/1.01³⁶⁰) = $10,286.13
```

Verified. Compare it to the interest-only payment on the same loan
($10,000): only **$286.13 a month** more buys full amortisation.

First month's split:

```
INT₁   = $1,000,000 × 0.01      = $10,000.00
AMORT₁ = $10,286.13 − $10,000   =    $286.13
```

> **After 15 years — half the loan's life — the balance is still
> $857,057.** Verified. Less than **15%** of the principal is repaid in the
> first half of a 30-year CPM. Almost all of the early payment is interest,
> so the balance declines very slowly; the principal is paid down rapidly
> only in the later years. This is the fact that surprises people, and it
> is why an investor with a 5–10 year holding period barely amortises
> anything.

**Advantages:** builds equity faster; no balloon payment, so no
refinancing stress at maturity (and reduced stress if you exit early);
a longer term reduces financial risk; can carry slightly *lower* rates than
interest-only; principal paydown reduces default risk over time; low
payments are possible with long amortisation periods; constant payments at
a fixed rate are easy to budget; flexible trade-offs between payment and
term.

**Disadvantages:** higher payments than interest-only — particularly on
shorter terms — hence **reduced cashflow**; and the critical one for this
unit, **the principal component is not tax deductible**.

> **Only the interest is deductible — never the principal.** This is the
> bridge to Topic 8 and the single most common error in an after-tax DCF.
> In cashflow terms you pay `PMT`; in tax terms you deduct only `IPMT`.
> Two different numbers doing two different jobs in the same model.

## 14.9 Why We Care — Finance in the DCF

```
Before finance:   property cashflow   →   property IRR
After finance:    equity cashflow     →   Equity IRR (EIRR)
```

The lecture's own summary: an after-finance (and later after-tax) cashflow
is **a more holistic approach to investment analysis**, because it lets the
return on **equity** be assessed rather than the return on the asset.

Structurally, three new lines enter the model:

| Where | Line | Sign |
|---|---|---|
| Period 0 | **Plus** loan received | Positive — it reduces your outlay |
| Every period | **Less** interest repayment (`IPMT`) | Negative |
| Every period | **Less** principal repayment (`PPMT`) | Negative |
| Final period | **Less** outstanding loan repaid | Negative |

And one line changes meaning: period 0 is no longer the purchase price,
it is **your equity contribution** — price + acquisition costs − loan.

The worked version of all of this, on the Week 3 residential model, is in
[Tutorial 5](#/USB245/13-tutorial-5-mortgages-and-the-after-finance-dcf).

## Checkpoint

1. A property returns 9%. Debt costs 9%. You gear to 70% LVR. What
   happens to the return on equity?
2. An investor has $300,000 equity in a $1,200,000 property. State the LVR
   and the leverage ratio.
3. A lender offers 65% LVR, with a minimum DCR of 1.25. The property's NOI
   is $500,000 and the value is $6,000,000. Interest-only at 7%. What
   caps the loan?
4. Why does an interest-only loan produce a *higher* after-tax return than
   an amortising loan on the same property, even though the amortising
   loan builds equity?

<details><summary>Answers</summary>

1. **Nothing.** The spread `(rP − rD)` is zero, so `rE = rD + LR × 0 = 9%`
   at any gearing level. Leverage magnifies a spread; with no spread there
   is nothing to magnify.
2. `D = 1,200,000 − 300,000 = $900,000`.
   **LVR = 900/1,200 = 75%.** **LR = V/E = 1,200/300 = 4.0** (check:
   `D/E + 1 = 3 + 1 = 4` ✓).
3. **The DCR test.**
   - LVR test: `0.65 × $6,000,000 = $3,900,000`.
   - DCR test: maximum debt service = `NOI / 1.25 = $500,000 / 1.25 =
     $400,000`. At 7% interest-only, that supports
     `$400,000 / 0.07 = $5,714,286`.

   The **LVR** is the binding constraint here at $3.9m — but note that you
   only know that by computing *both*. Reverse the numbers (a low-yielding
   asset) and DCR binds instead. The lecture's point stands: DCR is the
   lender's primary question, LVR is the cap.
4. **Because only interest is deductible.** Interest-only maximises the
   deductible payment and minimises the non-deductible one, so it maximises
   the tax shield and preserves cashflow. Amortising converts deductible
   interest into non-deductible principal repayment over time — you are
   buying equity with after-tax dollars. It is a genuine trade-off, not a
   free lunch: you finish the term owning more of the asset.
</details>

## Summary

- The capital stack runs senior debt (~8%, secured, paid first) →
  mezzanine (~15%) → equity (25%+, unsecured, paid last).
- `LR = V/E = D/E + 1`; `LVR = Debt / Value`. Different ratios, different
  scales, both examinable.
- `rE = rD + LR(rP − rD)`. **Positive leverage when `rP > rD`; negative
  when `rP < rD`.** Verified: at 8% interest the ROE ranking is 18/12/10%;
  at 18% it inverts to −22/2/10%.
- More equity = more dollars of profit but a **worse** percentage return.
  Percentage is the right measure.
- Lenders cap the loan with **DCR** (income, primary, buffer >1.2) and
  **LVR** (value, 60–65% commercial).
- Mortgage = loan agreement **+** mortgage document. Mortgagee lends;
  mortgagor borrows. Discharge of mortgage unencumbers the title.
- `PMTt = INTt + AMORTt`; `INTt = OBt−1 × rt`.
- **Interest-only:** constant balance, balloon at maturity, 3–5 years,
  commercial norm. **Fully amortising:** constant payment, shifting
  composition, 15–30 years, residential norm; after 15 of 30 years less
  than 15% of principal is repaid.
- Only **interest** is deductible. Principal never is.
- Two slide errors verified and corrected in §14.7 (`0.0583` should be
  `0.005833`; `$10,000` belongs to the 12% example) and one in §14.8
  (rows labelled 358–360 are months 298–300). One genuinely ambiguous
  workbook answer in Exercise 5(d).
