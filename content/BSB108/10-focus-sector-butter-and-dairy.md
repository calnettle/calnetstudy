# THE FOCUS SECTOR — Butter and Dairy Product Manufacturing

Every number the sector dossier needs, reconciled. The source is the
IBISWorld report on **Butter and Dairy Product Manufacturing in Australia**
(ANZSIC 1133 / C1133c, July 2025), cross-checked against its own Key
Statistics tables and against the Week 1 lecture.

Three figures in that report do **not** reconcile. They are flagged where
they occur, with the arithmetic that proves which reading is right.

> **"Five-year growth 2020–2025" means FY2019-20 to FY2024-25 — the base is
> the earlier financial year, not 2020–21.** Every one of those sixteen
> CAGRs reconciles on that reading and none reconciles on any other. This
> is what makes the rest of the reconciliation trustworthy: the base and
> the compounding convention are now pinned down, so any figure that does
> *not* fit is a genuine discrepancy rather than a misunderstanding on our
> part.

The profit margin, with the base named:

```
357.9 ÷ 8,520.4 = 4.2005%   ✓ stated 4.2%
(not 357.9 ÷ 8,500 = 4.211%)
```

And the average wage:

```
$687.8m ÷ 7,517 employees = $91,499.27   ✓ stated $91,499
```

> **The rounded "$8.5bn" is the wrong denominator for every derived
> percentage in the report.** Use $8,520.4m. The difference is small on the
> margin (4.20% vs 4.21%) but it is the difference between reproducing the
> report's figure and disagreeing with it — and a marker checking your
> arithmetic will find the disagreement, not the rounding.

Also note the "0.1 pp" movement on the profit margin: profit margin change
is quoted in **percentage points**, while every other metric is quoted as a
**CAGR in per cent**. Read the units off the table before you copy them.

## 10.2 What the sector makes

**Definition** (IBISWorld): manufacturers primarily producing dairy
products such as butter, yoghurt, condensed milk, evaporated milk and other
dairy products. **Cheese, ice cream, milk and milk powder manufacturing are
not included.**

Four product lines, revenue 2025:

| Segment | Revenue | Share |
|---|---|---|
| **Yoghurt and other cultured products** | $4.1bn | **47.9%** |
| Proteins | $1.7bn | 20.2% |
| Other dairy products | $1.6bn | 18.6% |
| Butter products | $1.1bn | 13.3% |
| **Total** | **$8.5bn** | **100.0%** |

```
1.7 + 1.1 + 4.1 + 1.6 = 8.5    ✓
20.2 + 13.3 + 47.9 + 18.6 = 100.0  ✓
```

> **SOURCE ERROR — the report's own "At a Glance" products table drops the
> largest segment.** The summary page on p. 3 lists only three lines:
> Proteins 20.2%, Butter products 13.3%, Other dairy products 18.6% —
> summing to **52.1%**. Yoghurt and other cultured products, at 47.9% and
> $4.1bn, is missing entirely. The full segmentation on p. 14 lists all
> four and sums to 100.0%, and elsewhere the report explicitly names
> "$4.1bn Yoghurt and other cultured products" as the **largest market**.
> The full segmentation is right; the summary table is incomplete. A
> student who reads only the At a Glance page will describe a sector where
> butter is the second-largest line, when in fact **yoghurt is 3.6 times
> larger than butter** and butter is the *smallest* of the four.

What each segment contains:

| Segment | Contents |
|---|---|
| **Yoghurt and other cultured** | Yoghurt, sour cream, other cultured dairy-based products including cultured buttermilk |
| **Butter products** | Butter blends, pure butter, anhydrous milkfat (pure milkfat, as distinct from butter which also contains water and added salt) |
| **Proteins** | Casein and whey. Casein is a basic component of cheese; whey is a byproduct of cheesemaking, sold as protein powder |
| **Other dairy products** | Custard, condensed milk, flavoured beverages, dairy desserts |

Related terms the report defines: **casein**, a major protein accounting for
around **80% of milk protein**, often a binding medium in foods like
margarine; **whey**, a byproduct of cheese or casein manufacturing;
**farmgate milk price**, the price received by farmers when selling milk.

> **Half the sector's revenue passes through two retailers, and that is the
> buyer-power finding.** Supermarkets and grocery stores are 50.2% of
> revenue, and IBISWorld states plainly that manufacturers "lack power in
> negotiations with supermarkets because securing shelf space is vital".
> Coles and Woolworths negotiate directly for lower prices, and cost-of-
> living pressure pushes consumers toward low-cost private-label product,
> which raises the retailers' leverage further. Buyer power is rated
> **moderate and increasing**.

> A $55 million spread, from shares rounded to one decimal place. Never
> reverse-engineer a total from a rounded share. The actual total is
> $8,520.4m, from the Key Statistics table.

**Concentration dynamics.** IBISWorld puts industry concentration at
approximately **51.9% in 2024–25** and describes it as considerably high.
It records an estimated **4.3% increase in market concentration in
2020–21**, driven by Bega's acquisition of Lion Dairy and Drinks. Bega's own
series shows the effect:

| Year | Bega market share | Bega revenue $m |
|---|---|---|
| 2020 | 4.4% | 323.9 |
| **2021** | **10.7%** | **817.2** |
| 2022 | 13.8% | 1,125.6 |
| 2025 | 16.2% | 1,378.7 |

```
Bega share  4.4% → 10.7%  = +6.3 percentage points in one year
Bega revenue 323.9 → 817.2 = +152.3%
```

Since then: Lactalis acquired Jalna Dairy Foods (2021–22); Saputo closed
its Maffra, Victoria site and sold two milk processing facilities to Coles
for $105.0 million in June 2024; Fonterra announced divestment of its
consumer-facing business in favour of B2B; Bega acquired Betta Milk,
Meander Valley Dairy and a Pyengana Dairy licence from TasFoods for **$11.0
million** in November 2023; and **Beston Global Food Company**, the largest
South Australian dairy company, **collapsed in September 2024**.

> **Rising concentration here is a symptom of decline, not of success.**
> The usual reading of a rising CR4 is that leaders are winning. IBISWorld's
> reading is the opposite: smaller players cannot compete for a shrinking
> milk supply at high inflation, so they exit, and the survivors' share
> rises on a falling base. Revenue fell at −1.8% a year over the same five
> years in which concentration rose. Say which mechanism you mean.

**Barriers to entry** (moderate and increasing): legal — strict food
standards codes and state or territory licences, with DAFF regulating dairy
trade; **start-up costs** — the initial investment in manufacturing
equipment and production facilities is the most significant barrier, worsened
by rising industrial land rents; **differentiation** — majors acquire
established brands to expand portfolios quickly; **capital expenses** —
economies of scale that new small-scale entrants cannot match.

**Substitutes** (moderate and increasing): **imports**, particularly butter,
buttermilk and casein; and **plant-based products** — margarine, and
coconut-, soy- and other plant-based yoghurts. Total household consumption
of plant-based substitutes reached an estimated **472.6 kilotonnes in
2023–24**, growing at an annualised **1.1%** over the four years to
2023–24. Consumption of plant-based fats and oils has *declined* as
awareness grows of the cardiovascular impacts of products like margarine —
which IBISWorld reads as an opportunity for dairy butter.

## 10.5 Cost structure

Operating costs as a share of revenue, 2025, against the manufacturing
sector average:

| Cost | Industry | Sector average |
|---|---|---|
| **Purchases** | **72.3%** | 57.97% |
| Wages | 8.1% | 13.53% |
| Other costs | 8.3% | 14.05% |
| **Profit** | **4.2%** | 6.91% |
| Utilities | 3.7% | 3.14% |
| Depreciation | 2.5% | 3.02% |
| Rent | 0.9% | 1.39% |

```
72.3 + 8.1 + 8.3 + 4.2 + 3.7 + 2.5 + 0.9 = 100.0  ✓
```

Two benchmark labels the report attaches: profit margin **lower than
sector**; average wage **higher than sector**.

> **Purchases at 72.3% of revenue against a sector norm of 58% is the
> single most important number in the dossier.** It is 14.3 percentage
> points above the manufacturing average, and it is why the margin is 2.7
> points below it. This sector converts milk into product with very little
> value added in between — recall that industry value added is only
> $1,261.0m against $8,520.4m of revenue, **14.8%**. Every conclusion about
> the sector's vulnerability follows from that structure: input price is
> almost the whole business.
>
> Do the sensitivity: a 10% rise in purchase costs is 7.23 percentage
> points of revenue against a 4.2% margin. The margin is gone nearly twice
> over. That is the arithmetic behind "the shrinking milk pool" being the
> sector's headline issue.

Trend commentary: purchases rose sharply when a 2022–23 drop in milk
production spiked the farmgate price; inflation lifted utility and rent
costs, with rents pushed up by shrinking industrial land supply;
**depreciation crept up** as the industry adopted more automated machinery
and majors expanded; wages fell as a share as automation and factory
consolidation reduced reliance on labour.

> **SOURCE ERROR — NSW population share is stated twice with two values.**
> The Business Locations table gives NSW at **31.3%** of population; the

> accounting for **31.2%** of the total population, as of 2023-24". One

> is internally consistent and 31.2 is the outlier.

**Establishments versus businesses.** 247 establishments against 134
businesses — an average of **1.84 establishments per business**. Those are
different units and the report uses both. An establishment is a physical
site; a business (enterprise) can own several.

**Why Victoria dominates:** Victoria is home to just under two-thirds of
Australia's dairy cattle and produced over two-thirds of the country's milk
in 2022–23. Manufacturers locate near fresh raw milk to minimise
farm-to-factory transport costs and maintain quality. Victoria's protein
products account for over half of dairy product exports in 2024–25.

**Why NSW is second:** the largest population (31.3%), providing downstream
demand and a large hospitality customer base, despite producing just over
one-tenth of raw milk.

**Why QLD and WA are under-represented:** limited access to milk inputs.
Drinking-milk processors obtain almost all Queensland-made milk, because
Queensland has smaller farm sizes and disparate farm locations. In WA,
non-drinking milk made up less than one-fifth of the state's milk in
2022–23, and Coles created direct purchasing contracts to source WA milk
for private-label milk, leaving less for butter and dairy manufacturers.

## 10.7 The economics of the sector, in one causal chain

```
climate volatility (drought, flood)
     ↓
milk production falls, dairy cattle numbers decline
     ↓
farmgate milk price rises (>25% in 2022-23 on a 6% output fall)
     ↓
purchase costs rise — already 72.3% of revenue
     ↓
margin (4.2%) compressed; small manufacturers exit
     ↓
milk allocated to HIGHER-RETURN products (proteins, yoghurt)
     ↓
domestic butter production falls
     ↓
BUTTER IMPORTS rise to fill unmet demand (mostly New Zealand)
     ↓
concentration rises as survivors consolidate (CR4 = 51.9%)
```

That chain is the report's argument, and every link in it has a number
attached above. It is the most efficient way to structure the sector
material in either assessment.

## 10.8 Key external drivers

| Driver | Impact |
|---|---|
| Demand from supermarkets and grocery stores | Positive |
| Cheese, butter and yoghurt consumption | Positive |
| Domestic price of milk | Positive |
| Milk production | Positive |
| **Trade-weighted index** | **Negative** |

> **"Domestic price of milk — positive" reads backwards until you remember
> whose price it is.** A higher milk price raises the *value* of the
> manufacturer's output, and IBISWorld classifies it as a positive revenue
> driver. It is also a cost, through purchases. The two effects run in
> opposite directions and the report's own volatility section makes the
> point that manufacturers "struggled to pass these higher costs on to
> consumers because of competitive market pressures and consumer price
> sensitivity, cutting into revenue". Do not quote the driver table without
> that qualification.

## 10.10 Outlook to 2029–30

- Revenue to **$8,566.6m** at +0.1% a year — essentially flat in real terms
- Milk production set to fall further on declining dairy cattle numbers and
  low rainfall restricting feed
- Consumer demand for butter and dairy products to inch upwards on health
  consciousness, so **imports grow** to fill the gap
- More cost-saving and consolidation
- Competition from **plant-based alternatives** intensifying, though many
  cannot replace dairy's nutritional value — oat yoghurt does not match
  dairy protein content
- Exports to grow slowly: **A-UKFTA** opens the UK (tariff-free quotas for
  butter, yoghurt and whey to December 2027, unlimited from January 2028),
  but a forecast **stronger Australian dollar** raises prices for foreign
  buyers
- Global competition intensifying as Argentina and New Zealand ramp up
  production
- **Employment falls to 5,733** (−5.3% a year) while revenue is flat —
  revenue per employee rising +5.7% a year, which is the automation story
  from Topic 6 showing up in the forecast

> **Flat real revenue with employment falling 5.3% a year is the whole
> sector strategy in two numbers.** The forecast has the industry producing
> the same real output with roughly a quarter fewer people by 2029–30. That
> is not growth; it is a productivity response to a margin problem. If you
> need one sentence on where the sector is going, it is that.

## Summary

- **$8,520.4m revenue, $357.9m profit, 4.2% margin, 134 businesses, 247
  establishments, 7,517 employees, $687.8m wages** in 2024–25, all in
  2025–26 dollars.
- Products: **yoghurt and cultured 47.9%**, proteins 20.2%, other dairy
  18.6%, butter 13.3%. The At a Glance table omits yoghurt.
- Markets: **supermarkets 50.2%**, other food manufacturers 25.6%, other
  retail 12.3%, wholesalers 11.9%.
- **CR4 = 51.9%**: Bega 16.2%, Saputo 16.1%, Lactalis 10.1%, Fonterra 9.5%.
- **Purchases are 72.3% of revenue** against a 58% sector norm; value added
  is only 14.8% of revenue. That structure explains everything else.
- Trade: exports $543.5m, imports $533.3m, **surplus $10.2m**. NZ supplies
  just under three-quarters of imports.
- Life cycle: **decline**. Concentration rising because small players exit.
- Three source errors flagged: the **At a Glance products table omits
  yoghurt**; **establishments sum to 249 against the table's 247**; and
  QLD+WA establishments are **20.7%, described as "just below one-fifth"**.
  NSW population is given as both 31.3% and 31.2%.
