## Part A — Definitions and frameworks

<details><summary>A1. State the unit's definition of the business environment and identify the three words that do the work.</summary>

**"An external environment that firms cannot necessarily influence or
control but need to understand to be able to adapt to."**

- **External** — outside the firm. Internal costs, pricing and management
  are out of scope.
- **Cannot necessarily influence or control** — it is an environment, not a
  strategy. The firm adapts; it does not set it.
- **Understand to be able to adapt** — the purpose is adaptation, not
  prediction and not recommendation.

</details>

<details><summary>A2. A student's A1 report contains a section headed "Political" that analyses the RBA's inflation target, the Dairy Code of Conduct and Australia's free trade agreements. Assess it.</summary>

All three are misplaced, and each in a different way.

| Content | Belongs in | Why |
|---|---|---|
| The RBA's inflation target | **A2 section 1** | Macroeconomic *policy*, not an institution. Naming the RBA as a regulator is A1; describing what monetary policy does is A2 |
| The Dairy Code of Conduct | **A2 section 2** | Regulation, not a regulatory institution. Naming the ACCC is A1; analysing the Code is A2 |
| Free trade agreements | **A2 section 3** | International trade — explicitly excluded from A1 |

The task sheet: "You will not receive any acknowledgement for raising these
topics in Assessment 1." The section earns nothing, and the marks that
should have gone to institutions, peak bodies and property rights are lost
as well.

</details>

<details><summary>A3. Name the five source-evaluation criteria and apply them to a 2019 industry association submission arguing against a proposed levy.</summary>

**Authority, Currency, Purpose, Objectivity, Reliability.**

| Criterion | Assessment |
|---|---|
| Authority | Reasonable — an industry body has standing to speak about its sector |
| Currency | Weak for describing the current environment; fine as evidence of what the sector argued in 2019 |
| **Purpose** | **Fails as neutral evidence** — the document exists to influence a decision |
| **Objectivity** | **Fails** — it is advocacy by construction |
| Reliability | Depends on whether its underlying claims are traceable |

**Verdict:** usable as evidence of *the sector's position* and of
rent-seeking behaviour in the political marketplace, cited as such. Not
usable as neutral evidence about the levy's effects.

</details>

<details><summary>A4. Distinguish a peak body from a professional association, and classify Dairy Australia.</summary>

A **peak body**'s membership predominantly consists of **other
organisations** of allied interests, and it is recognised by other peaks
and its sector as representative of the whole sector. Its primary aim is
advocacy and representation to government.

A **professional association**'s members are **individuals** working in a
field — DIAA is the example in this sector.

**Dairy Australia is neither.** It is an **industry-owned national service
organisation**, funded by farmer levies with matching Commonwealth R&D
funds. Its function is research, development and services, not advocacy.
The peak bodies for this sector are **ADIC** and **ADPF**.

</details>

## Part B — Macroeconomic indicators

<details><summary>B1. A sector has revenue of $8,520.4m and industry value added of $1,261.0m. What is its contribution to GDP, as a figure and as a share of revenue? Why are they different?</summary>

```
contribution to GDP = industry value added = $1,261.0m
1,261.0 ÷ 8,520.4 = 0.14800 → 14.8% of revenue
```

They differ because GDP counts only **final** goods and services. Revenue
includes **intermediate inputs** — principally raw milk, which is already
counted as output of the dairy cattle farming industry. Counting the full
$8,520.4m would double-count the milk.

</details>

<details><summary>B2. Over one year: unemployment rate 4.4% → 4.1%; participation rate 67.0% → 66.4%; employment 14,678,400 → 14,610,000. Has the labour market improved?</summary>

**No — this is a deteriorating labour market with an improving headline
rate.**

- Employment **fell** by 68,400 people.
- Participation **fell** 0.6 percentage points, so people left the labour
  force.
- The unemployment rate fell because the **denominator** (the labour force)
  shrank faster than the numerator.

This is the discouraged-worker signature. It is exactly why the ABS
publishes the participation rate and the employment-to-population ratio
alongside the unemployment rate, and why quoting the unemployment rate
alone is not analysis.

</details>

<details><summary>B3. Headline CPI is 7.8% year-ended and the trimmed mean is 6.8%. Next year headline is 4.1% and the trimmed mean is 4.2%. What has happened?</summary>

Year 1: headline **above** trimmed mean by 1.0 pp. Volatile components in
the tails were adding to the headline — the trimmed mean strips out the
extremes by keeping the middle 70% of price changes by expenditure weight.

Year 2: the trimmed mean is **above** the headline by 0.1 pp. The volatile
components have reversed and are now subtracting from the headline, while
**underlying inflation is more persistent than the headline suggests**.

The lesson: disinflation shows up in the headline first and in the
underlying measure later. A policymaker watching only the headline would
conclude the job is closer to done than the trimmed mean says. (Both
figures are from the RBA's G1 series for the December quarters of 2022 and
2023.)

</details>

<details><summary>B4. Unemployment is 4.4%; the ABS estimates NAIRU at around 5% and Treasury at 4.5–5%. Write one sentence for A1 and one that would be A2.</summary>

**A1 (indicators):** *"At 4.4 per cent, the unemployment rate sits below
both the ABS estimate of the non-accelerating inflation rate of
unemployment (around 5 per cent) and the Treasury working paper range of
4.5 to 5 per cent, indicating a labour market tighter than the estimated
non-inflationary level — consistent with the above-target inflation
reported over the same period."*

**A2 (policy — do not write this in A1):** *"With unemployment below NAIRU
and inflation above the 2–3 per cent target band, the RBA's contractionary
stance is consistent with its price stability objective."*

The first describes the indicators. The second prescribes or evaluates
policy.

</details>

## Part C — Data and statistics

<details><summary>C1. Sixteen countries. Poverty rates in 2010: 0.089, 0.065, 0.072, 0.141, 0.073, 0.075, 0.107, 0.109, 0.080, 0.139, 0.110, 0.091, 0.133, 0.127, 0.150, 0.167. Compute mean, median, sample SD, population SD and range. Then say what the mean and median together tell you.</summary>

```
mean            = 1.728 ÷ 16       = 0.10800
median          = (0.107 + 0.109)/2 = 0.10800
sample SD (n−1)                     = 0.03181
population SD (n)                   = 0.03080
range           = 0.167 − 0.065     = 0.10200
```

**Mean = median exactly.** The distribution is symmetric on those two
measures — no skew. Reported to two decimals as Table 3 does, both become
0.11 and the finding disappears.

Compare 2018, where mean = 0.11800 and median = 0.11050: the mean has moved
**above** the median, so the distribution has become **right-skewed**, with
a few countries (chiefly Hungary, 0.073 → 0.179) pulling away at the top.

</details>

<details><summary>C2. Using those 2010 figures, evaluate the claim "95% of all poverty rates fell roughly within the range of 0.11 plus or minus 0.06".</summary>

```
2 × sample SD = 2 × 0.03181 = 0.0636 → rounds to the stated 0.06  ✓ arithmetic checks

interval = 0.108 ± 0.0636 = 0.0444 to 0.1716
data     = minimum 0.065, maximum 0.167
```

The interval **strictly contains the entire range of the data**, so
**100% of observations fall inside it, not 95%**.

The ±2SD rule assumes a normal distribution. With n = 16 bounded
observations that assumption does not hold, and you can prove it fails from
the summary table alone by comparing the interval bounds to the stated
minimum and maximum. Use ±2SD as a rough dispersion indicator if the unit
asks for it, but do not attach "95%" to it without checking.

</details>

<details><summary>C3. A table reports mean $15,383.56, SD $6,694.12, range $23,742.11, minimum $2,510.34, maximum $26,252.45, n = 31. Run two internal consistency checks and one plausibility check.</summary>

**Consistency check 1 — does the range reconcile?**

```
26,252.45 − 2,510.34 = 23,742.11   ✓ exact
```

**Consistency check 2 — is the mean plausible given the bounds?**

```
midrange = (2,510.34 + 26,252.45) ÷ 2 = 14,381.40
mean                                  = 15,383.56
```

The mean sits above the midrange, so the data is bunched toward the upper
end with a longer left tail. Plausible, and informative.

**Plausibility check — the ±2SD interval:**

```
15,383.56 ± 13,388.24 = 1,995.32 to 28,771.80
```

Both bounds fall **outside** the observed minimum and maximum, so once
again 100% of observations lie inside. Do not claim 95%.

</details>

<details><summary>C4. Correlating the RBA cash rate against ABS Original month-on-month household spending gives r = −0.02. Against year-ended growth lagged 12 months, excluding COVID, it gives r = −0.53. Explain both, and state what the second one means.</summary>

**r = −0.02** because the Original series is **not seasonally adjusted**,
and the calendar dominates it. Mean month-on-month change is **+7.9% in
December** and **−15.1% in January**, every year, regardless of the cash
rate. The seasonal swing buries the policy signal.

**r = −0.53** because two corrections have been made. First, year-ended
change compares like month with like month, removing seasonality. Second, a
12-month lag matches the **monetary policy transmission lag** — rate
changes reach household spending over six to twelve months. Excluding the
COVID window removes 22 months in which spending swung between −23.4% and
+32.8% for reasons unrelated to the cash rate.

**What it means:** a moderate negative relationship, with

```
r² = 0.53² = 0.281 → about 28% of the variation shared
```

so 72% is unexplained, and the direction of causation is *supported by
theory rather than established by the correlation*. It is a much better
estimate than −0.02, and it is still only an association.

</details>

<details><summary>C5. Two spending series over the same 121 months: discretionary mean +5.05%, SD 9.61; non-discretionary mean +4.81%, SD 4.86. What is the finding, and how does it connect to the focus sector?</summary>

**Nearly identical averages, radically different dispersion.** The SD ratio
is 9.61 ÷ 4.86 = **1.98** — discretionary spending is about twice as
volatile for the same expected growth.

That is the unit's "be careful relying on averages" lesson with numbers. It
also measures the cyclical/counter-cyclical distinction: at a 12-month lag
discretionary spending correlates −0.55 with the cash rate against
non-discretionary's −0.41.

**For the focus sector:** its product portfolio spans both. Basic and
private-label butter behaves defensively — consumers trade *down into* it.
Dairy protein supplements, flavoured milk and dairy desserts are
discretionary and will swing roughly twice as hard through the cycle. A
report that calls the sector simply "cyclical" or simply "defensive" is
wrong either way.

</details>

## Part D — Policy

<details><summary>D1. Classify each as fiscal policy or not: (i) the RBA cuts the cash rate; (ii) the Queensland government funds a new hospital; (iii) the Commonwealth cuts income tax to stimulate demand; (iv) the Commonwealth increases defence spending to counter terrorism; (v) unemployment benefits rise automatically in a downturn.</summary>

| | Classification |
|---|---|
| (i) RBA cuts the cash rate | **Monetary** policy, not fiscal |
| (ii) Queensland funds a hospital | **Not fiscal policy** — state spending is excluded by definition, as it is not intended to affect the national economy |
| (iii) Commonwealth cuts income tax to stimulate demand | **Fiscal policy** — federal, and intended to move national aggregate demand. Expansionary and discretionary |
| (iv) Commonwealth increases defence spending to counter terrorism | **Not fiscal policy** — federal spending, but it is a *defence policy* action. The test is intent |
| (v) Unemployment benefits rise automatically | **Fiscal policy — an automatic stabiliser.** Counter-cyclical, and requires no decision |

</details>

<details><summary>D2. The cash rate goes from 0.10% to 4.35%. State the change three ways and say which to use.</summary>

```
percentage points:  4.35 − 0.10           = +4.25 pp
relative:           (4.35 ÷ 0.10) − 1     = +4,250%
multiple:           4.35 ÷ 0.10           = 43.5×
```

**Use percentage points.** The relative and multiple figures are
arithmetically correct and analytically useless — they are artefacts of a
near-zero base, and "+4,250%" reads as hyperbole. Interest rate changes are
conventionally expressed in percentage points or basis points (425 basis
points).

</details>

<details><summary>D3. Why can the RBA's F1.1 series show 1.77% for May 2016 when the RBA never set that rate?</summary>

F1.1 is the **monthly average of the daily cash rate target**, not the
decision-day target. The RBA cut from 2.00% to 1.75% effective 4 May 2016,
so the month contains both rates:

```
(3 × 2.00 + 28 × 1.75) ÷ 31 = 1.774 → 1.77   ✓
```

Two confirming cases:

```
Aug 2016, cut 1.75 → 1.50 effective 3 Aug:
(2 × 1.75 + 29 × 1.50) ÷ 31 = 1.516 → 1.52   ✓

May 2025, cut 4.10 → 3.85 effective 21 May:
(20 × 4.10 + 11 × 3.85) ÷ 31 = 4.011 → 4.01  ✓
```

If you need decisions, use the RBA's cash rate decisions page. If you plot
F1.1, say it is a monthly average.

</details>

<details><summary>D4. Government pays an electricity rebate. Headline CPI falls 0.4 percentage points. Has inflationary pressure fallen?</summary>

**Not established.** The rebate lowers the electricity component of the CPI
basket, so the headline index falls **mechanically** in the quarter it
applies. That is a change in a measured price, not in underlying demand
pressure.

The test is the **trimmed mean**, which strips out extreme component
movements by keeping the middle 70% of price changes by expenditure weight.
If headline falls and the trimmed mean does not, the policy moved the index
rather than the economy.

There is a further wrinkle worth a mark: the rebate raises household
disposable income, which is expansionary. A measure can reduce measured
inflation and increase inflationary pressure at the same time.

</details>

<details><summary>D5. A regional water resource is being overused. Identify the market failure, the property that causes it, and two policy responses.</summary>

**Market failure: common good.** Everyone has access, but use by one person
reduces the ability of someone else to use it.

**The causing property: non-excludability combined with rivalry.**
Non-excludability means non-payers cannot be kept out; rivalry means each
user's consumption depletes the resource for others. Together they produce
congestion and overuse — the tragedy of the commons.

**Two responses:**

1. **Assign property rights** — water entitlements, as in the
   Murray–Darling Basin, creating a market that allocates water to its
   highest-value use.
2. **Quotas or regulation restricting access** — direct limits on
   extraction.

**The equity caveat:** assigning property rights fixes efficiency, not
fairness. In the Murray–Darling, the Traditional Owners were told in earlier
entitlement rounds that no water remained for their needs, and were only
recently allocated entitlements for economic and cultural purposes. A market
can allocate efficiently forever from an unjust initial endowment.

</details>

<details><summary>D6. A market is a natural monopoly. Why is "increase competition" the wrong answer?</summary>

A **natural monopoly** exists where economies of scale are so large that
**one firm can supply the entire market at a lower average cost than two or
more firms** — distribution networks and electricity markets are the
lecture's examples.

Forcing entry means **duplicating the network**, which raises total industry
cost and therefore prices. Competition destroys the efficiency that made
the monopoly natural.

The correct response is **price and access regulation**: the ACCC setting
prices at a fair level, and the National Competition Council recommending
on third-party access to services provided by monopoly infrastructure.

</details>

<details><summary>D7. The Indigenous Procurement Policy exceeded targets of 3% of contract number and 1.25% of contract value, with 943 businesses securing 10,920 contracts in 2020-21. Evaluate.</summary>

```
10,920 ÷ 943 = 11.6 contracts per participating business
```

**The targets were met.** Both the volume target (3% by number) and the
value target (1.25% by value) were exceeded.

**The objective may not have been.** Four critiques from the source:

1. Most contracts have been **low value**, across only a **narrow range of
   industries**.
2. The **social value** created is being miscalculated.
3. **Policy mutation** — the objective drifted from stimulating Indigenous
   *entrepreneurship* to Indigenous *job creation*, shifting responsibility
   for Indigenous unemployment away from government and corporate Australia
   and onto Indigenous business.
4. **Black cladding** — non-Indigenous businesses structuring themselves to
   appear Indigenous-owned to access the policy.

**The analytical point:** the value target was set at 1.25% against a
volume target of 3%, so the policy's own design permitted a large number of
small contracts. Hitting a target set on the wrong measure is not achieving
the objective.

</details>

## Part E — Trade, geopolitics and the sector

<details><summary>E1. The sector exported $543.5m and imported $533.3m. Compute the balance, express the position two ways, and say which is honest.</summary>

```
balance      = 543.5 − 533.3 = $10.2m surplus
two-way trade = 543.5 + 533.3 = $1,076.8m
exports as a share            = 543.5 ÷ 1,076.8 = 50.5%
surplus as a share            = 10.2 ÷ 1,076.8  = 0.9%
```

**Version 1 (technically true, misleading):** "The sector is a net
exporter."

**Version 2 (honest):** "The sector was marginally net exporting in
2024–25, with a $10.2 million surplus on $1.08 billion of two-way trade —
0.9 per cent of the total. A two per cent movement in either flow would
reverse the balance."

</details>

<details><summary>E2. The US imposed a 10% baseline tariff on Australian goods in April 2025. Why might IBISWorld describe this as creating opportunities for Australian manufacturers?</summary>

Because the tariff was **reciprocal** — applying to US goods exported to
Australia as well — and the sector's exposure to the US is **asymmetric**.

- The US is **not** a top-four export destination (those are China,
  Singapore, New Zealand and Malaysia).
- The US **is** the second-largest import source, and supplied **66.1% of
  Australia's whey import value** in 2023–24.

So the tariff raises the delivered price of US product in Australia, while
the sector's own US export exposure is small. Combined with a relatively
strong US dollar, this discourages Australian purchases of US dairy inputs
and creates room for domestic producers. A trade barrier can protect you
when you are a bigger buyer than seller.

</details>

<details><summary>E3. New Zealand supplies just under three-quarters of the sector's imports. Why, and what does it rule out as a response?</summary>

**Why:** the **Closer Economic Relations** agreement (ANZCERTA) provides
**tariff-free dairy trade** with New Zealand, combined with geographic
proximity and New Zealand's production efficiency. Australia's own shrinking
milk pool created the demand gap that NZ butter filled.

**What it rules out:** tariff protection. The access is locked in by treaty,
so the sector cannot be shielded by a duty. The available responses are
cost competitiveness, product differentiation and value-adding — which is
exactly what IBISWorld observes: manufacturers reallocating scarce milk to
higher-return products and pursuing value-added lines.

</details>

<details><summary>E4. Purchases are 72.3% of revenue; margin is 4.2%. Farmgate milk prices rise 25% and half of purchases are milk. What happens?</summary>

```
milk = 50% of purchases = 0.50 × 72.3% = 36.15% of revenue
a 25% rise costs        = 0.25 × 36.15 = 9.04 percentage points of revenue
margin before                          = +4.2%
margin after, no pass-through          = 4.2 − 9.04 = −4.8%
```

The sector swings from a **4.2% profit to a 4.8% loss**.

**Why it cannot simply be passed on:** supermarkets and grocery stores are
**50.2% of revenue**, buyer power is moderate and increasing, and consumers
under cost-of-living pressure are shifting to low-cost private label.
IBISWorld records exactly this — manufacturers "struggled to pass these
higher costs on to consumers because of competitive market pressures and
consumer price sensitivity, cutting into revenue."

**What actually happened:** manufacturers reallocated scarce milk to
higher-return products, consolidated factories, and small players exited.
Beston Global Food Company collapsed in September 2024.

</details>

<details><summary>E5. Farmgate milk prices "skyrocketed by more than 25.0% in 2022-23 as output dipped 6.0%". What does this reveal?</summary>

A **6% supply contraction produced a price rise of more than 25%** — a
price response more than four times the size of the quantity change. That
implies demand for raw milk is **highly price-inelastic in the short run**:
processors cannot substitute away from their core input, and cannot reduce
usage much without reducing output.

**Why it matters:** it converts a farm-level climate shock into a
factory-level margin shock. With purchases at 72.3% of revenue and a 4.2%
margin, there is no buffer. It is the mechanism connecting the geopolitical
and climate material in section 4 to the sector's financial position.

</details>

<details><summary>E6. The Australian Dairy Sustainability Framework targets a 30% reduction by 2030, and 94% of dairy farms have implemented at least one emissions-reduction practice. What are the two precision problems?</summary>

**1. The target is emissions *intensity*, not absolute emissions.**
Intensity is emissions per unit of output. A 30% intensity reduction is
fully compatible with **rising total emissions** if production grows more
than 30%. Dropping the word converts an efficiency target into an absolute
one, which is a substantively different and stronger claim. The source says
intensity.

**2. "At least one practice" is an adoption count, not an outcome.** It
counts a farm that adjusted its feed mix identically to one that installed
full effluent capture. It is evidence of **engagement**, not of emissions
reduction, and it says nothing about magnitude. Report it as an adoption
statistic and say so.

</details>

## Part F — Integrated

<details><summary>F1. Explain how the ageing population, macroeconomic policy and the focus sector's cost structure connect. Use figures.</summary>

**Ageing (Topic 5).** The 65+ population rises from 2.5 million (2002) to a
projected 6.2 million (2042), from around 13% to around 25% of the
population. Working-age Australians per person aged 65+ fall from 7.4 in the
mid-1970s to 4.4 in 2015 and a projected 3.2 in 2055. Fiscally, ageing is
projected to **subtract 0.4%** from annual real growth in revenue and **add
0.3%** to annual real growth in spending.

**Fiscal policy (Topics 7 and 8).** That 0.7 percentage point annual gap is
structural, not cyclical, so it cannot be closed by the automatic
stabilisers. It shows up in the expenditure mix: social security and welfare
at 37.0% (up from 36.3%) and health at 15.9% (up from 15.3%), while
education fell to 6.9% (from 7.2%).

**Labour market (Topics 5 and 8).** A shrinking working-age share means
labour supply becomes the binding constraint, which is why participation
policies — subsidised childcare, parental leave, skills incentives for
40–70 year olds — are labour market policy in disguise, and why technology
that releases labour is framed as a positive rather than a threat
(Topic 6).

**The sector (Topic 10).** Wages are 8.1% of revenue against a sector norm
of 13.53%, and the average wage of $91,499 is above the sector average.
Employment is forecast to fall from 7,517 to 5,733 by 2029–30 (−5.3% a
year) while real revenue is flat. That is a labour-constrained,
automation-led productivity response — the ageing population arriving in a
single industry's cost structure.

</details>

<details><summary>F3. Under the same rules, what is the single most defensible criticism a marker could make of a report that scores well on content?</summary>

That it **describes rather than analyses**.

The Canvas rubric guidance names it directly: the assessment requires
"critical and objective analysis — it's **not simply a description** of the
relevant institutions."

The practical test on any paragraph: does it contain a **because**, a
**therefore**, or a **however** that connects a fact to a consequence for
the business environment?

| Description | Analysis |
|---|---|
| "The ACCC enforces the Competition and Consumer Act 2010." | "Because supermarkets account for 50.2% of the sector's revenue and buyer power is rated moderate and increasing, ACCC oversight of the retail–supplier relationship is more material to this sector than to most manufacturing." |
| "Inflation was 3.8% in the year to October 2025." | "Inflation of 3.8% sits above the RBA's 2–3% target, and with purchases already 72.3% of sector revenue, continued input-price growth compresses a margin that is only 4.2%." |

The second column also demonstrates the assessment triangle — big picture,
then sector-specific consequence.

</details>
