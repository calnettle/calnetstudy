# TOPIC 8 — Targeted Policy and Regulation

Weeks 7 and 8. Why government interferes in production and consumption
decisions at all, the taxonomy of market failure, the toolkit for fixing
it, how policy becomes regulation, how regulation is kept honest, and the
four policy areas the unit focuses on.

This is **A2 section 2**: an overview of the Australian targeted policy and
regulatory environment, plus analysis of **one** policy or regulation
directed at the focus sector.

## 8.1 The efficiency case, and where it breaks

The starting assumption is that markets, through prices, allocate resources
to their most valuable uses and eliminate waste — that is, they achieve
**economic efficiency**. Two components:

| | Definition |
|---|---|
| **Productive efficiency** | How goods are produced — at the lowest possible cost |
| **Allocative efficiency** | The particular mix of goods a society produces represents the combination that society most desires |

Where **market failure** is present, the outcome is sub-optimal, and
government intervention has a case.

> **Government intervention is justified by market failure, not by the
> problem being important.** That is the analytical discipline this topic
> teaches. "Obesity is bad" is not an argument for a sugar tax; "sugary
> drink consumption generates negative externalities borne by the health
> system" is. Identify the *failure* first, then the *tool*.

## 8.2 The market failure taxonomy

Learn these definitions verbatim — the tutorial's matching exercise uses
exactly this wording.

| Failure | Definition | Real-world example |
|---|---|---|
| **Externalities** | Markets may "encourage" activities by firms or individuals that impact **negatively upon those who do not partake** in those activities | Passive smoking |
| **Information asymmetry** | **One party in a transaction has better information** than the other party involved | Food labelling |
| **Public goods** | The business sector cannot produce certain goods because there is **no way to exclude non-payers** from receiving them — **non-rivalrous and non-excludable** | National military defence |
| **Common goods** | Everyone has access, but **use by one person reduces the ability of someone else to use it** — **non-excludable but rivalrous** | Public parks |
| **Merit goods** | Goods that would be **under-consumed** if provided by the business sector; they realise positive externalities and are subsidised or free at the point of use so consumption does not depend primarily on ability to pay | University education |
| **Uncompetitive markets** | Results in **profits for firms at the expense of the consumer** | Coles/Woolworths duopoly |

The lecture adds two more sources of sub-optimal outcomes:

- **Inequitable allocation** of goods and services — equality-of-access
  issues, whether income-related or spatial
- **Socially undesirable activities** — e.g. discrimination

> **Public goods and common goods differ on exactly one property, and it is
> the one students swap.** Both are **non-excludable** — you cannot keep
> non-payers out. The discriminator is **rivalry**:
>
> ```
> public good  = non-excludable + NON-rivalrous  (my use costs you nothing)
> common good  = non-excludable + RIVALROUS      (my use costs you something)
> ```
>
> Defence is non-rivalrous: protecting one more citizen does not reduce
> anyone else's protection. A national park is rivalrous: every extra
> visitor degrades the experience and the ground. That is why common goods
> get congested and overused — the tragedy of the commons — and why the
> typical policy response is **regulation to restrict access**.

|---|---|---|---|

| Information asymmetry | 6 | Food labelling | Food labelling → **regulatory standards** |
| Public goods | 2 | National military defence | — |

### Two case studies from Drilling Down 7

**Property rights: Murray–Darling water entitlements.** Water entitlements
are the foundation of the Australian water market and are the mechanism by
which water is allocated efficiently across competing users. But the
Drilling Down pack immediately qualifies the success story: the
**Traditional Owners of the Murray–Darling Basin have only recently been
allocated water entitlements** for economic and cultural purposes. In
previous entitlement rounds they were told there was no water left for
their needs.

> **"Assigning property rights solves the market failure" and "the initial
> allocation was inequitable" are both true, and the second does not
> disappear because of the first.** Efficiency says nothing about who gets
> the initial endowment. A market can allocate a resource efficiently from
> an unjust starting point forever. That distinction — efficiency versus
> equity — is worth a sentence in A2, and it links straight back to the
> property rights and ICIP material in A1's P.

**Common goods: freedom camping in New Zealand.** Tourists exploit the
**non-excludability** of freedom camping; congestion and overuse follow;
the proposed policy response is **regulation restricting access**. It is the
textbook common-good arc in one case study.

## 8.3 The policy toolkit

The lecture presents the same list twice, once as "tools used to address
market failures" and once as "tools used to correct markets". The union:

| Tool | Examples given |
|---|---|
| **Assigning property rights** | Murray–Darling Basin water entitlements |
| **Laws, regulations, standards, licences and permits** | Food labelling; minimum age for alcohol and gambling; aged and early childhood care; HGV licences; gambling licences |
| **Quotas** | Limit and control fishing; limit CO₂ emissions |
| **Nudges** | Organ donation with an opt-out default; improving tax compliance |
| **Price controls** | Minimum wage |
| **Public sector provision** | Health, education, defence |
| **Social marketing campaigns** | Anti-smoking; promoting exercise and healthy eating |
| **Subsidies** | Part-funding private sector investment into new technology (e.g. the Australian 5G innovation initiative) |
| **Tax** | A change in price (increase) to discourage activity — e.g. a tax to target obesity |

> **The lecture shouts one warning in capitals and it is worth heeding:
> "Tax — a change in price (increase) to discourage activity. DO NOT
> confuse with income tax."** A corrective tax here is a *price
> instrument*, aimed at changing behaviour by changing relative prices.
> Income tax is a *revenue instrument* and, when used counter-cyclically,
> a fiscal policy tool from Topic 7. Same word, three different roles, and
> a marker will notice.

## 8.4 From policy to regulation

The chain:

```
POLICY
  ↓ can only be enacted if underpinned by legislation
LEGISLATION — provides the legislative authority to undertake policy
  ↓ articulated into policy strategy statements by the relevant
    government department or dedicated agency
STRATEGY
  ↓ implemented through policy instruments/tools
REGULATION — enforcement, information campaigns, etc.
```

The worked example the lecture uses:

| Step | Competition policy |
|---|---|
| Policy objective | Ensuring competitive markets |
| Legislation | **Competition and Consumer Act 2010** |
| Agency and strategy | The **ACCC**'s role is to enforce the Act by promoting competition, fair trading and regulating national infrastructure for the benefit of all Australians |
| Regulation | Enforcement of regulations, information campaigns |

> **"Policy can only be enacted if it is underpinned by legislation" is the
> sentence that explains why regulating AI is hard.** Drilling Down 7 makes
> the link: the challenge for emerging sectors — Uber, the metaverse, AI —
> is "establishing legislative authority where it does not exist". There is
> no regulator to act until a parliament has passed something for it to
> enforce. That is the structural reason for the lag, not slowness.

## 8.5 Getting regulatory policy right

The lecture frames it as a Goldilocks problem — "red tape or visible hand?"

| Too little | Too much |
|---|---|
| Frameworks too limited, poorly conceived, redundant or incoherent | Frameworks overly complicated, lacking transparency, inefficiently or improperly enforced |
| Difficult to start a business, trade abroad, or comply with basic administrative procedures | Losses in organisational performance |

At worst, poor regulatory policy can lead to **corrupt behaviour**.

### Regulatory Impact Statements

The role of a **RIS**:

- Improving the **evidence base** for regulation
- **Critically examining** the impacts and consequences of a range of
  alternative options
- Promoting **policy coherence** by clearly illustrating the inherent
  trade-offs within regulatory proposals
- **Reducing regulatory failures**

Drilling Down 7 Task 6 sends you to the Office of Impact Analysis's
*Australian Government Guide to Policy Impact Analysis* for the **six
Principles for Australian Government Policy Makers** (page 6) and the
**seven RIS questions** (pages 9–11). Tutorial 7 then makes you pick a
published impact analysis — Enhanced Paid Parental Leave for Families, or
Drone Noise Reforms — and test whether it actually addressed the seven
questions, reporting the policy problem and the best option considered.

> **"A range of alternative options" is the part that gets skipped, and it
> is the part that matters.** A RIS that considers only "do this
> regulation" versus "do nothing" has not done impact analysis. The reason
> the requirement exists is that the first regulation anyone thinks of is
> rarely the least-cost one. When you evaluate a published impact analysis
> in A2, count the options — that single check tells you most of what you
> need to know about its quality.

Tutorial 7 also has you benchmark Australia internationally using the OECD
data explorer's **Stakeholder engagement, RIA, and Ex post evaluation
indexes** against Germany, the UK, Japan, Canada and New Zealand. The
tutorial notes the USA has only subordinate regulations in the dataset and
so is excluded — a data-consistency point of exactly the kind Topic 4
teaches. And it repeats the presentation rule: **do not cut and paste** —
download the table to Excel and build a stacked column chart yourself.

### Regulatory policy and technology disrupters

| The case for **less** regulation | The case for **more** regulation |
|---|---|
| Economic rewards, higher productivity growth | **Monopoly power** — the mega-corporations |
| Less information asymmetry | **Worker exploitation** and precarious employment contracts |
| | **Privacy and data issues** — surveillance capitalism |
| | **Ethical issues** — genetic technologies, artificial intelligence |

### Challenges to the policy and regulatory environment

- Promoting adoption of innovative technologies while managing or
  mitigating the risks — **regulating without stifling innovation**
- **Blurring of lines between regulatory domains** — consumers and
  producers; the shared and gig economies
- **Establishing legislative authority where it does not exist** — emerging
  sectors (Uber, the metaverse), AI
- **Managing the relevance of proposed policy change** — "does it tackle
  the issue, or has it been hijacked?"

### Can AI be regulated?

The Productivity Commission's position, as the unit presents it: in
general, **Australian law applies to the use of AI as it would to other
technologies**, and new AI-specific laws must be introduced carefully to
avoid being unnecessary and confusing. Two worked cases from Tutorial 7:

| Situation | Verdict |
|---|---|
| AI used to create inaudible frequencies encouraging truck drivers to keep driving excessively | **Existing regulation may work.** The National Heavy Vehicle Regulator (2020) already stipulates maximum permissible driving hours, making this use illegal |
| AI able to re-identify users from robustly de-identified data | **Existing regulation may need updating.** Laws governing personal information are still sound; they need to reflect how de-identified and re-identified data is used — e.g. by redefining personal information to include some forms of re-identified data |

> **"Do we need a new law?" is a genuine three-way question, and the
> default answer is no.** Existing law may already cover it; existing law
> may need amendment; only rarely is new legislation required. A2 answers
> that jump straight to "AI needs to be regulated" have skipped the
> analysis. The disciplined version identifies the harm, finds the existing
> instrument, and says specifically what it fails to reach.

## 8.6 Four policy areas

The lecture selects four, noting that others (trade, environmental,
Indigenous affairs, education and skills) are covered elsewhere and that
each policy area has a direct or indirect link to economic growth as the
key objective — with "growth" treated inclusively, covering social
wellbeing and equality.

### (i) Government expenditure

Largest components of Commonwealth expenditure by function, with the prior
year in brackets:

| Function | Share | Movement |
|---|---|---|
| **Social security and welfare** | **37.0%** | up from 36.3% |
| Health | 15.9% | up from 15.3% |
| Education | 6.9% | **down** from 7.2% |
| Defence | 6.6% | up from 6.5% |

```
these four = 37.0 + 15.9 + 6.9 + 6.6 = 66.4% of expenditure
```

Social security and welfare is redistribution of income to align with broad
societal objectives; the biggest items are the **Age Pension, aged care and
the NDIS**. Health, education and defence are the **public and merit
goods**.

> **Education is the only one of the four that fell, and it fell while
> health rose — that is the ageing population showing up in the budget.**
> Topic 5 gave the projection (ageing adds 0.3% to annual real growth in
> spending); this is it arriving. In A2, connect the two rather than
> reporting the percentages flat.

**Government procurement** — the process by which government acquires the
goods and services it needs by purchasing from commercial businesses.

| Method | Description |
|---|---|
| **Open** | Open to any business that can satisfy the requirements |
| **Limited** | Direct approach to a supplier of choice; only in very specific circumstances — extreme urgency, or where only one supplier can provide (e.g. a commissioned work of art) |
| **Selective tenders** | Open only to potential suppliers invited to submit a response |
| **Lists of preferred suppliers** | Suppliers who have met the necessary conditions to supply |

The policy angles: involving non-government actors in achieving policy
goals (public-private partnerships); **economic** goals — development of
small business, entrepreneurship, innovation; **social** goals — generating
employment, targeting ethically, environmentally and socially responsible
suppliers, and addressing institutional discrimination. Target: **20% of
procurement by value from SMEs**.

**Government physical capital expenditure.** Infrastructure projects are
usually too expensive for the private sector alone. Government capital
expenditure is **not subject to market discipline** — there are political as
well as economic incentives, and government does not go out of business if
a project returns a loss.

**Public Private Partnership example — Cross River Rail:**

```
total project        $6.9 billion
Queensland Govt      $5.4 billion
private sector       $1.5 billion
                     ─────────────
5.4 + 1.5 = 6.9  ✓ reconciles
private share = 1.5 ÷ 6.9 = 21.7%
```

**Crowding out** — a process where an increase in government expenditure
leads to a fall in private sector spending.

**Indigenous Procurement Policy.** The objective is to stimulate Indigenous
entrepreneurship, business and economic development, and to address
institutional discrimination in government contracting practice. The
evidence Tutorial 8 presents is deliberately two-sided:

| For | Against |
|---|---|
| **943 Indigenous businesses** secured more than **10,920 Commonwealth contracts** in 2020–21 | Most contracts have been **low value** with only a narrow range of industries |
| Exceeded 2020–21 targets of **3% of the number** and **1.25% of the value** of accessible contracts | The **social value** created is being miscalculated |
| | **Policy mutation** shifted responsibility for Indigenous unemployment away from government and corporate Australia onto Indigenous business |
| | **Black cladding** — non-Indigenous businesses structuring to appear Indigenous-owned to access the policy |

```
10,920 ÷ 943 = 11.6 contracts per business
```

> **The volume target was exceeded and the value target was much lower —
> and those two facts belong together.** The targets themselves were 3% by
> *number* and only 1.25% by *value*, and the critique is that contracts
> have been low-value. A policy can hit its stated targets and still fail
> its stated objective if the targets are set on the wrong measure. That is
> a strong, evidence-backed evaluative paragraph for A2 section 2.

### (ii) Public ownership: Government Business Enterprises

**Objective:** provide services necessary to society, ensuring equality of
access and provision, which may not be attractive to private businesses.

**Three key characteristics:**

1. Government **controls** the enterprise
2. The enterprise is **principally engaged in commercial activities** — it
   may have additional non-financial performance objectives
3. The enterprise has a **legal personality separate from government**, as
   specified under the Public Governance, Performance and Accountability
   legislation

**Worked example — NBN Co.** A GBE built to deliver the infrastructure
underpinning Australia's position in the global digital economy. What it
delivers: **Fibre to the Node (FTTN)**. What competitors in the global
digital economy are likely to have completed: national rollouts of **Fibre
to the Premises (FTTP)** — faster connections.

The lecture then runs a "fact check" using **OECD broadband statistics**
(fixed broadband subscriptions per 100 inhabitants by speed tier, June
2023) and reports:

> The OECD did a simple correlation analysis between fixed broadband
> subscription and GDP; the simple correlation coefficient was **0.54
> (moderately correlated)**.

> **r = 0.54 means about 29% of the variation is shared, not 54%.**
>
> ```
> r² = 0.54² = 0.2916 → roughly 29%
> ```
>
> Seventy-one per cent of the variation is unaccounted for, and the
> direction of causation is undetermined — richer countries can afford more
> broadband at least as easily as broadband makes countries rich, and
> national income plausibly drives both. If you cite this figure in A2,
> cite r, r² and the caveat. It is the same discipline Note 04 applies to
> the cash rate and household spending.

Tutorial 8 asks you to build the **commercial case** and the **broader
social case** for Australia Post and NBN separately — which is the right
structure, because a GBE exists precisely where those two cases diverge.

### (iii) Competition policy

**The problem when markets are not competitive:** profit maximisation leads
to **higher prices and lower output** than is economically efficient,
compared with a competitive market.

**Common culprits:**

- **Duopolies and oligopolies**, and collusive behaviour
- **Natural monopolies** — where economies of scale are so large that one
  firm can supply the entire market at a lower average cost than two or
  more firms. Examples: distribution networks, electricity markets

**Policy solution:** regulate against anti-competitive behaviour that
increases prices.

**Who is responsible:**

| Body | Role |
|---|---|
| **ACCC** | Enforcing the Competition and Consumer Act 2010; setting prices at a fair price |
| **Australian Energy Market Commission** | Rule maker for Australian electricity and gas markets |
| **National Competition Council** | Recommends on the regulation of third-party access to services provided by monopoly infrastructure |
| **State independent economic regulators** | Essential services — water, electricity, gas, ports, rail |

> **A natural monopoly is the one case where breaking up the monopolist
> makes things worse.** If one firm genuinely supplies the whole market at
> lower average cost than two, forcing competition duplicates the network
> and raises costs for everyone. That is why the policy response to natural
> monopoly is **price and access regulation** — the NCC's third-party access
> role — rather than structural separation. Do not offer "increase
> competition" as the answer to every uncompetitive market.

### (iv) Labour market policy

Labour market policy covers a broad range of areas, organised by objective:

| Objective | Content |
|---|---|
| **Labour supply** | Population — fertility and immigration; participation |
| **Labour productivity** | Education, skills and training — human capital investment; a highly educated and skilled workforce contributes to a higher rate of economic growth |
| **Functioning of the labour market** | Enterprise agreements, contractual rights, **Modern Slavery Act 2018** |
| **Spending** | Unemployment and related social benefits; early retirement benefits |

**"Labour market policies in disguise"** — the lecture's point that many
policies are labour market policy without being labelled as such:

| Target | Policies |
|---|---|
| **Population and participation** | Pro-natalist policies creating financial and social incentives to increase population — baby bonus / cash incentives, tax incentives, subsidised day care, parental leave |
| **Productivity** | Education and skills policy — testing (NAPLAN) and curriculum, apprenticeships, skills and training incentives for 40–70 year olds, the **Job Ready Graduates Package** |

Tutorial 8's matching exercise:

```
Ensuring labour supply                     → Cheaper childcare
Improving labour productivity              → Jobs Ready Package
Efficient functioning of the labour market → Abolition of penalty rates
Protection of workers                      → Modern Slavery Act (2018)
```

> **Cheaper childcare is labour supply, not "family policy" — and that
> reclassification is the whole lesson.** A childcare subsidy is usually
> presented as cost-of-living or family support. Its labour market function
> is to raise participation, particularly female participation, which Topic
> 5 identified as the main offset to the falling working-age share. Being
> able to see the labour market objective behind a policy that is not
> labelled as one is exactly the analytical move A2 section 2 rewards.

## 8.7 The sector-specific policy: the Dairy Code of Conduct

A2 section 2 requires analysis of **one** policy or regulation directed at
the focus sector. The Dairy Code is the obvious choice, and it maps cleanly
onto everything above.

| Element | Detail |
|---|---|
| **Instrument** | Competition and Consumer (Industry Codes — Dairy) Regulations 2019 |
| **In force** | 1 January 2020 |
| **Market failure addressed** | Information asymmetry and unequal bargaining power between processors and farmers |
| **Trigger** | The ACCC's 2018 Dairy Inquiry, which found "inherent bargaining power imbalances" and recommended a mandatory code with minimum standards and dispute resolution |
| **Precipitating event** | In 2015–16 two of the largest milk processors imposed **retrospective price step-downs** — lowering the price paid for milk already delivered, leaving farmers suddenly owing money or receiving less income |
| **Regulator** | ACCC |
| **Enforcement precedent** | In 2022 the Federal Court found **Lactalis Australia** had breached the Code, including through unilateral contract variations |

**Core provisions and their business-environment effects:**

| Provision | Effect on farmers | Effect on processors |
|---|---|---|
| Written Milk Supply Agreements meeting minimum standards | Greater certainty and clarity | Higher compliance and contract administration costs |
| Prohibition of **retrospective step-downs** | Reduced pricing risk | Less pricing flexibility |
| Publication of standard-form agreements by a set date each year | Ability to compare offers | Increased transparency obligations |
| Cooling-off period and limits on unreasonable exclusive supply | Genuine exit options | Reduced supplier lock-in |
| Mandated dispute resolution — internal review, mediation, arbitration | Lower cost of resolving disputes | More formal dispute processes |
| Record-keeping and ACCC enforcement powers | Stronger accountability | Higher reporting burden and penalty risk |

**Evaluation.** The second statutory review reports a broad consensus that
the Code rebalanced power and improved fairness. Farmers enter each season
knowing price terms are firm and comparable, which restores trust in
contracting. Processors carry higher compliance cost but gain fewer
disputes, more stable supply arrangements and greater predictability.

> **The Code is a transparency and process instrument, not a price
> instrument — and confusing the two will misstate its effect.** It does
> not set the farmgate milk price. It sets *when* the price must be
> published, *whether* it can be changed retrospectively, and *how*
> disputes are resolved. So it does not shield farmers from a falling
> milk price: IBISWorld records that Saputo and others dropped minimum
> prices in 2024 in response to inflationary pressure, entirely within the
> Code. What the Code removed was the *surprise*, not the *risk*. State the
> mechanism precisely and your evaluation will be far better than "the Code
> helped farmers".

1. Distinguish a public good from a common good, and give the policy

2. A government imposes a tax on sugary drinks and raises the top marginal
   income tax rate in the same budget. Are these the same kind of
   instrument?
3. A RIS considers "introduce the regulation" and "do nothing". What is
   missing?
4. Explain why the answer to an uncompetitive market is not always "more
   competition".
5. IPP exceeded targets of 3% of contract number and 1.25% of contract
   value, yet is criticised for creating little social value. How can both
   be true?
6. Does the Dairy Code protect farmers from falling milk prices?

<details><summary>Answers</summary>

1. Both are **non-excludable**. A **public good** is **non-rivalrous** — one
   person's use does not reduce another's (national defence) — so the
   response is **public sector provision**, because no firm can charge for
   it. A **common good** is **rivalrous** — one person's use does reduce
   another's (public parks, water, fisheries) — so it becomes congested and
   overused, and the response is **regulation restricting access** or
   **assigning property rights**.

2. **No.** The sugary drinks tax is a **price instrument** aimed at
   discouraging an activity by changing relative prices — targeted policy.
   The income tax rise is a **revenue instrument**, and if it is intended
   to reduce aggregate demand it is **contractionary fiscal policy** from
   Topic 7. The lecture flags this explicitly: do not confuse a corrective
   tax with income tax.

3. **A range of alternative options.** A RIS is required to critically
   examine the impacts and consequences of a range of alternatives — not a
   single proposal against the status quo. Without alternatives it cannot
   demonstrate that the chosen instrument is the least-cost way to achieve
   the objective, and it cannot illustrate the trade-offs.

4. Because of **natural monopoly**. Where economies of scale are large
   enough that one firm supplies the whole market at lower average cost
   than two or more, forcing entry duplicates the network and raises total
   cost. The correct response is **price and third-party access
   regulation** — the NCC's role — not structural competition.

5. Because the **targets were set on the wrong measure**. Hitting 3% by
   number and 1.25% by value is consistent with a large volume of
   **low-value** contracts across a **narrow range of industries** —
   averaging 11.6 contracts per participating business. Meeting a
   quantitative target is not the same as achieving the objective of
   stimulating Indigenous entrepreneurship and economic development. The
   further critiques — social value miscalculation, policy mutation onto
   Indigenous business, black cladding — are about the objective, not the
   target.

6. **No.** The Code is a transparency and process instrument. It requires
   agreements to be written, published by a set date and free of
   retrospective step-downs, and it mandates dispute resolution. It does
   not set the price. Processors reduced minimum prices in 2024 in response
   to cost pressure, entirely within the Code. It removed the surprise, not
   the risk.

## Summary

- Intervention is justified by **market failure**, not by importance.
  Efficiency = **productive** + **allocative**.
- The taxonomy: **externalities, information asymmetry, public goods,
  common goods, merit goods, uncompetitive markets**, plus inequitable
  allocation and socially undesirable activities.
- **Public = non-rivalrous; common = rivalrous.** Both non-excludable.
- The toolkit: property rights, laws and standards, quotas, nudges, price
  controls, public provision, social marketing, subsidies, corrective tax.
  A corrective **tax is not income tax**.
- **Policy → legislation → strategy → regulation.** No legislative
  authority, no regulation — which is why emerging sectors go unregulated.
- **RIS** exists to force evidence, alternatives, coherence and fewer
  failures. Count the options.
- Expenditure: **social security and welfare 37.0%**, health 15.9%,
  education 6.9% (falling), defence 6.6%.
- **GBEs** = government-controlled, commercial, separate legal personality.
  The NBN broadband correlation is **r = 0.54, r² = 0.29**.
- **Natural monopoly** gets access and price regulation, not forced
  competition.
- Many policies are **labour market policy in disguise** — childcare is
  participation policy.
- The **Dairy Code of Conduct** is the sector-specific instrument: a
  transparency and process fix for bargaining-power asymmetry, not a price
  floor.
