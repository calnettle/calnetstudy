## 9.1 Trade fundamentals

**International trade** is the exchange of goods and services between
countries.

```
exports (X)      products sold to the global market
imports (M)      products bought from the global market
net exports (NX) = X − M
                 net purchases of Australian output by overseas countries
```

**Why trade?** The lecture's chain:

- International markets allow countries to trade what resources or capital
  they have for the resources they need
- A country can use its resources — labour, technology, capital — **more
  efficiently**
- By **exploiting comparative advantage through specialisation**
- Which results in increased consumer choice, competitive markets, reduced
  end prices, increased quality of products and services
- And **increased GDP**

> **Comparative advantage is about opportunity cost, not about being
> better.** A country can be more efficient at producing everything and
> still gain from trade, by specialising where its *opportunity cost* is
> lowest and importing the rest. This is why "New Zealand produces butter
> more cheaply, so Australia should not produce butter" is not the
> argument. The argument is about what Australia gives up to produce butter
> versus what it gives up to produce whey protein — and the sector's own
> behaviour reflects it exactly: with milk scarce, manufacturers have
> allocated the limited supply to **higher-return products** and imported
> butter to fill the gap.

**Net exports and GDP.** NX is a component of expenditure-side GDP, which
is why a trade deficit subtracts and a surplus adds — the link back to
Topic 3.

| **China** | Australia's largest trading partner | **26%** |
| **United States** | 2nd largest | **9.9%** |

top three combined = 26 + 9.9 + 9.0 = 44.9% of two-way trade

> **Concentration is the risk, and one number carries it.** China alone is
> more than a quarter of two-way trade and more than two and a half times
> Australia's second-largest partner. That is the structural geopolitical
> exposure of the Australian business environment, and it is why the trade

**How open to trade is Australia?** The lecture's finding, with a "top tip"
attached — relative comparisons help establish context:

> Trade is a larger proportion of GDP for Australia than for the United
> States and Japan, but is **lower than most other countries**.

Both halves of that sentence are needed. Australia is more open than two
very large economies and less open than most of the rest — which is what
you would expect, since large domestic economies trade less as a share of
GDP.

## 9.3 Foreign direct investment

**Definition:** FDI is when an individual or entity from outside a country
**establishes a new business** or **acquires a substantive share — 10% in
Australia — or more of an enterprise**.

**Why it matters:** FDI is a source of external finance for investment, and
an engine for economic and employment growth.

**Australia's position:** the **16th-highest** amount of direct foreign
inward investment in the world; top-placed are the USA, China and the UK.

**What attracts investment to Australia**, per the lecture:

- Political stability
- Strong regulatory environment (including legal and financial)
- Strong economic growth
- A skilled workforce

The lecture flags this explicitly: "MAKING THE LINK: Lec 2 & 3 and the P
and E in PEST; Lec 7 — the policy environment."

> **The FDI attraction list is your A1 P section, read back to you as an
> investment thesis.** Political stability, a strong regulatory environment
> and enforceable property rights are exactly the institutions you
> described in A1. In A2 you can close the loop: the institutional quality
> established in section P is *why* Australia attracts foreign capital, and
> that capital is part of what shapes the sector's competitive environment
> — Saputo, Lactalis and Fonterra are all foreign-owned entrants holding
> **35.7%** of the focus sector between them.

**Austrade priority sectors for attracting FDI:** tourism infrastructure;
**agribusiness and food**; resources and energy; major infrastructure;
advanced manufacturing services and infrastructure.

## 9.4 Globalisation

**Definition:** the spread of products, technology, information and jobs
across national borders and cultures.

Four dimensions:

| Dimension | Content |
|---|---|
| **Economic** | Interconnectedness of economies through trade and the exchange of resources; integration of international financial markets; growth of transnational corporations in international markets |
| **Political** | Policies designed to facilitate international trade and commerce, facilitated through national governments and international institutions — UN, WTO, IMF |
| **Cultural** | Convergence of cultures through increased ease of communication and transportation, brought about by technology |
| **Social** | Sharing of ideas and information between and through different countries; greater interaction between populations |

The lecture links globalisation directly back to Topic 6: each industrial
revolution enabled a further wave of it.

## 9.5 Global value chains

**The headline figure:** world trade statistics show that **70–80% of world
trade in goods and services is related to Global Value Chains** — where
different stages of the production process are located across different
countries.

**Drivers:**

- Liberalisation of trade and investment
- Lower transport costs
- Advances in information and communication technology
- Innovations in logistics — e.g. **containerisation**
- Transnational corporations continuously restructuring and relocating
  operations for competitive reasons

**Two mechanisms that are routinely confused:**

| | Definition |
|---|---|
| **Offshoring** | Labour-intensive stages of production **shift from industrialised economies to low-wage, labour-abundant developing countries** |
| **Outsourcing** | Production activities are increasingly undertaken by **third parties with no equity links** to the transnational corporation |

> **Offshoring is about *where*; outsourcing is about *who*.** They are
> independent choices and all four combinations exist. A firm can offshore
> without outsourcing — a wholly owned plant overseas. It can outsource
> without offshoring — a domestic contract manufacturer. Using the words
> interchangeably in A2 is a straightforward marks loss, and the sector has
> examples of both: Fonterra divesting its consumer business is
> outsourcing-adjacent restructuring, while importing New Zealand butter to
> fill domestic demand is not offshoring at all — it is simply importing.

**Reshoring** — relocating value chain activities, particularly production,
back to the domestic market. The lecture's four drivers:

| Driver | Content |
|---|---|
| **Adoption of Industry 4.0** | Reduction of cost differentials between home and host countries |
| **National policies (endogenous)** | Sustainability objectives; nationalism |
| **Social licence to operate** | Customer dissatisfaction — e.g. country-of-origin effects; social responsibility pressures |
| **External disruptors (exogenous)** | COVID; geo-politics — trade wars, conflicts |

> **Industry 4.0 is a reshoring driver because automation erodes the wage
> gap.** If labour is a small share of production cost, locating near
> cheap labour stops paying. That is the direct link from Topic 6 to this
> topic, and it is the mechanism behind the lecture's otherwise abstract
> "reduction of cost differentials". It also explains why reshoring rarely
> brings back the same number of jobs it took away.

**Trade interdependence** — the lecture's two illustrations are COVID-19
vaccines and the 2015 Australian passport, both assembled from inputs
sourced across many countries. The point is that even a sovereign document
is a global value chain product.

## 9.6 The focus sector's trade position

Every figure below is from the IBISWorld C1133c report for 2024–25 and has
been reconciled against its own data tables.

| | 2024–25 | 5-yr CAGR to 2025 | 5-yr CAGR to 2030 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Total imports | **$533.3m** | −2.4% | +0.6% |
| Total exports | **$543.5m** | +4.2% | +0.3% |
| Trade balance | **Surplus $10.2m** | | |

```
543.5 − 533.3 = 10.2  ✓ the stated surplus reconciles exactly
```

Verified CAGRs from the report's own Key Statistics table (2019–20 base):

```
exports  443.1 → 543.5 over 5 years:  (543.5/443.1)^(1/5) − 1 = +4.17%  ✓ 4.2%
imports  601.0 → 533.3 over 5 years:  (533.3/601.0)^(1/5) − 1 = −2.36%  ✓ 2.4% fall
```

> **A $10.2 million surplus on $1.08 billion of two-way trade is a rounding
> error, not a comfortable position.** The sector is 50.6% exporter and
> 49.4% importer by value. Describing it as "a net exporter" is technically
> right and analytically misleading — the balance would flip on a 2%
> movement in either direction. Say "marginally net exporting" and give
> both gross figures.

**Import composition:**

- **New Zealand, the United States, France and India** make up just over
  **five-sixths** (≈83.3%) of butter and other dairy product imports
- **New Zealand alone** supplies just below **three-quarters** of total
  imports
- **Butter accounts for over half** of butter and dairy product imports
- The United States was the largest supplier of whey, at **66.1% of total
  whey import value in 2023–24**

**Export composition:**

- Top four export destinations: **China, Singapore, New Zealand, Malaysia**
- Those four expected to account for **over 50%** of total export revenue
  through 2024–25
- In 2023–24: **China, Indonesia, the Netherlands and Japan** were the
  largest markets for **protein** products, while **Singapore, New Zealand,
  Malaysia and Hong Kong** were the top destinations for **yoghurt**

> **The trade partner list changes with the product, and quoting one list
> for the sector is a scope error.** Protein goes to China, Indonesia, the
> Netherlands and Japan. Yoghurt goes to Singapore, New Zealand, Malaysia
> and Hong Kong. Those are different markets with different drivers —
> Indonesia is price-sensitive and scaled back on higher protein prices,
> while Japan pays a premium for quality. A single "top export markets"
> sentence flattens the two most important facts about the sector's export
> exposure.

**The exchange rate mechanism** is central and runs in both directions:

```
AUD APPRECIATES →  exports dearer overseas   → export volumes fall
                →  imports cheaper here      → import competition rises
                →  imported inputs cheaper   → input costs fall

AUD DEPRECIATES →  the reverse on all three
```

IBISWorld lists the **trade-weighted index as a negative key external
driver** for the sector, and attributes both the slowing export outlook and
intensifying import competition to a forecast stronger Australian dollar.

## 9.7 Trade policy and agreements

**Multilateral.** As a WTO member, Australia operates under rules that
limit protective tariffs and prohibit arbitrary trade restrictions,
improving access to foreign markets and providing a **dispute mechanism**
if a market imposes unjustified barriers.

**Bilateral and regional agreements relevant to the sector:**

| Agreement | Effect |
|---|---|
| **JAEPA** (Japan) | Improved access for Australian dairy into Japan |
| **ChAFTA** (China, 2015) | Phased out Chinese tariffs on dairy |
| **CPTPP** | Reduced Japanese tariffs on certain cheese products; new duty-free quotas for butter and skim milk powder |
| **A-UKFTA** (in force 2022–23) | Expands tariff-free quotas for **butter, yoghurt and whey through December 2027**; from **January 2028**, unlimited tariff-free volumes of yoghurt, whey and butter |
| **RCEP** | Further regional liberalisation |
| **ANZCERTA** (Closer Economic Relations) | Tariff-free dairy trade with New Zealand — the reason NZ dominates imports |

> **Trade liberalisation cuts both ways and the sector feels both edges at
> once.** The same low-tariff environment that opens Asian markets to
> Australian whey and yoghurt is what lets New Zealand butter take just
> under three-quarters of Australian dairy imports. In A2, resist writing
> the agreements up as unambiguously positive: the honest framing is
> "expanded export access **and** intensified import competition", which is
> precisely how the lecture and IBISWorld both put it.

**A protectionist counter-example.** In **April 2025 the United States
introduced a 10.0% baseline tariff** on Australian goods exported to the
US, and on US goods exported to Australia. IBISWorld's read: the US is not
a top-four export destination for this sector but **is its second-largest
import source**, so the tariff plus a relatively strong US dollar is
expected to raise US supplier prices and discourage purchases from the
US — *providing more opportunities for domestic manufacturers*.

> **A tariff on your imports can help you. That is worth saying explicitly
> in A2** because it is counter-intuitive and it is well evidenced here.
> The sector's exposure to the US is asymmetric — small as a customer,
> large as a supplier of whey (66.1% of whey import value) — so a barrier
> that raises the price of US product is, on net, protective for domestic
> producers even though nobody designed it that way.

## 9.8 Geopolitics: the section 4 material

A2 section 4 asks for an overview of contemporary geopolitical issues with
a focus on **one** that has substantially impacted the sector.

### The general disruption picture

- **Global trade tensions** — tariff regimes, trade wars
- **COVID-19** — supply chain disruption, demand collapse and rebound
- **The Russia–Ukraine war** — 2022 energy price surge worldwide, filtering
  through to higher farm input costs such as fertiliser and diesel
- **Shipping and freight** — maritime route disruption raising global
  freight costs

The transmission chain to a dairy manufacturer:

```
geopolitical shock → energy and fertiliser prices → farm input costs
    → farmgate milk price → processor purchase costs (72.3% of revenue)
    → margin (4.2%)
```

> **In a 4.2% margin business with purchases at 72.3% of revenue, a small
> input shock is a large profit shock.** A 5% rise in purchase costs is
> 3.6 percentage points of revenue — most of the entire margin. That
> arithmetic is why input-cost geopolitics matters more to this sector than
> to most, and it is the sentence that turns a general geopolitics
> paragraph into sector analysis.

### Climate change as the focus issue

**International governance:**

- Nearly 200 countries adopted the **Paris Agreement** in 2015, committing
  to limit warming to well below 2 °C, aiming for 1.5 °C
- Over 120 nations, including Australia, have signed the **Global Methane
  Pledge** to collectively reduce methane emissions **30% by 2030**

**Australian policy:**

- In 2022 the federal government legislated an emissions reduction target
  of **43% below 2005 levels by 2030**, and **net zero by 2050**
- Australia joined the Global Methane Pledge; the Agriculture Minister
  framed it as helping "ensure ongoing access to international markets for
  Australia's $24 billion livestock and dairy industry"
- The **$5 billion Future Drought Fund** provides **$100 million per year**
  for drought resilience programs

**The measured cost to farms:** ABARES estimates that changing seasonal
conditions from **2001–2020 reduced average annual farm profits by about
23%**, roughly **$29,000 per farm**.

```
if 23% ≈ $29,000, the implied baseline profit is
29,000 ÷ 0.23 = $126,087 per farm
```

The two figures are internally consistent, which is a good sign for the
source.

**Industry response:**

| Initiative | Content |
|---|---|
| **Australian Dairy Sustainability Framework** | Target of reducing greenhouse gas **emissions intensity** by 30% by 2030 |
| **Dairy Australia Climate Change Strategy (2020–2025)** | Guides investment in research and farmer decision support to cut on-farm emissions and improve climate resilience |
| **On-farm adoption** | **94%** of Australian dairy farms have implemented at least one practice to reduce greenhouse emissions |
| **Adaptation** | Diversifying feed sources and contracting feed from multiple regions; on-farm fodder storage; additional water infrastructure |
| **Innovation** | Feed additives such as Australian-made red seaweed supplements to cut methane; heat-tolerant pasture crops |
| **Global** | Major dairy companies have joined alliances to curb methane and improve transparency in emissions reporting |

> **The Framework's target is emissions *intensity*, not absolute emissions
> — and dropping that one word changes the claim.** Intensity is emissions
> per unit of output. A 30% intensity reduction is fully compatible with
> *rising* total emissions if production grows more than 30%. the submitted A2
> reported it as "reducing greenhouse gas emissions by 30% by 2030",
> dropping "intensity" — which converts an efficiency target into an
> absolute one. The source (and the Dairy Australia page behind it) says
> intensity. Write intensity.

> **A second precision point in the same paragraph: "94% of farms have
> implemented at least one practice" is an adoption count, not an outcome.**
> "At least one practice" is a very low bar — it counts a farm that changed
> its feed mix identically to one that installed a full effluent-capture
> system. It is evidence of engagement, not of emissions reduction. Report
> it as adoption and say so.

### Milk supply as the transmission point

The climate story reaches the focus sector through the **milk pool**:

```
drought / flood → lower milk production → farmers exit dairy farming
    → smaller domestic milk pool → constrained manufacturer output
    → milk allocated to higher-return products
    → butter shortfall filled by imports (mostly NZ)
```

IBISWorld records the volatility directly: farmgate milk prices "skyrocketed
by more than **25.0%** in 2022-23 as output dipped **6.0%**".

```
a 6.0% fall in output associated with a >25% rise in price
implies demand for the input is highly price-inelastic
```

> **That price-output pair is the single best piece of quantitative
> evidence available for section 4.** A 6% supply contraction producing a
> 25%+ price rise means processors have almost no ability to substitute
> away from milk in the short run — which is exactly why a climate shock at
> the farm gate lands as a margin shock at the factory. It is a measured
> elasticity in a section that otherwise fills with qualitative claims.

## Summary

- **NX = X − M**, and it is a component of GDP.
- Trade rests on **comparative advantage** — opportunity cost, not absolute
  efficiency.
- Partners: **China 26%**, **US 9.9%**, **Japan 9.0%** of two-way trade.
  China alone is 2.6× the US.
- **FDI** = a new business or ≥**10%** of an existing enterprise. Australia
  ranks **16th** for inward FDI, attracted by political stability,
  regulatory strength, growth and skills.
- Globalisation has **economic, political, cultural and social**
  dimensions; **70–80% of world trade** is GVC-related.
- **Offshoring = where. Outsourcing = who.** Reshoring is driven by
  Industry 4.0, national policy, SLO and external disruption.
- The sector is **marginally net exporting**: $543.5m out, $533.3m in,
  $10.2m surplus. NZ supplies just under three-quarters of imports; the US
  supplies 66.1% of whey.
- **A-UKFTA** gives tariff-free quotas for butter, yoghurt and whey to
  December 2027, then unlimited from January 2028.
- Climate: **43% below 2005 by 2030**, net zero 2050, Global Methane Pledge
  30% by 2030. The industry target is **30% emissions intensity**, not
  absolute.
- Transmission runs **climate → milk pool → purchase cost (72.3% of
  revenue) → margin (4.2%)**. A 6% output fall moved farmgate prices more
  than 25%.
