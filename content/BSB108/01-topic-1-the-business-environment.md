# TOPIC 1 — The Business Environment and PEST

Week 1. What BSB108 means by "the business environment", how the PEST
framework is used here (and the three ways students misuse it), how to
judge whether a source is fit for a professional report, and how to define
a focus sector in 200 words.

## 1.1 The unit's definition

The definition you are assessed against is narrow and worth memorising
word for word:

> The business environment is "an external environment that firms cannot
> necessarily influence or control but need to understand to be able to
> adapt to".

Three load-bearing words:

- **External** — outside the firm. Anything internal to a company (its
  costs, its pricing, its management) is out of scope.
- **Cannot necessarily influence or control** — this is what makes it an
  *environment* rather than a *strategy*. A firm adapts to it; it does not
  set it.
- **Understand to be able to adapt** — the purpose of the analysis is
  adaptation, not prediction and not recommendation.

The Week 1 lecture presents four key elements of the business environment
on slide 2, reproduced from an OpenStax figure (Rice University, CC-BY
4.0) that Drilling Down 1 also uses. **That slide is an image with no
extractable text**, so these notes do not list the four elements — check
the slide itself rather than trusting a paraphrase. What Drilling Down 1
asks you to do with it is clear enough: summarise the key points against
each external sub-environment in the figure.

## 1.2 PEST as BSB108 uses it

PEST is political, economic, social, technology. You may have met it
before. **The version you have met before will fail this assessment.**

| | Common commercial use of PEST | BSB108 use of PEST |
|---|---|---|
| Unit of analysis | One firm | The national business environment |
| Purpose | Assess influences on a company's profitability | Scan the macro environment a sector operates in |
| Output | Strategic recommendation | Objective overview, no recommendation |
| Political means | Government stability, tax policy, trade policy | The **institutions** that create, enforce and apply laws, plus the sector's peak bodies |
| Economic means | Exchange rates, interest rates, company costs | The state of the economy via **GDP, unemployment and inflation** only |
| Social means | Customer demographics for the firm | National social and economic **trends** |
| Technology means | Tech the firm could adopt | How technology **shapes the business environment and productivity** |

The A1 task sheet is explicit: using PEST as an internal analysis of a
company's profitability "will result in a failing grade".

> **Three word-pairs decide whether you pass A1.** The unit repeats them in
> the lecture, the tutorial and the Canvas drafting pages:
>
> - **political (A1) ≠ policy (A2)** — P is who makes and enforces the
>   rules; policy is what the rules do. Policy earns zero marks in A1.
> - **regulatory institutions (A1) ≠ regulation (A2)** — naming the ACCC
>   and FSANZ is A1; analysing the Dairy Code of Conduct is A2.
> - **macroeconomic indicators (A1) ≠ macroeconomic policy (A2)** — the
>   inflation *rate* is A1; the RBA's *response* to it is A2.
>
> The task sheet adds: "You will not receive any acknowledgement for
> raising these topics in Assessment 1."

## 1.3 The assessment triangle

Every tutorial from week 2 onward shows the same diagram. It is the single
most useful structural device in the unit.

```
                    THE BIG PICTURE
        macro-level analysis of the Australian
        business environment as a whole
                         │
                         ▼
              SECTOR-SPECIFIC EXAMPLES
        micro-level: how that environment
        impacts the focus sector
```

Each element of PEST should do both: describe the national environment,
then drill into what it means for butter and dairy product manufacturing.

Two qualifications the unit makes explicitly:

1. **You will not be able to link every point to the focus sector, and that
   is fine.** The tutorial says so directly: a defining question still
   belongs in the report even if it has no sector hook, because it is
   still a critical element of the environment the sector operates in.
2. **Macroeconomic policy is an exception.** Tutorial 6 notes that if macro
   policy is working it is good for every sector, so for A2 section 1 the
   marker expects more big picture and fewer sector-specific examples.
   Don't force the triangle there.

> **"Sector-specific" is not "company-specific".** Analysing Bega's margin
> or Saputo's pricing strategy is firm-level analysis and belongs to a
> different unit. Sector-specific means the *sector's* exposure — its
> regulator, its peak body, its position in the business cycle.

## 1.4 Defining the focus sector (~200 words)

The A1 report opens with a short sector definition. Tutorial 1 gives the
structure:

1. **What is the focus sector, and what activities does it undertake?**
2. **Is it part of a larger industry?** If so, give the client context.
3. **How big is it?**
4. **How important is it?** Role in the economy, whether it has always
   been important, whether it will be in future — and why.

Tutorial 1 then asks you to mark a deliberately bad answer. The bad answer
(written about aged care) reads:

> "There are a wide range of providers and organisations that offer
> services… Billions of dollars have been invested into this sector, so
> this is important for the economy. The number of employees within the
> sector is sizeable and the government also provides the sector with
> massive amounts funding."

What is wrong with it, against the four prompts:

| Prompt | Bad answer | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Activities | "a wide range of providers" — no activities named | Name the production activities and the value chain |
| Larger industry | Not addressed | Say what it sits inside and what it excludes |
| How big | "billions", "sizeable", "massive amounts" | Cite a revenue figure, a business count, an employment count |
| How important | "will continue to be important for the elderly" | Give the economic role, with a source |

Every quantifier in the bad answer is unfalsifiable. That is the lesson:
in a professional report, an adjective without a number is a filler word.

> **The lecture is not a citable source.** Drilling Down 1 says it and
> Tutorial 1 repeats it. The lecture gives you the *facts* to chase — the
> sector is worth $8.5 billion, there are four big players, there are 134
> manufacturers — but you must find and cite the primary source for each.
> IBISWorld, DAFF, Dairy Australia and the ABS are where those numbers
> actually live.

## 1.5 Evaluating a source: the five criteria

Drilling Down 1 Task 4 gives five criteria, drawn from QUT Study Smart
module 3.1.2. Every source in both reports should survive all five.

| Criterion | Question to ask | Failure looks like |
|---|---|---|
| **Authority** | Who wrote it? What standing do they have? | An unattributed blog; a Discord post; a lecture slide |
| **Currency** | When was it published? Is that recent enough for the claim? | 2018 data used to describe "the current economy" |
| **Purpose** | Why does it exist — to inform, to sell, to lobby? | A peak body's advocacy paper cited as neutral fact |
| **Objectivity** | Is there a position being argued? | An industry submission presented as an evaluation |
| **Reliability** | Can the claim be traced and verified? | A statistic with no underlying data source |

The unit's rule: **if a resource fails, do not use it.**

Drilling Down 1 adds a nuance most students miss. Currency is not
absolute. Drilling Down 5 uses a 2007 article on Indigenous wellbeing and
says so openly: "strictly speaking [it] does not pass the currency test",
then explains that the *message* being drawn (that disparities existed) is
still valid, and pairs it with the Productivity Commission's current
Closing the Gap dashboard for the present-day position.

> **You can relax currency — but only if you say what you are relaxing it
> for.** An old source is legitimate for establishing that something *was*
> the case, or for a definition, or for a historical baseline. It is never
> legitimate as evidence of the *current* state of the environment. State
> which use you are making of it.

### Primary vs secondary vs summary sources

Drilling Down 2 makes a related point using the ACCC's 2019 Digital
Platforms Inquiry. The final report is long; the ABC's news summary of it
is short. The unit's guidance:

- Summaries from state-owned national media (ABC, BBC) are useful "abridged
  versions" that walk you through the salient points.
- They are **not a perfect substitute** for the primary resource.
- Always verify the detail by cross-referencing back to the primary source.

## 1.6 The focus sector: butter and dairy product manufacturing

The Week 1 lecture defines the sector by what it makes and by what it
deliberately excludes.

**Included — four core production activities:**

| Activity | Examples |
|---|---|
| Butter products | Table butter, spreadable butter |
| Yoghurt and cultured products | Natural and flavoured yoghurts, sour cream, buttermilk |
| Dairy proteins | Casein and whey protein powders |
| Other dairy products | Custard, condensed milk, flavoured milk beverages |

**Excluded:** cheese, ice cream and drinking milk itself. All three are
separate ANZSIC classes.

> **The exclusions are the whole point of the definition.** A client with
> no Australian knowledge will assume "dairy" includes cheese and milk. If
> you quote a whole-of-dairy figure — Australia's ~$3.8 billion of dairy
> exports, say — against a sector whose own exports are $543.5 million, you
> have overstated the sector by roughly seven times. Name the base every
> time you quote a number: is it the sector, or the industry it sits in?

**Key value-chain activities**, as the lecture lists them:

- Sourcing raw milk from dairy farmers and contracting milk supply
- Processing, separation and concentration (cream, skim, whey, casein)
- Fermentation and culture management
- Drying, blending and standardising powdered proteins and dairy ingredients
- Packaging, labelling and compliance with the Food Standards Code
- Product development and reformulation (high-protein, reduced/zero sugar,
  flavoured products)

**Scale**, per the lecture: 134 manufacturers, four big players (Bega,
Saputo, Lactalis, Fonterra), and an $8.5 billion annual contribution to the
Australian economy.

Note 10 works through the full sector dossier with every one of those
figures reconciled against the IBISWorld data tables.

## 1.7 Two research warnings the unit issues

### "I got a 7, just do what I did"

Tutorial 3 quotes a fictional Discord post advising students to analyse a
company's profitability, pricing strategy and exchange-rate exposure for
the E in PEST, and to screen-grab any figures that look relevant. The
tutorial's response is a small masterclass in source evaluation:

- **Authority fails.** "Students can claim any mark for a piece of work.
  Whether you can verify it is another matter."
- **The content is wrong.** Company profitability is firm-level; exchange
  rates and international trade are A2, not A1.
- **The method is a fail.** Screen-grabbing breaches PC3.1.

### IBISWorld and the paywall

Tutorial 1 notes that Copilot's definition of the sector appears to come
from IBISWorld — but IBISWorld sits behind a paywall, so a public LLM can
only see the abstract. You, through the QUT library, have the full report.
Comparing the two is the tutorial exercise, and the point generalises: an
AI summary of a paywalled source is a summary of the marketing blurb.

## 1.8 A statistics claim in the unit's own materials

Tutorial 1 reports an in-house analysis to encourage engagement:

> The team took Semester 2 2024 A1 results, used completion of each weekly
> formative quiz as a proxy for engaging in all that module's learning,
> "stuck all the data into a statistical model", noted "a lot of
> variability in the model", and found the quizzes statistically
> significant — "each quiz that a student completed added four marks to
> their assessment results".

Doing the work almost certainly does help. But the claim as stated is a
useful object lesson for Topic 4, because it has three identifiable
weaknesses:

1. **Proxy variable.** Quiz completion stands in for "did all the
   learning". A student can click through a quiz in two minutes.
2. **Selection, not treatment.** Students who complete optional quizzes
   differ systematically from those who don't — in organisation, time
   available, and prior preparation. An observational association is not
   an experiment.
3. **"A lot of variability" is an admission about R², not about
   significance.** A coefficient can be statistically significant and still
   explain very little of the variation between students.

> **Statistical significance answers "is the effect distinguishable from
> zero?", not "how big is it?" and not "did X cause Y?"** Those are three
> separate questions and the exam will reward you for keeping them
> separate. The same trap appears in Topic 8 with the OECD's broadband
> correlation of 0.54.

## Summary

- The business environment is **external** and **not controllable** by the
  firm; you analyse it to adapt to it.
- BSB108's PEST is a **macro scan of the national environment**, not an
  internal analysis of a company. Using it the commercial way fails.
- **Political ≠ policy. Regulatory institutions ≠ regulation.
  Macroeconomic indicators ≠ macroeconomic policy.** A1 gets the first of
  each pair; A2 gets the second.
- The **assessment triangle** — big picture, then sector-specific example —
  structures every section. Macro policy in A2 is the one exception.
- Sector definition covers **activities, larger industry, size,
  importance**, all with numbers and citations.
- Judge every source on **Authority, Currency, Purpose, Objectivity,
  Reliability**, and discard the ones that fail.
- The focus sector is butter, yoghurt and cultured products, dairy proteins
  and other dairy products — **not** cheese, ice cream or drinking milk.
  134 manufacturers, four majors, $8.5 billion.
