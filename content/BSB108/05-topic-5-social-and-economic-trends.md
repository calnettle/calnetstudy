# TOPIC 5 — Social and Economic Trends (the S in PEST)

Week 4 lecture, week 5 tutorial. The structural shift out of manufacturing
and into services, an ageing and changing population, the truth about
"casualisation", and what wellbeing data says about how Australians are
actually doing.

The scope for A1's **S**: sectoral composition of the economy, population
and demographics, social attitudes, employment, and trends in life
satisfaction/well-being.

## 5.1 Why trends are hard to track

The lecture opens with two warnings that are worth a sentence in the report
itself:

- **Data availability does not always keep up** with the pace of economic
  and social change.
- **Definitions change over time.**

Both matter for this section more than any other, because the concepts —
"casual", "gig worker", "household" — are contested.

## 5.2 The changing structure of the economy

Australia has shifted away from manufacturing toward services. Drilling
Down 4 frames it as a normal feature of economic development: as economies
become wealthier, consumers spend a greater share of income on services,
which feeds back into sectoral composition.

Two figures from the sources:

| Measure | Value | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Services share of Australian production | 72.8% (1994–95) → 80.0% (2017–18) | ABS, *Services in the Australian economy* |
| Manufacturing share of output | 5.7% | RBA economy composition snapshot |
| Health and education share of output | 13.9% | RBA economy composition snapshot |
| Mining share of output | 9.9% | RBA economy composition snapshot |

> **A rise from 72.8% to 80.0% is +7.2 percentage points, or +9.9% in
> relative terms — and only one of those is the right number to quote.**
>
> ```
> percentage point change = 80.0 − 72.8            = +7.2 pp
> relative change         = (80.0 ÷ 72.8) − 1      = +9.9%
> ```
>
> For a *share*, the percentage-point change is the honest statement. If
> you write "services grew by 10%" a reader will hear "output grew 10%",
> which is a different and much larger claim. The unit tests this
> distinction; use "percentage points" explicitly.

The lecture's business-environment point: growth in **business services**
and **household services** is driving demand for high-level cognitive
skills. Drilling Down 4 asks which industries make up each category — and
the tutorial adds a warning worth heeding:

> "Don't think you know what the definition of terms are just because you
> understand the individual words. It is not a good look to argue that 'the
> increase in household services means more people are cleaners'."

For a manufacturer, the sectoral shift is a **competition-for-inputs**
story rather than a demand story: workforce attraction, regional plant
viability and investment priorities all slip relative to growing sectors.

## 5.3 Population and demographics

### Three drivers of the changing age structure

- Declining fertility rates
- Increased life expectancy
- Migration

### The ageing numbers

From the lecture (drawing on the Intergenerational Report tradition):

| Cohort | 2002 | 2042 | Change |
|---|---|---|---|
| Aged 65+ | 2.5 million | 6.2 million | +148% |
| Aged 65+ as share of population | ~13% | ~25% | +12 pp |
| Aged 85+ | 300,000 | 1.1 million | +267% |

The dependency-ratio series, working-age Australians per person aged 65+:

```
mid-1970s   7.4
2015        4.4
2055        3.2  (projected)

7.4 → 3.2 is a fall of 56.8%
```

The submitted A1 report adds the current Treasury projection: 65+ rising
from **16% of the
population in 2019–20 to 23% by 2060–61** (2021 Intergenerational Report),
and the 2023 Population Statement's projection of **26 million (June 2022)
to 30.9 million by June 2034**.

> **Two ageing series, two different numbers, and both are right.** The
> lecture's "13% to 25% between 2002 and 2042" and Treasury's "16% to 23%
> between 2019–20 and 2060–61" describe the same phenomenon over different
> windows with different projection assumptions. Do not average them,
> splice them, or quote one as an update of the other. Pick one series,
> state its source and its window, and stay in it.

### The impacts, by category

The lecture organises them under three headings — a ready-made structure
for this part of the S section.

**Political**

- The "grey vote": baby boomers accounted for close to **1 in 4** of the
  16.4 million people who voted on 18 May 2019 — roughly **4.1 million
  voters**.

**Economic**

- Fiscal budget: ageing is projected to **subtract 0.4%** from annual real
  growth in revenue and **add 0.3%** to annual real growth in spending.
- Government spending on health, aged care and the age pension: increasing
  total spending by **0.7% of GDP ($16 billion) in 2028–29** above the level
  it would otherwise be.
- Consumer spending: "cashed up baby boomers like to spend", described as
  an above-average fast-spending mass-market demographic.

**Labour market**

- The population of prime working age has begun to fall.
- The dependency ratio series above.

> **A 0.4% revenue drag and a 0.3% spending push are growth rates, not
> levels — and they compound.** Read carefully: ageing reduces the *annual
> real growth rate* of revenue by 0.4 percentage points and raises the
> *annual real growth rate* of spending by 0.3. A 0.7 percentage point
> gap opening every year is a structural deficit, not a one-off cost.
> That is why the $16 billion figure for 2028–29 is expressed as "above
> levels in the absence of ageing" rather than as total spending.

### Generational cohorts

Generational cohorts are people born during a particular period who are
assumed to have shared experiences, behaviours and ideals. The unit uses
the McCrindle framework (Builders, Boomers, Gen X, Gen Y/Millennials, Gen
Z, Gen Alpha born 2010–2024).

The tutorial's quiz asks which is the **biggest cohort in Australia** and
tells you to check your answer rather than trusting instinct.

> **"Assumed to have shared experiences" is the load-bearing phrase.**
> Generational labels are a marketing construct laid over a continuous
> variable — a person born on 31 December is not categorically different
> from one born the next day. They are useful for framing, and dangerous
> as evidence. In a report, use them to organise a discussion of changing
> attitudes, not as a causal explanation.

### Household structure

| Trend | Figure | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Lone-person households | 26–28% of all households by 2046 | ABS Household and Family Projections |
| 19-year-olds living with parents | 72% in 2021, "up 9% since 2006" | AIFS |

> **"Up 9% since 2006" is ambiguous and you should not repeat it as
> written.** It reads two ways:
>
> ```
> percentage points:  72% − 9 pp        → 63% in 2006
> relative:           72 ÷ 1.09         → 66.1% in 2006
> ```
>
> Three percentage points apart, from the same sentence. Go to the AIFS
> release, read which it means, and write "up 9 percentage points from
> 63%" or "up from 66% to 72%". The general rule: never carry a bare "%"
> increase applied to a figure that is itself a percentage.

Business-environment reading: more single-adult households points to
smaller pack sizes and convenience-led shopping; young adults living at
home longer, linked to housing affordability and cost of living, points to
higher grocery price sensitivity.

## 5.4 Social attitudes and cost-of-living pressure

The submitted A1 report uses the Scanlon Foundation's *Mapping Social
Cohesion 2023*:
**47% of 18–44 year olds** reported "just getting along" financially, and
**66% of 18–24 year olds** reported feeling isolated. Note the two figures
use **different age bases** — 18–44 and 18–24 — so they cannot be compared
with each other.

Consumer values, from the FSANZ Consumer Insights Tracker: **73%** of
consumers reported putting effort into maintaining a healthy diet, and
nutrition was the most-selected food value at **66%**.

CSIRO's *Our Future World* identifies seven megatrends out to 2042. The two
that sit under "social and economic" for this sector are **"Leaner, cleaner
and greener"** and **"The escalating health imperative"**.

## 5.5 Casualisation — the myth-busting exercise

The lecture sets this up as "let's play myth busters". The headlines say
casualisation is rising. The ABS data says otherwise.

### The definitional problem

The **ABS definition of a casual worker** is an employee with:

- No paid leave entitlements — including paid sick leave or holiday leave
- A casual loading — a higher hourly rate compensating for the absence of
  paid holiday and/or sick leave

That is a narrow, entitlement-based definition. What most people *mean* by
"casualisation" is something broader: insecurity, unpredictability, and
the decline of the traditional permanent nine-to-five job.

### What the data shows

| Claim | Data |
|---|---|
| Casualisation is rising | Not according to ABS data |
| Part-time work is rising | Yes — but the growth is in **permanent** part-time |
| Casual part-timers, 2009–2016 | **+13%** |
| Permanent part-timers, 2009–2016 | **+36%** |
| Multiple job holders, 1997 | 5.2% |
| Multiple job holders, 2016 | 5.6% |
| Multiple job holders, 2021 | 6.3% |

```
permanent part-time growth ÷ casual part-time growth = 36 ÷ 13 = 2.8×
multiple job holders 5.2% → 6.3%  = +1.1 pp = +21% relative
```

> **Permanent part-time grew nearly three times faster than casual
> part-time — which inverts the headline entirely.** The rise in part-time
> work is real; the rise in *casual* part-time work is comparatively small.
> If you report only "part-time work is up", you have supported a
> casualisation narrative the underlying data contradicts.

Why part-time work rose, per the lecture: structural change in the economy
(growth of health, education, tourism and hospitality), an increase in
full-time education, and an increase in female participation.

### The gig economy

- Virtual platforms provide "a new, cheap form of control that may replace
  the need for the employment relationship".
- **ABS classifies gig work as a form of self-employment** — and
  self-employment has *decreased*.
- "Gigification" measured by multiple job holding rose from 5.2% (1997) to
  6.3% (2021) — real, but a 1.1 percentage point rise over 24 years.
- The submitted A1 report adds the ABS estimate that **0.96% of employed
people** are
  digital platform workers.

> **When the data contradicts the headline, the usual culprit is the
> definition, not the data.** Gig work sits inside "self-employment" in the
> ABS framework, so a person who leaves a small business to drive for a
> platform shows up as no change at all. Drilling Down 4 makes the same
> point via the Peetz (2023) report: the *character* of self-employment is
> shifting from running an independent business toward gig work, without
> the headline count moving. The trend is real and the indicator cannot see
> it. Saying that is worth more marks than either "casualisation is rising"
> or "casualisation is a myth".

Drilling Down 4 closes the exercise with a note on research method:

> "Were you surprised that the data did not confirm an epidemic of
> casualisation in the workplace? When researching be aware of your own
> cognitive bias to seek out research that confirms your beliefs."

### Other employment trends

From the submitted A1 report, sourced to ABS *Characteristics of Employment*
(August 2025)
unless noted:

| Trend | Figure |
|---|---|
| Employed people who usually work from home | 36% |
| Casual employees | 2.4 million |
| Casuals as a share of employees | 19% |

```
2.4m ÷ 0.19 = 12.6 million employees
```

> **19% of *employees*, not 19% of *employed people*.** The implied
> employee base of 12.6 million is well below total employment of about
> 14.7 million, and the gap is owner-managers, who are not employees. Get
> the base wrong and you will misstate the casual share by roughly two
> percentage points. Name the denominator.

### Female participation

Female participation has risen from about **40% to 60% over three
decades**, concentrated in sectors offering more flexible hours. Despite
that growth, female participation and pay remain lower than male.

Drilling Down 4 sends you to the **WGEA Data Explorer** to compare gender
statistics at economy, industry and employer level, targeted at the focus
sector, and to the BCEC/WGEA *Gender Equity Insights 2020* report for the
business case.

## 5.6 Life satisfaction and wellbeing

### The HILDA survey

The Household, Income and Labour Dynamics in Australia survey collects data
from more than **17,000 Australians each year** on economic and personal
wellbeing, labour market dynamics and family life. Its core question:

> "All things considered, how satisfied are you with your life overall?"
> Responses range from **0 to 10**.

### What HILDA found

Over almost two decades (2001–2018), Australians' life satisfaction has
been **fairly constant at relatively high levels**, driven by basic factors
— health, safety and social contact.

Life satisfaction is **U-shaped in age**: the oldest and youngest report the
highest satisfaction.

| Group | Finding |
|---|---|
| Females vs males | Females happier |
| Ages 35–44 and 45–54 | Consistently the **least** satisfied |
| Unemployed people | Much less satisfied |
| Higher education | **Lower** reported life satisfaction |
| Indigenous vs non-Indigenous Australians | Lower satisfaction with finances, housing and health |

> **"Higher education is associated with lower reported life satisfaction"
> is the finding students most often mis-state.** It is an *association*
> between two measured variables, not a claim that education makes people
> unhappy. Plausible mechanisms run in several directions — higher
> expectations, different reference groups, correlated age and occupation
> effects. Report the association and name the limitation; do not upgrade
> it to a cause.

The submitted A1 report adds the ABS *Measuring What Matters* figure:
average overall life
satisfaction of **7.2 out of 10 in 2020, down from 7.5 in 2019** — a
0.3-point fall on a 0–10 scale in the first COVID year.

### The casual employment and education finding

Both Drilling Down 4 and Tutorial 4 set up a media claim — "higher
education leads to casual employment" — and answer it with HILDA (Wilkins,
2021, p. 70):

> "Higher educational attainment is negatively associated with casual
> employment, with the rate of casual employment highest for those who have
> not completed high school and lowest for those who have a bachelor's
> degree or higher … the rate of casual employment increased by 4.2
> percentage points for the 'less than high school completion' education
> group, 2.5 percentage points for the 'high-school completion' education
> group and 1.8 percentage points for the 'other post-school' group, while
> it **decreased by 0.9 percentage points** for the 'bachelor's degree or
> higher' group."

The gradient is monotonic and the direction reverses at the top:

```
less than high school completion   +4.2 pp
high-school completion             +2.5 pp
other post-school                  +1.8 pp
bachelor's degree or higher        −0.9 pp
```

> **Every figure in that quotation is in percentage points, and the source
> says so.** It is the cleanest example in the unit of why the distinction
> matters: a "4.2% increase" in a casual employment rate of, say, 30% is a
> rise to 31.3%, while a 4.2 percentage point increase is a rise to 34.2%.
> The unit quotes it correctly — make sure you do too. And note the
> methodological point the tutorial is really teaching: *"you may know
> plenty of people with degrees in casual positions, but this is anecdotal
> evidence and should not inform academic or professional discourse."*

### Other wellbeing sources

<details><summary>Questions</summary>

1. Services rose from 72.8% to 80.0% of production. Write the change two
   ways and say which belongs in a report.
2. Between 2009 and 2016 casual part-time work grew 13% and permanent
   part-time work grew 36%. What does this do to the "casualisation is
   rising" headline?
3. Why does ABS data show self-employment falling at the same time as gig
   work is visibly growing?
4. HILDA finds higher education associated with lower life satisfaction.
   What can and cannot be said about this?
5. There are 2.4 million casual employees, which is 19% of employees. What
   is the implied denominator, and why is it not total employment?
6. The Wilkins quotation gives "+4.2", "+2.5", "+1.8" and "−0.9". What is
   the unit of those numbers, and why does it matter?

</details>

<details><summary>Answers</summary>

1. ```
   +7.2 percentage points   (80.0 − 72.8)
   +9.9% in relative terms  (80.0 ÷ 72.8 − 1)
   ```
   **Percentage points** belongs in the report. The relative figure reads as
   a claim about output growth rather than about a share, and will be
   misunderstood.

2. It **undercuts it**. Permanent part-time grew about **2.8 times faster**
   than casual part-time. Part-time work is rising, but the growth is
   overwhelmingly in *permanent* part-time employment, not casual. Reporting
   only "part-time work is up" supports a conclusion the data contradicts.

3. Because of a **classification boundary**. The ABS classifies gig work as
   a form of **self-employment**, so a person moving from running a small
   business to platform work produces no change in the headline count. The
   *character* of self-employment is shifting without the *level* moving.
   The trend is real; the indicator cannot see it.

4. **Can say:** the two variables are negatively associated in the HILDA
   data. **Cannot say:** that education reduces life satisfaction. It is an
   observational association with plausible confounders — expectations,
   reference groups, age and occupation effects — and no causal design.

5. The implied denominator is **12.6 million employees** (2.4 ÷ 0.19). Total
   employment is about 14.7 million; the difference is **owner-managers**,
   who are employed but are not employees. Using total employment as the
   base would give about 16%, understating the casual share by roughly
   three percentage points.

6. **Percentage points.** It matters because a percentage-point change and a
   percentage change of a rate are different quantities. On a base casual
   rate of 30%, "+4.2 percentage points" means 34.2% while "+4.2%" means
   31.3% — a difference of nearly three points of the workforce.

</details>

## Summary

- Two structural warnings: **data lags social change**, and **definitions
  shift**.
- Services rose from **72.8% to 80.0%** of production (**+7.2 pp**);
  manufacturing is **5.7%** of output.
- Ageing: **65+ from 2.5m to 6.2m**, share **13% → 25%**; working-age per
  65+ falling **7.4 → 4.4 → 3.2**. Fiscal effect is on *growth rates*
  (−0.4% revenue, +0.3% spending), not levels.
- **Casualisation is largely a myth on ABS definitions.** Permanent
  part-time grew 36% against casual part-time's 13%. Multiple job holders
  rose 5.2% → 6.3% over 24 years.
- Gig work hides inside **self-employment**, so the indicator misses it.
- HILDA: life satisfaction stable and high, **U-shaped in age**, lowest for
  35–54; unemployment strongly negative; higher education *associated* with
  lower satisfaction.
- Watch **percentage points vs percent** everywhere in this topic. It is the
  single most common marks-losing error in the S section.
