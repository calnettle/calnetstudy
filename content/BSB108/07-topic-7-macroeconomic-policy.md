# TOPIC 7 — Macroeconomic Policy

Week 6. Fiscal and monetary policy: what they are, who owns them, how they
transmit to the economy, what happens when the cash rate hits zero, and how
they read against the real Australian data.

This is **A2 section 1** — an overview of the contemporary Australian
macroeconomic policy environment and its implications for the business
environment.

## 7.1 The personal trainer and the physiotherapist

The lecture's framing device runs through weeks 6, 7 and 8 and is worth
reproducing because it makes the A2 section boundary obvious.

| | **Macroeconomic policy** (section 1) | **Targeted / micro policy** (section 2) |
|---|---|---|
| Analogy | The **personal trainer** of the economy | The **physiotherapist** |
| Focus | Overall fitness — keeping the key vitals on track | A specific part of the economy or socio-economic problem needing correction |
| Targets | **GDP, unemployment, inflation** | Production and consumption activities within markets |
| Route to growth | **Directly** | **Indirectly** |
| Tools | Fiscal policy, monetary policy | Taxes and subsidies, property rights, laws and regulations, nudges |

Both aim at **economic growth**; they differ in whether they get there
directly or indirectly.

**The three shared objectives of macroeconomic policy:**

```
stable and positive economic growth
low unemployment
price stability
```

Every fiscal and monetary decision is justified against those three.

## 7.2 Macroeconomic policy and the business cycle

The policy job is to damp the cycle from Topic 3.

| Cycle position | Indicators | Policy direction |
|---|---|---|
| **Expansion / overheating** | GDP ↑, unemployment ↓, inflation ↑ | **Contractionary** — raise taxes / cut spending; raise the cash rate |
| **Contraction / slowdown** | GDP ↓, unemployment ↑, inflation ↓ | **Expansionary** — cut taxes / raise spending; cut the cash rate |

Tutorial 6 makes you fill this table in twice — once for fiscal, once for
monetary. The answers are symmetric, which is the point: fiscal and
monetary policy are supposed to **reinforce each other**.

## 7.3 Fiscal policy

**Definition:** the use of **federal** taxes and spending to achieve
**national macroeconomic objectives**.

The lecture's two exclusions are more examinable than the definition:

> **Not everything a government spends is fiscal policy.**
>
> - **State and local government spending is not fiscal policy**, because
>   it is not intended to impact the national economy.
> - **Not all federal tax and spending decisions are fiscal policy
>   actions.** Cutting taxes for citizens who take out health insurance is
>   a *health policy* action. Increasing defence spending to counter
>   terrorism or fund a war is *defence policy*.
>
> The test is **intent**: was the measure designed to move national GDP,
> unemployment or inflation? If it was designed to fix a specific problem,
> it is targeted policy and belongs in A2 section 2.

### Automatic stabilisers

Fiscal policy stabilises the cycle without anyone deciding anything.

```
EXPANDING ECONOMY (GDP increasing)
  automatic REDUCTION in govt spending    — unemployment payments ↓
  automatic INCREASE in govt revenue      — income taxation ↑
        → the budget tightens by itself, damping the boom

CONTRACTING ECONOMY (GDP decreasing)
  automatic INCREASE in govt spending     — unemployment payments ↑
  automatic DECREASE in govt revenue      — income taxation ↓
        → the budget loosens by itself, cushioning the downturn
```

> **Automatic stabilisers are counter-cyclical by construction, which means
> a deficit in a downturn is not evidence of a policy decision.** Part of
> every recession deficit is simply the stabilisers working — more benefit
> payments, less income tax. Distinguishing that from discretionary
> stimulus is exactly the cyclical/structural distinction below, and it is
> the difference between describing a budget and analysing one.

### Discretionary fiscal policy

| | **Expansionary** | **Contractionary** |
|---|---|---|
| Action | Increase government expenditure and/or **decrease** taxes | Decrease government expenditure and/or **increase** taxes |
| Effect | Stimulates activity — more spending on goods and services, more government investment, more household expenditure as tax falls | Slows activity — less spending on goods and services, less government investment, less household expenditure as tax rises |
| Australian examples | GFC response (the Rudd stimulus package); COVID-19 JobKeeper and JobSeeker | — |

### Cyclical vs structural spending

Drilling Down 6 introduces the distinction, and A2 section 1 is much
stronger with it:

- **Cyclical spending** responds to short-term fluctuations in the business
  cycle. In a downturn, government engages in cyclical spending to boost
  the economy — which implies expenditure exceeding revenue and a rising
  fiscal debt.
- **Structural spending** occurs **regardless of the state of the
  economy**. It contributes to improving societal wellbeing and underpins
  longer-term growth objectives — ageing population, climate change,
  workforce training.

> **A budget is a mixture, and calling it "expansionary" or
> "contractionary" without unpicking that mixture is where students lose
> marks.** Drilling Down 6's worked case is the 2024–25 Budget: post-COVID
> the economy had positive GDP growth, above-target inflation and
> historically low unemployment, which "would suggest that contractionary
> fiscal policy should be triggered". Instead the budget targeted cost-of-
> living relief, housing and Medicare — it "did have the look and feel of
> an expansionary budget", with the government "attempting to balance the
> fine line between feeding into inflation… whilst ensuring that the
> economic sentiment remains buoyant". The pack does **not** answer the
> question; it says commentators disagree and points you at *The Guardian*.
> Say that in A2 — a genuine disagreement, argued, beats a confident
> single answer.

### The COVID fiscal response and debt

The lecture puts the COVID fiscal response at approximately **$1,300,000,000,000**
— $1.3 trillion — and then asks how bad the resulting debt is. Three views:

| Body | View |
|---|---|
| **Government** | Stronger economic growth coupled with low costs of servicing debt will mean a steady and declining debt-to-GDP ratio over time; fiscal stance has been to return to surplus |
| **RBA** | Public sector debt remains low as a share of GDP; borrowing costs low; the economy is strong |
| **IMF** | Australia's institutions, policies and macroeconomic fundamentals are considered robust |

The lecture then adds a pointed aside: "didn't you cover some of that in P
and E of PEST?" — the IMF's judgement rests on the institutional strength
you described in A1.

Drilling Down 6 Task 3 sends you to an ABC piece with three framing
questions worth answering in A2: how big is Australia's debt relative to
other countries; what is a structural deficit and what is its solution; and
how can inflation be good for debt.

> **Inflation is good for debt because debt is nominal.** A fixed-dollar
> debt shrinks as a share of a nominal GDP that inflation is inflating. It
> is a real transfer from lenders to the borrower. That is the answer the
> Drilling Down question is fishing for, and it is worth stating explicitly
> because it explains why the debt-to-GDP ratio can fall without any debt
> being repaid.

## 7.4 Monetary policy

**Definition:** the use of monetary tools by the **Central Bank** to pursue
macroeconomic objectives.

**Who:** the **Reserve Bank of Australia** develops and implements monetary
policy. Its objectives are consistent with fiscal policy — stable and
positive growth, low unemployment, and **price stability achieved by
keeping CPI inflation within 2–3%**.

The lecture's closing line on the section: "Monetary policy and fiscal
policy need to reinforce each other."

### The instrument

The RBA's conventional instrument is the **cash rate target**, transmitted
through market interest rates to borrowing, spending and investment.

| | **Expansionary** | **Contractionary** |
|---|---|---|
| Action | **Reduce** the cash rate | **Increase** the cash rate |
| Purpose | Stimulate activity — more spending on goods and services, more investment | Control inflationary pressures in an overheating economy — slow spending and investment |
| Australian example | GFC response: 2008–2009 cash rate reduced from **7.25% to 3%** | Resource boom: 2001–2008 cash rate increased from **4.25% to 7.25%** |

Verified against the RBA's F1.1 monthly average cash rate target series:

```
September 2008   7.02%
April 2009       3.06%      → the GFC easing, confirmed
```

### The verified modern cycle

Every figure below is recomputed from the RBA F1.1 series (published
2 January 2026), monthly average of the daily cash rate target.

| Period | Cash rate | Note |
|---|---|---|
| Dec 2020 – Apr 2022 | **0.10%** | The lowest ever, held for 17 months |
| May 2022 | 0.33% | First rise |
| Dec 2023 – Jan 2025 | **4.35%** | Peak, held for **14 consecutive months** |
| Feb 2025 | 4.25% | First cut |
| Sep – Dec 2025 | **3.60%** | Held |

```
tightening:  0.10% → 4.35%  = +4.25 percentage points
easing:      4.35% → 3.60%  = −0.75 percentage points
```

Against the inflation series from Topic 3: headline CPI peaked at **7.8%
year-ended in the December quarter 2022**, and the cash rate peaked
**twelve months later** in December 2023. That gap is the transmission lag
made visible.

> **F1.1 is a *monthly average*, not the decision-day target — and the
> difference will make your chart look wrong.** The series shows May 2016 at
> **1.77%**, a number the RBA never set. It is a day-weighted blend of the
> 2.00% target that applied for the first three days and the 1.75% that
> applied for the rest:
>
> ```
> (3 × 2.00 + 28 × 1.75) ÷ 31 = 1.774   ✓ matches 1.77
> (2 × 1.75 + 29 × 1.50) ÷ 31 = 1.516   ✓ matches Aug 2016's 1.52
> (20 × 4.10 + 11 × 3.85) ÷ 31 = 4.011  ✓ matches May 2025's 4.01
> ```
>
> If you need the decisions themselves, use the RBA's cash rate decisions
> page, not F1.1. If you plot F1.1, describe it as the monthly average.

> **A second warning: the cash rate is not a mortgage rate.** The cash rate
> is the overnight interbank rate. What households and businesses pay
> depends on bank funding costs and margins, and pass-through is neither
> complete nor instant. "The cash rate rose 4.25 points so repayments rose
> 4.25 points" is not a claim the data supports.

### Transmission

The chain the lecture sets out, and the one A2 section 1 should walk
through:

```
cash rate → market interest rates → borrowing costs, deposit returns,
    asset prices, the exchange rate → household consumption and
    business investment → aggregate demand → GDP, employment, inflation
```

Note 04 measures the household-spending link on real ABS data. The
headline result, for year-ended growth in total household spending against
the cash rate over 121 months to November 2025:

| Lag | Full sample | Excluding COVID (Mar 2020 – Dec 2021) |
|---|---|---|
| 0 months | +0.111 | −0.064 |
| 6 months | −0.170 | −0.430 |
| 12 months | −0.321 | **−0.531** |

And discretionary spending is about twice as interest-sensitive as
non-discretionary (−0.546 vs −0.405 at 12 months, ex-COVID).

> **At zero lag the correlation has the wrong sign — which is why "the RBA
> raised rates and spending stayed strong" is not evidence that monetary
> policy failed.** The relationship only appears at a lag of six to twelve
> months. Any A2 claim about whether tightening "worked" has to specify the
> lag it is measuring over, or it is measuring nothing.

## 7.5 Unconventional monetary policy

**The problem:** as the cash rate approaches zero, conventional monetary
policy becomes less effective, because businesses and households become
less sensitive to further reductions. At **0.1%** — reached in November
2020 and held through April 2022 — the RBA needed other tools.

| Tool | What it did |
|---|---|
| **Policy interest rate** | Target for the cash rate at **0.1%**, the lowest ever |
| **Forward guidance** | A stance on monetary policy until a specific point in time — the RBA would not raise the cash rate target until actual inflation was **sustainably within the 2–3% target range** |
| **Asset purchases** | A programme of purchases to lower the whole structure of interest rates across the economy, and to re-affirm the RBA's commitment to keep rates low (quantitative easing) |
| **Adjustments to open market operations** | Increased the size of regular operations, ensuring ample liquidity for financial markets to function smoothly |
| **Term Funding Facility** | Low-cost borrowing for banks from the RBA at an interest rate of **0.1%** |
| **Interest rate on Exchange Settlement balances** | Set to **zero**, putting a **floor** on all interest rates in the economy |

> **Forward guidance is a promise, and a promise can be broken at a cost.**
> The RBA said it would not raise the cash rate until inflation was
> sustainably in the 2–3% band, with commentary at the time pointing to
> 2024. Inflation arrived early and the first rise came in **May 2022**.
> That was one driver of the subsequent RBA Review. The A2-worthy point is
> that forward guidance works only through credibility, so using it spends
> a resource that has to be rebuilt afterwards.

### The RBA Review

Tutorial 6 covers the outcome:

- **Why:** to consider the interaction between monetary and fiscal policy,
  and governance, culture and operations.
- **Key changes:** a monetary policy board reflecting greater diversity;
  policy meetings reduced from **11 to 8 a year** — less frequent, longer
  meetings giving the board more time to examine issues in detail and have
  deeper discussions on strategy, alternative options and risks.

## 7.6 When fiscal policy fights inflation

The lecture's final section: how fiscal policy can help reduce inflation
without simply cutting demand.

- **Targeted fiscal policy offering cost-of-living relief**
- **Smoothing the inequality impacts** of inflationary pressures — e.g. tax
  cuts
- **Action that restrains prices directly**, by reducing the cost of
  specific items and services:
  - Increasing the **childcare subsidy** → reduces the cost of childcare
  - Increasing **Commonwealth Rent Assistance** → reduces the cost of renting
  - Reducing **non-concessional co-payments** for prescribed medication →
    reduces the cost of pharmaceuticals
  - **Energy relief subsidies** → reduce the cost of electricity

> **These measures cut the *measured* CPI directly, which is not the same
> as reducing inflationary pressure.** An electricity rebate lowers the
> electricity component of the CPI basket in the quarter it applies, so
> headline inflation falls mechanically. Whether underlying inflationary
> pressure has fallen is a separate question — and it is precisely why the
> **trimmed mean** exists, since it strips out the extreme movements a
> subsidy creates. This is the cleanest link in the unit between Topic 3
> and Topic 7: use it. If headline falls and the trimmed mean does not, the
> subsidy moved the index, not the economy.

## 7.7 Corrections to a worked A2 attempt

The submitted A2 section 1 is a useful specimen, because most of it checks
out and
the parts that do not are instructive. Corrections, with the arithmetic:

**1. "GDP transitioned from an annual deficit of 2–3% to 0.7% by 2018-19."**
This describes the **budget balance**, not GDP. GDP does not run a deficit.
And the figure disagrees with the report's own Figure 2, which shows the
underlying cash balance at **0.0%** of GDP in 2018–19 and −0.5% in 2017–18.
Write "the underlying cash deficit narrowed from around 2–3% of GDP to
approximately balance by 2018–19".

**2. "An unexpected budget surplus of $15–20 billion" in 2022-23.**
The same report's Figure 2 shows the 2022–23 balance at **+0.9% of GDP**.
Use the report's own internal anchor to test that:

```
from the report:  2019-20 deficit = $85bn = 4.3% of GDP
                  → implied GDP 2019-20 = 85 ÷ 0.043 = $1,977bn

2022-23 nominal GDP is larger than 2019-20, so
  0.9% of GDP > 0.9% × 1,977 = $17.8bn — and materially so
```

A surplus of "$15–20 billion" cannot be reconciled with 0.9% of a
2022–23-sized economy; the two figures in the same report describe
different numbers. The accompanying research notes used **0.6% of GDP**, which
does give $15–20bn — so the text carries the note's figure while the chart
carries a different one. **Check the primary Budget papers and use one
figure consistently.**

**3. "The deficit reached $85 billion (4.3% GDP) in 2019-20 and $134
billion (6.5% GDP) in 2020-21."** The first pair reconciles cleanly at an
implied GDP of $1,977bn. The second implies $2,062bn. But the report's own
Figure 2 shows **−6.4%** for 2020–21, not 6.5%. A tenth of a point, but it
is the same number stated twice with two values.

**4. "2026: 3.60 → 3.85, Increasing, Inflation persistence."**
The RBA F1.1 dataset in the A2 folder was published 2 January 2026 and ends
at **3.60% for December 2025**. It cannot support a 3.85% figure. If that
came from a later RBA decision, cite the decision; if it is a forecast, say
so and attribute it.

**5. Figure 1 is titled "Australian household saving ratio, 2015-2021" but
its x-axis runs Sep-17 to Sep-25.** Neither endpoint matches the title.
Retitle to the actual plotted range.

> **The technique here is worth more than the corrections.** The report's
> $85bn/4.3% pair reconciles to an implied GDP of $1,977bn, which is a
> plausible figure for 2019–20. Because that pair reconciles exactly, it
> pins the units and the definition — and that is what proves the
> $15–20bn/0.9% pair cannot both be right. Find the parts that reconcile
> first; they become the ruler you measure the rest with.

## Summary

- Macro policy is the **personal trainer** — direct, aimed at GDP,
  unemployment and inflation. Targeted policy is the **physiotherapist**.
- Fiscal policy is **federal tax and spending intended to move the national
  economy**. Test by intent — state spending and health-motivated tax
  concessions are not fiscal policy.
- **Automatic stabilisers** move counter-cyclically without a decision;
  **discretionary** policy is the decision. Split **cyclical** from
  **structural** spending before labelling a budget.
- Monetary policy sits with the **RBA**, targeting **2–3% CPI**, using the
  **cash rate**.
- Verified cycle: **0.10% (Dec 2020 – Apr 2022) → 4.35% (Dec 2023 – Jan
  2025) → 3.60% (from Sep 2025)**. A **4.25 percentage point** tightening.
- F1.1 is a **monthly average**, not a decision. The cash rate is **not** a
  mortgage rate.
- At the zero lower bound the RBA used **forward guidance, asset purchases,
  expanded open market operations, the Term Funding Facility, and a zero
  rate on ES balances**.
- Cost-of-living subsidies lower **measured** CPI directly; the **trimmed
  mean** is how you tell whether pressure actually fell.
