# TOPIC 6 — Technology (the T in PEST)

Week 5. What "technology" means when an economist says it, the four
industrial revolutions, what is genuinely different about Industry 4.0, the
sharing economy, and the honest state of the evidence on AI and
productivity.

The scope for A1's **T**: how technology has shaped and is shaping the
business environment — with productivity as the through-line. Not a list of
gadgets.

## 6.1 Two definitions, and why the unit gives you both

### Ask an economist

> **Technology** is the process through which **inputs are transformed into
> output**. Inputs include physical inputs, physical capital, labour and
> human capital.
>
> **Technological change** is **increasing productivity** — the ability to
> produce **more output using the same inputs**.

That is a definition of a *process*, not of a *device*. A reorganised
production line with no new equipment is technological change if it raises
output per input.

The lecture adds the timing pattern: technological change is associated
with **short-term disruption and an expectation of longer-term benefits**.

### Ask a sociologist

- Technology drives social change.
- Technology itself is **neutral** — it depends on how society uses it.
- **Technological determinism**: the view that technology shapes society's
  structure and values. Examples with far-reaching societal impacts: the
  printing press, the internet.

> **Write the economist's definition first, then use it.** The most common
> failure in the T section is drifting into "here are some technologies
> dairy plants use". The assessment wants the business environment. If your
> paragraph does not contain the word productivity, output or inputs, it is
> probably drifting. The Canvas drafting page says it directly: "Resist the
> urge to default to describing technological advancements that will impact
> service delivery at the individual level/micro level. Your analysis needs
> to consider the fundamental shifts that technology will drive across the
> whole sector."

## 6.2 The four industrial revolutions

The lecture's framing is "change isn't a new thing" — each revolution
produced the same pattern of disruption then gain.

| | Broad character |
|---|---|
| **1st IR** | Mechanisation; steam and water power |
| **2nd IR** | Mass production; electricity; the assembly line |
| **3rd IR** | Electronics, computing, automation |
| **4th IR** | Cyber-physical systems, connectivity, data and AI |

### The distributional lesson from the 1st and 2nd

Over the 1st and 2nd IR period there were sustained increases in:

- **Real full-time earnings**, from higher labour productivity
- **Health and life expectancy**, from industrialised agriculture giving
  more sustainable food supplies, and improvements in sanitation and public
  health
- **Living standards**, as measured by height

But the lecture is blunt about who got them:

> Gains not equally distributed both **inter- and intra-generationally**
> (the modern post-code lottery) — spatial unemployment, and life
> expectancy: "in Liverpool in the 1860s, the life expectancy fell to **25
> years of age**."

And on the policy lag:

> Long road to the introduction of modern economic policy — transition from
> the poor-house model to modern social welfare took **200 years** from the
> 18th-century beginnings of the Industrial Revolution.

> **The 200-year policy lag is the most transferable fact in this topic.**
> It is the historical evidence for the claim you will make about AI in
> Topic 8: institutions and regulation move far slower than technology.
> Liverpool's life expectancy of 25 is what happens in the gap. If an exam
> question asks why regulating emerging technology is hard, this is your
> historical anchor.

Drilling Down 5 pairs this with GDP per capita since 1820 for Australia and
the UK (Bolt and van Zanden, 2020) and asks a question that connects
directly back to Topic 3: why must we be extremely careful using GDP per
capita as an indicator of economic and social wellbeing over time? The
answer is the Indigenous wellbeing case — 120 years of rising GDP per
capita alongside persistently poorer health, social and economic conditions
for Indigenous Australians. The Productivity Commission's Closing the Gap
dashboard is the current-data source.

## 6.3 Industry 4.0

### What is different this time

The lecture's six-point list:

- Occurring **simultaneously across every region**
- **Acceleration** of the rate of innovation, disruption and market
  penetration
- **Historically unprecedented returns to scale**
- **Changing role of capital vs labour**
- Heavy **cross-disciplinary integration** of new discoveries and technology
- **Unexpected new markets and ecosystems**

### The challenges

- Effect of **rising economic inequality**
- Effects of **deinstitutionalisation, democratisation and
  decentralisation**
- **Uncertainty** around the future of work and the future of economic
  growth

> **"Historically unprecedented returns to scale" is the mechanism behind
> the inequality challenge, and they belong in the same sentence.** When a
> platform's marginal cost of serving one more user is near zero, scale
> produces winner-take-most outcomes. That is why the same lecture series
> lists mega-corporations as a challenge to government authority (Topic 2)
> and rising inequality as a challenge of the 4th IR. One economic
> property, two consequences.

### Focus on 3D printing

**Additive manufacturing** — the process of joining materials to make parts
from three-dimensional model data, one layer at a time.

| Angle | Detail |
|---|---|
| **Productivity** | Faster production time; lower production cost by **30–65%**; less human intervention |
| **Sustainability** | Minimises waste — uses only the material required for the final component plus minor support structures; can use recycled materials |
| **Application** | Health and medicine, housing, space exploration, retail end goods, food |
| **Challenges** | IP and patent issues; printing of illicit goods |

```
a 30–65% cost reduction leaves cost at 70% to 35% of the original
```

> **A cost range that wide is a finding about uncertainty, not a
> measurement.** "30–65%" spans more than a two-to-one difference in the
> saving. Quote the range, not the midpoint, and do not write "roughly 50%"
> — that number appears nowhere in the source and implies a precision the
> evidence does not support.

## 6.4 Decentralisation and the sharing economy

**Definition:** a model of the economy based on a socio-economic system
built around the **sharing of resources** — covering sharing, collaborative
production, collaborative consumption, peer-to-peer, mesh, commons-based
peer production and access economy.

It is a **fundamental shift in the traditional market model**, which is
premised on private ownership.

**Five core features:**

1. Decentralised exchange
2. A focus on **access over ownership** of resources
3. Firms as **facilitators of exchange** rather than producers
4. Self-governance
5. Value seen as not purely financial, but also **social and
   environmental**

**Where the gains come from:** increased access to **idle and underutilised
resources**, enabled by dividing goods and services into smaller parts in
both **time and space**.

**Threat to the sharing economy**, per the lecture: **excessive
regulation**.

> **Underutilisation is the whole value proposition, and it is what
> Drilling Down 5 makes you answer first.** A car parked 95% of the time is
> a stranded asset. The platform's contribution is not the car and not the
> transaction — it is the reduction in the search cost of matching an idle
> asset to someone who wants it for an hour. If your explanation of the
> sharing economy does not mention idle capacity, you have described a
> marketplace, not the sharing economy.

Note the tension the unit sets up deliberately: Topic 2 lists digital
decentralisation as a **challenge to government authority**, Topic 6 lists
excessive regulation as a **threat to the sharing economy**, and Topic 8
asks whether regulatory frameworks can keep up. That is one argument seen
from three sides — good material for an evaluative paragraph.

### Sharing finance

The lecture extends the model into capital markets: "it's not just about
cheap eats and dog-sitting".

| Model | What it does |
|---|---|
| **Crowdfunding / crowd-sourced funding** | Alternative route to access capital; democratises finance; provides validation — builds a community of champions and ambassadors who will share the business |
| **Marketplace / peer-to-peer lending** | Online platforms connect borrowers with investors willing to offer loans, **bypassing financial intermediaries** |

For the focus sector, the submitted A1 report makes the right connection:
crowd-sourced
funding (regulated by ASIC) lowers the capital barrier for small
entrants launching niche products such as health-focused cultured
products — which is a *business environment* point, because it changes who
can enter the market.

## 6.5 AI and the Australian business environment

The lecture treats AI as a technological revolution with the same
characteristics as previous ones, but with one distinguishing feature:
**automation of complex productive processes which were previously
invulnerable to previous waves of automation**.

### The opportunity framing

Australia's productivity problem is the reason the unit cares:

- Skills and labour gaps — AI can **augment labour** in skilled and
  knowledge work, open new opportunities for automation in part or full,
  and enable smarter use of a limited workforce.
- Productivity has **slowed across all advanced economies**, with the
  service sector particularly challenging.

Drilling Down 5 adds the supply-side argument explicitly: given the ageing
population and declining fertility rates covered in Topic 5, labour is a
constrained resource, so technology that **releases labour to other areas
of the economy** is a potential positive rather than simply a threat.

### Current state of adoption

| Question | Answer from the source |
|---|---|
| How much AI adoption? | **1% (ABS) to 44% (CSIRO)** have deployed AI into their operations |
| What kind of uptake? | **Passive** — "by default", via updates to existing software and outsourced services |
| Risk profile? | **Low risk** — human decision-making assisted by AI, a co-pilot model |
| Pattern? | Consistent with the technology **S-curve**: slowly building, then a sharp uptick |

> **A range from 1% to 44% is a 44-fold spread, and that *is* the finding.**
> Two credible national bodies measuring the same thing and differing by a
> factor of forty-four means they are not measuring the same thing. It is
> almost certainly definitional — does "deployed AI" mean a bespoke model
> in production, or a spreadsheet that got an AI feature in an update? This
> is Topic 4's consistency check applied to a live example: quote both
> figures, name both sources, and say the divergence is definitional. Never
> pick the one that suits your argument.

The submitted A1 report adds a firm-size gradient from the Department of
Industry,
Science and Resources: AI adoption at **82% of firms with 200–500
employees** against **33% of micro businesses**. And from the Australian
Industry Group: only **23% of businesses** named technology adoption a
leading focus for 2025, and only **4%** ranked AI a top priority.

> **The size gradient is the competition story.** If large firms adopt at
> 82% and micro businesses at 33%, AI widens the gap between firms that can
> invest in data, skills and systems and firms that cannot. In a sector
> where the top four already hold 51.9% of revenue and small players are
> exiting, that points toward further concentration. That is a
> business-environment argument, which is what the T section is for.

### The evidence on productivity

Specific studies the lecture cites:

- Software engineers coded **2× faster** using an AI-based tool
- Professional writing tasks completed **significantly faster**
- Call centre operators became **14% more productive** using AI

And the overall verdict:

> "Overall there is **scant empirical evidence** that AI uptake causes an
> increase in firm productivity" — early days.

> **Three positive case studies and a null overall finding is not a
> contradiction — it is the shape of early evidence.** The case studies are
> narrow tasks in specific settings; the overall claim is about
> **firm-level** productivity, which requires the task gain to survive
> reorganisation, training, error correction and management overhead. A
> report that quotes only the 2× figure is misleading. A report that quotes
> only "scant evidence" is incomplete. Quote both and explain why they
> differ — that is the evaluative move the rubric rewards.

Drilling Down 5 adds a warning about the literature itself: there is a lack
of consensus on employment impacts from Industry 4.0 disruption, explained
by "differences in methodology and focus as well as **author bias**". And
on tone:

> "The language around AI and employment is emotive… As a student writing a
> professional report, your analysis of AI on employment in the business
> environment needs to consider it objectively."

## 6.6 Technology and employment

The recurring fear since the dawn of the industrial age is that
technological change spawns mass unemployment. The unit's position, stated
in Drilling Down 5: "when technology destroys jobs people do find other
jobs".

The mechanism is **creative destruction** — new technologies displace some
jobs and business models in the short run while enabling new industries,
roles and productivity gains over the longer run (OECD).

> **"Historically, net job creation has outweighed the decline" is a
> statement about aggregates, and the aggregate is not the experience.**
> Topic 6's own 1st-IR material is the counter-example: real earnings rose
> nationally while life expectancy in Liverpool fell to 25. The honest
> formulation is that technological change has historically raised total
> employment and output *while* imposing concentrated, durable losses on
> particular workers and places. Both halves of that sentence are needed.
>
> An earlier draft of the submitted A1 report contained the sentence *"The
common thought
> is that new technology typically causes mass unemployment, which is
> true"* immediately before explaining that it is not — a self-contradiction
> caught and removed before submission. Read your own transitions.

Drilling Down 5 sends you to McKinsey for which sectors have the highest
automation potential and which skills are predicted to matter most, and to
Shet and Pereira (2021) for the managerial and organisational competencies
needed to navigate Industry 4.0 — then asks you to reflect on whether your
degree is developing them.

## 6.7 The sector angle

For butter and dairy product manufacturing, the T section should run
through productivity, because that is where the sector's problem is: a
**4.2% profit margin**, below the sector average, with **purchases at
72.3% of revenue**.

| Technology | Business-environment effect |
|---|---|
| Automation and process analytics | Reduce downtime; improve real-time quality monitoring; optimise energy and water use — all material in a utility-intensive, regulated food plant |
| Depreciation as a share of revenue rising | IBISWorld attributes this directly to growing investment in automation and expansion — the adoption is visible in the cost structure |
| Renewable energy in processing | Named for Saputo; reduces carbon footprint and links to the sustainability expectations in Topic 5 |
| Crowd-sourced funding | Lowers capital barriers for niche entrants |
| AI adoption skewed to large firms | Reinforces concentration in a sector already at CR4 = 51.9% |

> **The strongest T-section evidence is in the cost structure, not in a
> press release.** IBISWorld reports depreciation rising as a share of
> industry revenue and attributes it to automation investment and factory
> consolidation. That is technology adoption showing up in audited
> financial data rather than in marketing claims — a far better source than
> a vendor case study, on every one of the five evaluation criteria.

<details><summary>Questions</summary>

1. Give the economist's definition of technology and of technological
   change.
2. A dairy plant reorganises its production schedule and raises output 8%
   with the same staff and equipment. Is this technological change?
3. ABS says 1% of businesses have deployed AI; CSIRO says 44%. How should
   this appear in a report?
4. Software engineers coded twice as fast with an AI tool, but the
   Productivity Commission finds "scant empirical evidence" that AI raises
   firm productivity. Reconcile these.
5. What single property makes idle capacity valuable in the sharing
   economy, and why is a platform necessary?
6. Why does the 200-year lag from the Industrial Revolution to modern
   social welfare matter for a report on AI?

</details>

<details><summary>Answers</summary>

1. **Technology** is the process through which inputs — physical inputs,
   physical capital, labour, human capital — are transformed into output.
   **Technological change** is increasing productivity: producing **more
   output using the same inputs**.

2. **Yes.** The definition is about the input-to-output transformation
   process, not about equipment. More output from the same inputs is
   technological change by definition. This is exactly why the economist's
   definition is given first.

3. Quote **both**, attribute **both**, and state that the **44-fold
   divergence is almost certainly definitional** — the two bodies are not
   measuring the same thing by "deployed AI". Selecting whichever figure
   suits the argument fails the objectivity criterion.

4. They measure different things at different levels. The 2× figure is a
   **task-level** gain in a narrow, well-suited application. The
   Productivity Commission's finding is about **firm-level** productivity,
   which requires the task gain to survive reorganisation, retraining,
   error correction and management overhead. Early-stage evidence typically
   shows exactly this pattern: real micro gains, no measurable macro
   effect yet.

5. **Underutilisation.** An asset idle most of the time is stranded
   capacity. The platform's contribution is reducing the **search and
   matching cost** of connecting that idle capacity to someone who wants it
   briefly, and enabling the good to be divided into smaller parts in time
   and space.

6. Because it is the historical evidence that **institutions and regulation
   move far slower than technology**, and that the gap has real costs —
   Liverpool's life expectancy falling to 25 in the 1860s. It is the
   strongest available anchor for the argument that AI regulation is
   currently lagging its deployment.

</details>

## Summary

- **Technology** = the input-to-output transformation process.
  **Technological change** = more output from the same inputs. It is about
  productivity, not gadgets.
- Sociologically, technology is **neutral**; **technological determinism**
  is the claim that it shapes society regardless.
- The 1st and 2nd IRs raised earnings, health and living standards, but
  distributed the gains unevenly, and policy took **200 years** to catch up.
- **Industry 4.0** differs in simultaneity, speed, returns to scale, the
  capital/labour balance, cross-disciplinary integration and unexpected
  markets.
- **3D printing**: 30–65% lower production cost; waste minimisation; IP and
  illicit-goods challenges.
- **Sharing economy**: access over ownership, firms as facilitators, value
  from **idle and underutilised resources**. Threatened by excessive
  regulation.
- **AI adoption is 1% (ABS) to 44% (CSIRO)** — a definitional divergence,
  not a data point. Adoption skews to large firms (82% vs 33%).
- Task-level AI gains are real (2× coding, 14% call centre); firm-level
  productivity evidence is **scant**. Report both.
