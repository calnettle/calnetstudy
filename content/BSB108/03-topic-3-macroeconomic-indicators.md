# TOPIC 3 — Macroeconomic Indicators (the E in PEST)

Week 3. The three indicators BSB108 assesses you on — GDP, unemployment
and inflation — what each one actually measures, what it misses, and how
they move together through the business cycle.

The scope for A1's **E** is deliberately tight: **GDP, unemployment,
inflation. Nothing else.** Not exchange rates, not international trade,
not policy, not the firm.

## 3.1 What a macroeconomic indicator is

Indicators are numerical measures derived from statistical data collected
by government and relevant agencies. They align to the three
macroeconomic goals:

```
GOAL                    INDICATOR
economic growth    ←→   GDP
high employment    ←→   unemployment rate
price stability    ←→   inflation (CPI)
```

Sources the unit expects you to use: the **ABS** (key indicators page),
the **RBA**, and the **OECD** for international comparison.

## 3.2 Gross Domestic Product

**Definition:** GDP measures the **market value of all domestic final
goods and services**.

Three exclusions built into that sentence:

- Only **new** goods or services purchased by the **final user**
- **Not** intermediate goods or services
- **Not** the value of used (second-hand) goods

> **The intermediate-goods exclusion is a double-counting rule.** If a
> processor buys $1 of raw milk and sells $2 of butter, GDP records $2 —
> not $3. Counting the milk separately would count it twice, once on its
> own and once inside the butter. This is why a sector's *revenue* is not
> its *contribution to GDP*: revenue includes bought-in inputs, value added
> does not. The IBISWorld report gives both for the focus sector — revenue
> $8,520.4m and industry value added $1,261.0m in 2024–25. The GDP
> contribution is the second figure, about **14.8%** of revenue.

### Nominal vs real

```
nominal GDP = output valued at current prices
real GDP    = output valued at constant prices (base year)
```

- Nominal GDP captures **both** rising prices and rising output.
- Real GDP holds prices constant, so it captures **output only**.
- When prices are rising, **real GDP will be lower than nominal GDP**.
- Only real GDP can be meaningfully compared with previous years.

> **"Adjusted for inflation" and "chain volume measure" both mean real.**
> The ABS publishes GDP as "chain volume measures, seasonally adjusted",
> which is the real series and the one the Week 4 exercise tells you to
> download. If you compare a nominal figure from one year with a real
> figure from another, the difference you report is partly pure price
> change and the comparison is meaningless.

### What GDP does not measure

The lecture's list:

| Missing | Why it matters |
|---|---|
| **Distribution** | GDP per capita helps, but an average per person says nothing about who has it |
| **Value of leisure** | Working longer hours raises GDP and lowers wellbeing |
| **Level, quality and access to health care and education** | Not captured |
| **Pollution and negative effects of production** | Not deducted |
| **Crime and other social problems** | Not adjusted for |

Drilling Down 5 pushes this further with the Indigenous wellbeing case:
Australia has had 120 years of rising GDP per capita while Indigenous
populations have experienced persistently poorer health, social and
economic conditions. GDP per capita is an average that can rise while a
group's position does not.

### Alternative rankings

The lecture's comparison table (data 1 December 2025) shows how differently
countries rank depending on the measure:

| Measure | Source | Australia's rank |
|---|---|---|
| GDP (current US$) | World Bank, 2024 | 13 |

Finland tops happiness, Iceland tops HDI, the USA tops GDP, Liechtenstein

### The shadow economy

The lecture flags that GDP is incomplete in a second way.

- **No universally agreed definition** — underground, non-observed, hidden,
  cash or informal economy.
- **No universally agreed measurement** — OECD estimates range from under
  1% of GDP in some countries to over 20% in others.
- How the ABS handles it:
  - Household production — a minor adjustment for "backyard" production
  - Informal economy production — ABS does not consider it significant
  - Illegal drug economy — ABS estimates **0.4% of GDP**, and this is
    **not included** in the published figures
- Emerging challenges: the shared economy, the gig economy,
  cryptocurrencies

> **A 20-fold range in the estimate is the finding, not a footnote.** If
> the shadow economy is somewhere between under 1% and over 20% of GDP
> depending on the country, then cross-country GDP comparisons carry an
> error term of that size. It is a currency-and-consistency problem, not a
> rounding problem.

## 3.3 Unemployment

### Who counts

The labour force framework matters more than the headline number:

```
                    working-age population
                   /                      \
            LABOUR FORCE              NOT IN LABOUR FORCE
           /            \             (retired, studying,
      employed      unemployed         discouraged, caring)
                    (not working,
                     available,
                     actively
                     looking)
```

```
unemployment rate  = unemployed ÷ labour force × 100
participation rate = labour force ÷ working-age population × 100
employment-to-population ratio = employed ÷ working-age population × 100
```

> **The unemployment rate's denominator is the labour force, not the
> population.** This is the single most exploitable feature of the
> statistic. If discouraged workers stop looking, they leave the labour
> force entirely: the numerator falls, the denominator falls, and the
> unemployment rate *improves* while fewer people are working. The
> employment-to-population ratio and the participation rate are the two
> figures that catch this, which is why the ABS publishes all three
> together. Quote at least two of them.

### The lecture's snapshot

| Indicator | Value |
|---|---|
| Unemployment rate | 4.4% (unchanged) |
| Participation rate | 67.0% (increased) |
| Employment | 14,678,400 (increased) |
| Employment-to-population ratio | 64.0% (unchanged) |
| Underemployment rate | 5.8% (unchanged) |
| Monthly hours worked | 1,993 million (increased) |

> data was retrieved December 2025; the bullet immediately under it reads
> "Key statistics (**trend June 2025**)". Those are two different reference
> periods six months apart. Do not cite a number whose reference period you
> cannot pin down — go to the ABS *Labour Force, Australia* release, read
> the reference month off the release itself, and cite that. (The submitted
A1 report used
> the seasonally adjusted rate of 4.3% for November 2025, from the release
> rather than the slide — that is the right move.)

Also note this snapshot is described as **trend**, while the Week 4
exercise builds a chart from the **seasonally adjusted** series. Trend and
seasonally adjusted are different series and will give different numbers
for the same month.

### What the headline rate hides

- **Regional unemployment rates** can be significantly higher than the
  national rate.
- **Youth unemployment (15–24)** was 9.8% in October 2025 against a
  national 4.4% — **2.2 times** the headline rate.
- **Underemployment** (5.8%) captures people working fewer hours than they
  want. It is larger than the unemployment rate itself.

### Types of unemployment

| Type | Cause |
|---|---|
| **Frictional** | Normal labour turnover, including seasonal unemployment |
| **Structural** | Changes in technology and the structure of the economy |
| **Cyclical** | Fluctuations in economic activity — booms and recessions |

**Full employment** occurs when all unemployment is frictional and
structural — i.e. **the absence of cyclical unemployment**. It is not zero
unemployment.

**NAIRU** — the non-accelerating inflation rate of unemployment — is the
lowest level of unemployment before inflation starts taking off. The ABS
estimates it at **around 5%**; a Treasury working paper puts it at
**4.5% to 5%**.

> **At 4.4%, the reported unemployment rate sits below both NAIRU
> estimates.** That is not a data error — it is the interesting finding,
> and it is the correct thing to say in the E section: the labour market is
> tighter than the estimated non-inflationary rate, which is consistent
> with the persistent above-target inflation reported alongside it. You can
> say that in A1 because it is a statement about the *indicators*. The
> moment you say what the RBA should do about it, you are in A2.

## 3.4 Inflation

**Definition:** a measure of the rate of change (%) of the price level of
goods and services that households buy — the average price of the basket
of goods and services in the **Consumer Price Index**.

```
inflation rate = (CPI_t − CPI_{t−1}) ÷ CPI_{t−1} × 100
```

Note the definition: inflation is a rate of change of a *level*. Falling
inflation means prices are still rising, just more slowly. Only **negative**
inflation (deflation) means prices are falling.

### The sweet spot

| Regime | Rate | Consequences |
|---|---|---|
| **Deflation** | negative | Lower economic growth; deflation spiral — firms respond to lower prices by slowing production and cutting salaries, which reduces consumer spending further |
| **Low and stable** | ~2–3% | Contributes to sustainable economic growth. The RBA's target band |
| **High** | ~10%+ | Reduces the real value of money; increases uncertainty and costs; wage inflation; reduces the real rate of return on investment. Can be demand-pull or cost-push |
| **Hyperinflation** | extreme | Extremely rapid increases in the general price level |

### Headline vs underlying

The lecture's snapshot (data 1 December 2025):

- **Inflation rate 3.8%** in the 12 months to October 2025
- **Trimmed mean annual inflation 3.3%**, up from 3.2% in the 12 months to
  September 2025
- Largest annual price rises: **Housing +5.9%**, **Recreation and culture
  +3.2%**, **Food and non-alcoholic beverages +3.2%**

The trimmed mean "provides a view of underlying inflation by reducing the
effect of irregular or temporary price changes that can impact the CPI".
The ABS constructs it by ordering all CPI components by their price change
in the quarter and taking the expenditure-weighted average of the **middle
70 per cent**.

> **The lecture's 3.8% / 3.3% is the monthly CPI indicator, not the
> quarterly CPI — and mixing the two will contradict your own report.**
> The RBA's G1 series (published 30 October 2025) gives for the **September
> quarter 2025**: headline year-ended **3.2%** and trimmed mean **3.0%**.
> The lecture's figures are for the 12 months to **October 2025** from the
> monthly indicator. Both are correct; they are different series with
> different coverage and different reference periods. Say which one you are
> quoting.

### Verified inflation history

Recomputed from the RBA's G1 Consumer Price Inflation series, year-ended
percentage change:

| Quarter | Headline | Trimmed mean |
|---|---|---|
| Jun 2020 | −0.3% | 1.2% |
| Jun 2021 | 3.8% | 1.6% |
| Mar 2022 | 5.1% | 3.8% |
| Sep 2022 | 7.3% | 6.1% |
| **Dec 2022** | **7.8%** | **6.8%** |
| Dec 2023 | 4.1% | 4.2% |
| Dec 2024 | 2.4% | 3.3% |
| Jun 2025 | 2.1% | 2.7% |
| Sep 2025 | 3.2% | 3.0% |

The peak in both series is the **December quarter 2022**: headline 7.8%,
trimmed mean 6.8%.

Two things to read off that table:

1. **The June 2020 headline is negative (−0.3%) while the trimmed mean is
   positive (1.2%).** Free childcare and collapsing fuel prices dragged the
   headline below zero; underlying inflation never went negative. That gap
   is exactly what the trimmed mean exists to reveal.
2. **In December 2023 the trimmed mean (4.2%) exceeded the headline
   (4.1%)**, and it stayed above it through 2024. Underlying inflation was
   more persistent than the headline suggested on the way down, just as it
   was more moderate on the way up.

> **A longer view changes the story, and so does the method.** The lecture
> shows a century of Australian inflation and notes that "how we compute
> the CPI has changed". The RBA's own notes confirm it: before September
> quarter 1948 the series is the 'C Series' Retail Price Index with
> consumption weights not updated after 1936, and there are classification
> breaks in the tradables/non-tradables split at September 2011 and
> December 2016. A single long line chart of "Australian inflation since
> 1922" is not one consistent measurement. If you plot it, say so.

## 3.5 The business cycle

| Expansion (upswing) | Contraction (downswing) |
|---|---|
| Strong growth — **GDP increasing** | Growth slows — **GDP falling** |
| Employment increasing — **unemployment falling** | Employment decreasing — **unemployment increasing** |
| **Inflation increasing** — capacity constraints bite, wage growth | **Inflation lowering** — spare capacity in the economy |

The reference line on the diagram is **potential output** — what the
economy can produce with full use of its resources. Expansion pushes actual
output toward and past potential; contraction opens a gap below it.

> **The three indicators are not independent and the exam expects you to
> say so.** They move together in a predictable pattern, which is why you
> can diagnose the cycle position from any two of them. Falling
> unemployment with rising inflation is late expansion. Rising unemployment
> with falling inflation is contraction. Rising unemployment *and* rising
> inflation is the one combination the simple model does not produce — and
> it has a name, stagflation, which is why it is worth flagging when you
> see it.

### Cyclical vs counter-cyclical sectors

One of the A1 framing prompts asks whether the focus sector is cyclical or
counter-cyclical. The test is how demand for its output responds to the
cycle.

- **Cyclical** — demand rises in expansion and falls in contraction.
  Discretionary goods and services.
- **Counter-cyclical** — demand holds up or rises in contraction. Staples
  and inferior goods.

Butter and dairy manufacturing is split across both. Note 04 quantifies
this from ABS data: over the decade to November 2025, year-ended growth in
**discretionary** household spending was about **twice as sensitive** to
the cash rate as non-discretionary spending, and its standard deviation
was about twice as large (9.6 percentage points versus 4.9).

The mapping onto the sector's own product lines:

| Sector segment | Likely behaviour |
|---|---|
| Basic butter, private-label | More defensive; consumers trade down *into* it |
| Yoghurt and cultured products | Mixed — staples plus premium lines |
| Dairy proteins | More discretionary; supplements are a cut-back category |
| Flavoured milk, dairy desserts | More discretionary |

<details><summary>Answers</summary>

1. Its contribution is its **value added**, not its revenue. Raw milk is an
   **intermediate good** — its value is already inside the value of the
   butter. Counting both would double-count. On the IBISWorld figures for
   2024–25 the sector's industry value added was **$1,261.0m** against
   revenue of **$8,520.4m**, so value added is about **14.8%** of revenue:

   ```
   1,261.0 ÷ 8,520.4 = 0.14800 → 14.8%
   ```

2. **Not necessarily, and the numbers suggest not.** Both the numerator and
   the denominator of the unemployment rate can fall together when people
   stop looking for work and leave the labour force. A falling
   participation rate alongside a falling unemployment rate is the classic
   discouraged-worker signature. Check the **employment-to-population
   ratio** and total employment before concluding anything.

3. Because **frictional** unemployment (normal turnover, including
   seasonal) and **structural** unemployment (technological and structural
   change) always exist. Full employment means the absence of **cyclical**
   unemployment only.

4. That **1.0 percentage point of the headline rate is coming from
   irregular or temporary price changes** in the extreme tails of the
   distribution — the components the trimmed mean strips out by keeping
   only the middle 70% of price changes by expenditure weight. Underlying
   inflation is 6.8%; the extra point is volatile and may not persist.

5. **No.** Inflation is the *rate of change* of the price level. At 3.2%
   prices are still rising, just more slowly than at 7.8%. Prices only fall
   when inflation is **negative** — deflation.

6. **A1:** report that the unemployment rate sits at or below the estimated
   non-accelerating inflation rate of unemployment, which is consistent
   with the above-target inflation reported alongside it. That is a
   statement about the indicators. **A2:** any sentence about what the RBA
   or the Treasurer should therefore do — that is macroeconomic policy.

## Summary

- **GDP** = market value of all domestic **final** goods and services.
  Excludes intermediate and second-hand goods. **Real** for comparison over
  time; real < nominal when prices rise.
- GDP misses **distribution, leisure, health, education, pollution and
  crime**; the **shadow economy** adds a measurement error of anywhere from
  under 1% to over 20% of GDP internationally.
- **Unemployment rate** = unemployed ÷ **labour force**. Read it alongside
  **participation** and **employment-to-population** or you will misread
  discouraged workers as an improvement.
- **Full employment** = frictional + structural only. **NAIRU** ≈ 4.5–5%.
- **Inflation** = rate of change of the price level. RBA target **2–3% on a
  medium-term average**. **Trimmed mean** = middle 70% of price changes by
  expenditure weight.
- Verified peak: **7.8% headline, 6.8% trimmed mean, December quarter
  2022.**
- **Business cycle**: expansion = GDP ↑, unemployment ↓, inflation ↑;
  contraction is the reverse.
