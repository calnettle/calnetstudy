# TOPIC 2 — Institutional Framework (the P in PEST)

Week 2. The legal, political and financial institutions that make an
Australian business environment work: property rights and the rule of law,
the economic case for government, how business pushes back on government,
peak bodies, market and financial regulators, and social licence to
operate.

This is the material for the **P** in A1. It is about *institutions*, not
about *policy*.

## 2.1 Why any institutions at all?

The lecture gives three reasons a country needs legal, political and
financial institutions:

1. **To enforce the laws of the country** — particularly protecting private
   property rights and enforcing contracts.
2. **To ensure a regulatory framework** that facilitates activity in the
   business environment in a way that maximises economic and social welfare.
3. **To maintain a well-functioning and stable financial system.**

Every institution in this topic maps back to one of those three.

## 2.2 Property rights

A property right has three components:

```
PROPERTY RIGHT = legal ownership of a resource
               + the right to earn income from the good
               + the right to transfer the good to others
```

All three matter. Ownership without the right to earn income is not much
of a right; ownership without the right to transfer is not tradeable, and
so cannot support a market.

The lecture's punchline: in practice this means "essentially nothing
unless property rights are **enforceable** (or secured in law)". A right
you cannot enforce is a hope.

The tutorial's working definition adds scope:

> Property rights scores measure the degree to which a country's laws
> protect private property rights, and the degree to which its government
> enforces those laws… 'Property' does include land and fixtures or
> structures upon the land — but extends beyond that to include all
> tangible personal property and intangible property [such as] copyright,
> intellectual property, shares in a corporation.

The chain the unit wants you to be able to state:

```
enforceable property rights → ability to contract → exchange
    → investment → a well-functioning economy → prosperity
```

### The data task

Drilling Down 2 Task 1 sends you to the World Bank's Prosperity Data360
property rights indicator (WEF GCI 4.0) to compare Australia, Germany,
USA, China, Brazil, India, DR Congo and Zimbabwe. Drilling Down 7 Task 5
sends you to the Heritage Foundation's Index of Economic Freedom for the
**business freedom** score against Australia, China, Germany, US, Canada,
NZ and UK.

> **The Drilling Down pack warns the World Bank interface is glitchy** — if
> the ranking looks wrong it is sorting by rank position rather than
> score. Toggle between score and rank a few times to fix it. Worth knowing
> because a mis-sorted chart pasted into a report is a marked error, not a
> tool problem.
>
> A second, deeper point: these are **composite index scores built from
> survey and expert judgement**, not measured quantities. They are ordinal
> at best. Saying "Australia scores 8.1 and Germany 7.9, so Australia's
> property rights are 2.5% stronger" is a misuse of the data. You can say
> Australia ranks higher; you cannot treat the gap as a ratio.

### Indigenous Cultural and Intellectual Property (ICIP)

The lecture treats ICIP as the place where the Western property-rights
model breaks down. ICIP is part of Indigenous peoples' cultural heritage
and includes:

- Moveable cultural property
- All literary and artistic works — music, dance, song, ceremonies,
  symbols and designs, narratives and poetry
- Scientific, agricultural, technical and ecological knowledge
- Human remains
- Sacred sites, burials and sites of historical significance
- Documents of Indigenous peoples' heritage — film, photographs, video and
  audio recordings, archival collections

The incompatibility the lecture identifies:

| Western IP system | Indigenous cultural systems |
|---|---|
| Built on **individual ownership** | Communal rights and responsibilities |
| Promotes **commercialisation and commodification** | Knowledge held in custodianship |
| Fixed term, then public domain | Obligations that do not expire |
| Requires an identifiable author and date | Knowledge transmitted across generations |

Two named failure modes: **fake Aboriginal art**, and
**bioprospecting/biopiracy** — patenting Indigenous knowledge of plants and
medicines for profit.

The Australian Government has committed to introduce **stand-alone
legislation** to protect First Nations traditional knowledge and cultural
expressions, including addressing the harm caused by fake art, merchandise
and souvenirs.

> **ICIP is a P-in-PEST issue for a dairy manufacturer even though dairy is
> not an art form.** The exposure is in branding: names, imagery, motifs
> and "native ingredient" claims. Standalone ICIP legislation would change
> what a food manufacturer can put on a label. That is a property-rights
> and institutions point, so it belongs in A1's P — not in A2.

## 2.3 The case for government

The lecture's list of what government is *for*, economically:

| Function | What it means |
|---|---|
| Legal system and the rule of law | Enforceable contracts and property |
| Maintaining or enforcing competition | Preventing market power abuse |
| **Correcting market failure** | Externalities, common resources, public goods, merit goods, asymmetric information |
| Equity | Income equalisation |
| Stabilisation | Macroeconomic policy |

The five market failures named in Week 2 are expanded and defined properly
in Topic 8 — Week 2 only names them. The last two functions (equity and
stabilisation) are the bridge to A2.

### How government influences the environment

The lecture splits the toolkit two ways — and flags it as "a spoiler for
A2, not A1":

```
MACRO POLICY                        MICRO POLICY
  Fiscal policy                       Traditional tools
    government spending                 legislation
    and taxation (income)               regulation
  Monetary policy                       taxes and subsidies
    interest rates                      information
                                      Behavioural tools
                                        social marketing
                                        behavioural economics
```

> **Do not write this table into A1.** The lecture puts it in Week 2
> because the institutions and the tools are hard to separate in the
> abstract — but the assessment separates them absolutely. Naming the
> Treasury and the RBA as institutions is A1. Describing what fiscal and
> monetary policy *do* is A2 section 1.

## 2.4 The tricky business of government

Government is not a neutral optimiser. The lecture introduces the
**political marketplace**:

- **Rent-seeking**: individuals and groups lobby government for
  regulations, policies and outcomes that benefit them — "seeking economic
  rents at the expense of the general public and economic efficiency".
- **Voters** vote for the candidate that gives themselves the greatest
  benefit.
- **Individuals and corporations** seek influence through political
  donations, lobby groups, trade unions, action groups and peak bodies.
- **Politicians** are self-interested, utility-maximising individuals.

Checks and balances named: a **lobby group register** and **disclosure on
political donations**.

> **Rent-seeking is not the same as lobbying.** Lobbying is the activity;
> rent-seeking is the *objective* — capturing value through the political
> process rather than by creating it. A peak body arguing for clearer
> labelling standards is lobbying. A peak body arguing for an import
> restriction that raises its members' prices is rent-seeking. In an exam
> answer, say which one you mean and why.

### Challenges to government authority

The lecture lists six pressures diminishing the state's decision-making
authority — a direct answer to one of the A1 framing prompts.

| Challenge | The lecture's example |
|---|---|
| **Rise of mega-corporations** | Google/Alphabet, Amazon, Apple, Meta — "combined revenue would qualify them for the G20". Economic power, or just power? |
| **Digital globalisation** | Uber deployed business models that ignored existing point-to-point taxi regulation, prompting calls for intervention |
| **Digital decentralisation** | Sharing economies; bitcoin |
| **Power shifts** to private, inter-governmental or multi-stakeholder governance | The Paris Agreement |
| **Underground economy** | Untaxed, unmeasured activity |
| **Econ-social issues** | Global pandemics, obesity, ageing population, climate impacts |

The common thread: each moves a decision that used to sit inside a
national government to somewhere it cannot easily reach — offshore, into a
protocol, onto a platform, or into an unmeasured market.

## 2.5 Peak bodies

**Definition** (lecture): representative, non-government organisations
whose membership predominantly consists of *other organisations* of allied
interests, and which are recognised by other peaks and their sectors
generally as representative of their whole sector.

The membership test is the discriminator: a peak body's members are
organisations, not individuals.

**Primary aim:** advocacy and representation to government and other
decision makers — influencing public or institutional policies and systems
on behalf of the sector.

**Other aims:** research and policy development; advice to government and
to the sector; information dissemination; sector consultation and
coordination; sector capacity building.

### Peak bodies for the focus sector

Tutorial 2 names four, and they are not interchangeable:

| Body | What it is |
|---|---|
| **Australian Dairy Industry Council (ADIC)** | The national policy and advocacy body for the whole value chain, working through its two constituent bodies (ADF and ADPF) |
| **Australian Dairy Products Federation (ADPF)** | Peak policy body for commercial / non-farm members — entities manufacturing, marketing or trading dairy products |
| **Dairy Australia (DA)** | Industry-owned national **service** organisation, funded by farmer levies with matching R&D funds from the Australian Government |
| **Dairy Industry Association of Australia (DIAA)** | Professional association for people working in dairy manufacturing and allied trades |

> **Only two of these four are peak bodies on the unit's own definition.**
> ADIC and ADPF represent member organisations and advocate — they fit.
> Dairy Australia is a levy-funded *service and R&D* organisation, and
> DIAA is a *professional association* whose members are individuals, not
> organisations. Getting this distinction right is exactly the kind of
> precision the P section rewards; listing all four as "peak bodies" is the
> kind of imprecision that costs a mark.

Note also that a sector can have several peak bodies, split by state or by
product type. The unit's contrasting example is Universities Australia —
one industry, one peak body.

## 2.6 Regulators

### The market and consumer regulator: the ACCC

- Primary responsibility: ensuring individuals and businesses comply with
  Australian **competition, fair trading and consumer protection** laws —
  in particular the **Competition and Consumer Act 2010**.
- Promotes competition and fair trade in markets to benefit consumers,
  businesses and the community.
- Regulates national infrastructure services.

### The financial system: the Council of Financial Regulators

The CFR is the coordinating body. Its members, and the tutorial's
matching exercise:

| Regulator | Role (tutorial 2 wording) |
|---|---|
| **ASIC** | Australia's integrated corporate, markets, financial services and consumer credit regulator |
| **ACCC** | Promotes competition and fair trade in markets to benefit consumers, businesses and the community; also regulates national infrastructure services |
| **Treasury** | Provides sound economic analysis and authoritative policy advice on the economy, budget, taxation, financial sector, foreign investment, structural policy, superannuation, small business, housing affordability and international economic policy |
| **RBA** | Duty is to contribute to the stability of the currency, full employment, and the economic prosperity and welfare of the Australian people |
| **APRA** | Independent statutory authority that supervises institutions across banking, insurance and superannuation, accountable to the Australian Parliament |

> **ASIC and APRA are the pair students swap.** APRA is **prudential** — it
> supervises the *institutions* so they stay solvent and can meet their
> obligations. ASIC is **conduct** — it polices how firms and markets
> *behave* toward investors and consumers. A bank that lends recklessly is
> an APRA problem; a bank that mis-sells is an ASIC problem. The 2017–19
> Banking Royal Commission (fees for the dead, cash bribes) was largely a
> conduct failure, so largely ASIC's domain.

### The Reserve Bank of Australia

**Main roles:**

1. To maintain the integrity and stability of the financial system.
2. To manage and implement monetary policy.

**Monetary policy goals** (the statutory three):

- Stability of the currency of Australia
- Maintenance of full employment
- Economic prosperity and welfare

**How it achieves them:** by managing interest rates, and by pursuing an
inflation target of **between 2 and 3 per cent per year on a medium-term
average**.

> **"On a medium-term average" is doing serious work in that sentence.**
> The target is not a band the RBA must sit inside every quarter — it is an
> average to be achieved over time. That is why headline CPI could hit
> 7.8% year-ended in the December quarter 2022 without the target being
> abandoned. In A1 you report the inflation *number*; if you start
> explaining how the RBA responded to it, you have crossed into A2.

### Regulators of the focus sector

Tutorial 2 unpicks who actually regulates butter and dairy manufacturing —
and the answer is "several people at two levels of government".

| Level | Body | Relevance |
|---|---|---|
| Sector regulator | **Food Standards Australia New Zealand (FSANZ)** | Develops the Food Standards Code, including Standard 4.2.4, the Primary Production and Processing Standard for Dairy Products |
| Federal | Department of Agriculture, Fisheries and Forestry (DAFF) | Manages dairy trade and export regulation |
| Federal | ACCC | Competition, consumer law, and the Dairy Code of Conduct |
| Federal | Fair Work Ombudsman | Workplace law compliance |
| State | NSW Food Authority | Food safety enforcement in NSW |
| State | Safe Food Queensland | Food safety enforcement in Queensland |

> **FSANZ writes the standard; the states enforce it.** That split is the
> business-environment point. A manufacturer operating in three states
> works to one Code but three enforcement agencies, with three inspection
> regimes and three licensing processes. Compliance cost scales with the
> number of jurisdictions, not the number of standards.

## 2.7 Social licence to operate

There is no agreed definition, and the tutorial says so before giving one
(Moffat et al., 2015):

> "SLO tends to be regarded as the ongoing acceptance or approval of an
> operation by those local community stakeholders who are affected by it …
> and those stakeholders who can affect its profitability. SLO is best
> understood as a set of demands and expectations, held by local
> stakeholders and broader civil society, for how a business should
> operate. SLO is a process of industry 'fitting in and adapting to the
> prevailing social norms'."

The critical attributes to highlight:

- **Ongoing** — it is not granted once. It can be withdrawn.
- **Granted by the community**, not by government. No regulator issues it.
- **Held in beliefs and perceptions**, so it is not fully controllable by
  the firm.
- **Two stakeholder groups**: those *affected by* the operation, and those
  who can *affect its profitability*.
- The tutorial notes the term's intangibility "has been part of its
  appeal", and that it has sometimes been "opportunistically used" by
  companies, activists and governments alike.

Drilling Down 2's two case studies: **horse racing** and **coal seam gas**.

> **A licence and a social licence fail in opposite directions.** A legal
> licence is granted by a regulator, is documented, and is lost through a
> defined process. A social licence is granted by a community, is
> undocumented, and can evaporate after a single news cycle. A firm can be
> fully compliant and still lose its social licence — that is the whole
> reason the concept exists. For butter and dairy the live pressure points
> are animal welfare, environmental performance and supply-chain
> transparency.

## 2.8 Three levels of government

Drilling Down 2 Task 2 asks you to summarise the roles of each level. The
advantage of sub-national government it identifies: within their
boundaries, sub-national governments "can formulate policy to address
local economic, social and environmental goals that are more nuanced to
reflect geographical and cultural considerations."

It also warns against dismissing them: sub-national policy "is not any
less complex" than national policy and covers social services, health
care, education, housing, planning, building and environmental protection.

Broadly:

| Level | Typical responsibilities |
|---|---|
| Federal | Economy-wide settings — trade, taxation, immigration, defence, monetary and fiscal policy |
| State / territory | Major service delivery and industry regulation — health, education, food safety enforcement, workplace safety |
| Local | Planning, permits, local infrastructure |

1. **Legal ownership of the resource; the right to earn income from it;

   and perceptions, and can be withdrawn without process. A firm can hold
   every legal licence and still lose its social licence.

   scores are not.

## Summary

- Institutions exist to **enforce law, provide a welfare-maximising
  regulatory framework, and keep the financial system stable**.
- A property right is **ownership + income + transfer**, and is worth
  nothing unless **enforceable**.
- **ICIP** is where the Western individual-ownership model fails; the
  Government has committed to stand-alone legislation.
- Government corrects **market failure** and provides **equity** and
  **stabilisation** — but operates in a **political marketplace** shaped by
  rent-seeking.
- Government authority is under pressure from **mega-corporations, digital
  globalisation and decentralisation, multi-stakeholder governance, the
  underground economy and econ-social issues**.
- **Peak bodies** represent organisations and advocate. For this sector,
  **ADIC and ADPF**.
- **ACCC** for competition and consumer law; **CFR** (RBA, APRA, ASIC,
  Treasury, ACCC) for the financial system; **FSANZ** plus state food
  authorities for the sector.
- **SLO** is granted by the community, is ongoing, and can be lost while
  fully compliant.
