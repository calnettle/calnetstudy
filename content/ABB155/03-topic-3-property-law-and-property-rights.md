# TOPIC 3 — Property Law and Property Rights

Week 3, Dr Andrea Blake. The doctrine of tenure, what an estate actually
is, why nobody in Australia "owns" land, and native title. This is the
lecture Assessment 1 is built on.

The deck's own three key takeaways, stated up front because everything else
hangs off them:

> - Property is about **rights**, not objects
> - Rights are **powerful but limited**, and will change over time
> - **Institutional characteristics and legislation** shape utility, land
>   use and value

## 3.1 "Property" is not a constant

The lecture opens with Macpherson, quoted in *Yanner v Eaton* [1999] HCA:

> "The meaning of property is not constant. The actual institution and the
> way people see it, and hence the meaning they give to the word, all change
> over time. The changes are related to changes in the purposes, which a
> society or the dominant classes in society expect the institution of
> property to serve."

Two things to take from this. First, property is a **social institution**,
not a natural fact — which is why the same word covers a 1916 freehold
subdivision and a 2014 volumetric perpetual lease over a toll tunnel.
Second, *Yanner v Eaton* is a native title case, so the quote is doing work
later in the lecture: if the meaning of property can change, then the common
law's recognition of native title in 1992 is a change in meaning, not an
invention of a new thing.

## 3.2 Real vs personal property

| | Real property | Personal property (chattels) |
|---|---|---|
| Latin | ***in rem*** — a right in the thing, good against the world | ***in personam*** — a right against a person |
| Subject | Land and things attached to it | Goods, chattels, choses in action |
| Enforcement | Different rights and different methods of enforcement | |

The deck says the distinction "is still important" and gives three reasons
that are all commercial rather than doctrinal:

- Important from a **valuation** perspective
- May have **taxation** implications
- May have **contractual** implications in sale/purchase agreements

> **The exam-friendly framing is fixtures.** The real/personal boundary bites
> where an item is attached to land: attached becomes part of the realty and
> passes with the land; unattached remains a chattel and does not. That is
> why the distinction shows up in valuation, tax and sale contracts rather
> than in abstract theory — the money question is always "is that item
> included in the sale?"

## 3.3 Three characteristics of land

The deck lists three headings and then develops only the second:

```
Physical characteristics       (fixed, indestructible, heterogeneous)
Institutional characteristics  (the doctrine of tenure — developed below)
Economic characteristics       (scarcity, long life, large capital)
```

The institutional characteristics are the ones that matter here, because
they are "your legal rights" and they "affect the way the land is **bought,
sold, used, taxed, resumed, inherited and leased**."

That seven-verb list is a good checklist for the Assessment 1 tenure

## 3.4 The doctrine of tenure

The single most important idea in the lecture:

> The doctrine of tenure is the principle that **all land is ultimately
> owned by the Crown**, and private individuals hold rights granted from the
> Crown rather than "owning" land absolutely.

The three-step structure:

```
Crown holds radical title
        ↓
Crown grants estates
        ↓
Individuals hold estates
```

How it got here:

- When Australia was colonised, **English common law was applied** and the
  Crown became the radical title holder to all the land.
- This was done on the basis that Australia was **"settled"** under the
  international law of the time (Blackstone's *Commentaries*), meaning
  English common law applied automatically so far as it was applicable to
  local conditions.
- Under the doctrine of tenure the Crown became the **absolute and
  beneficial owner** of all land in Australia. This "was later changed to
  '**radical title**' following **Mabo No. 2**, which first recognised
  native title."
- The modern interpretation recognises that **native title can coexist with
  the Crown's radical title**.
- "Individuals do not 'own' the land itself; they hold a **tenure**, which
  is a recognised legal interest granted by the Crown."
- "The effect in a modern context is now minimal, however an understanding
  of the doctrine is still fundamental to understanding land law today."

> **Absolute beneficial ownership → radical title is the whole point of
> *Mabo No. 2*, and it is a two-word change with enormous consequences.**
> Absolute beneficial ownership leaves no room for anyone else's prior
> interest; radical title is a bare sovereign title that *can* coexist with
> pre-existing native title. Writing "Mabo abolished terra nullius" is true
> but shallow — the doctrinal move is the downgrade of the Crown's title.

### Why the doctrine still matters

Three modern consequences, straight from the deck:

1. It **explains the structure** of land ownership and property rights for
   all estates in land — freehold and leasehold — as derived from the
   Crown's radical title.
2. It **supports the Torrens system** of property titling: the State
   guarantees title *because it is the ultimate source of title*.
3. It **shapes government powers**:
   - Compulsory acquisition
   - Crown/State land management — granting freehold and State leases
   - Native title coexistence and extinguishment

> **Point 2 is the one to quote in Assessment 1.** Indefeasibility under the
> *Land Title Act 1994* is not a free-floating statutory gift — the State
> can guarantee your title because the State is the source of it. That is
> the link between the medieval doctrine and the modern register, and it
> explains why "rights and interests reserved to the Crown by Deed of Grant"
> still sits on a 2026 title search as encumbrance number one.

## 3.5 From colonies to Constitution

The constitutional layer, because land administration sits with the States
and planning with local government under State law.

- By the mid-1800s each colony — **including Queensland from 1859** —
  gained responsible self-government with its own parliament.
- In **1901** the colonies federated to form the Commonwealth of Australia
  under the Australian Constitution.
- The Constitution **explicitly states the powers that go to the
  Commonwealth (section 51)**; the balance go to the States.
- **Local government is not recognised in the Commonwealth Constitution**
  but is empowered under Queensland legislation.

And the separation of powers: **Parliament · Judiciary · Executive**.

| Layer | Source of power | Includes |
|---|---|---|
| **Commonwealth** | s 51 of the Constitution | (xxvii) immigration and emigration; (xxix) external affairs; **(xxxi) the acquisition of property on just terms** from any State or person for any purpose in respect of which the Parliament has power to make laws |
| **Queensland** | All powers not in s 51 | Education, health, **land administration** |
| **Local** | Created under State law | **Local planning schemes**; local roads, parks, waste, community services; local laws on parking, animals, signage. Powers can be **expanded or limited by the State** |

> **Two traps live in this table.**
> **(1)** Land administration is a *State* power, so the statutes that
> matter for tenure are Queensland ones — *Land Title Act 1994*, *Land Act
> 1994*, *Property Law Act 1974*, *Queensland Heritage Act 1992*,
> *Acquisition of Land Act 1967*. Citing Commonwealth legislation for
> tenure is the "jurisdictional error" the Assessment 1 CRA punishes.
> **(2)** The just terms guarantee in **s 51(xxxi) binds the Commonwealth
> only**. The lecture states it flatly: "**No such provision in the state
> constitutions.**" Queensland compensation on resumption comes from
> statute — the *Acquisition of Land Act 1967* (Qld) — not from a
> constitutional guarantee. Writing that a Queensland resumption is
> protected by s 51(xxxi) is wrong.

The deck's slide numbering for s 51 lists "(xxxvi) Immigration and
emigration"; the immigration/emigration power is in fact **s 51(xxvii)**,
with (xxxvi) being the "matters in respect of which this Constitution makes
provision until the Parliament otherwise provides" power. Use (xxvii) if
you cite it, and check the Constitution rather than the slide.

## 3.6 Estates in land — the bundle of rights

```
                    ESTATES
                   /        \
            Freehold        Leasehold
            /      \
      Fee simple   Life estate
```

Note the lecture's own caveat: it recommends lawshelf.com's *Real Property
Ownership: The Estate System and Future Interests* — "an American site, but
the estates system explanation is sufficiently similar to help understand
the terminology." Useful for terminology, not for Queensland law.

### Rights in a fee simple estate

Documented through **the title document and the survey plan** — which is
exactly why Assessment 1 gives you both for each lot. The seven rights:

| Right | What it lets the holder do |
|---|---|
| **Exclusive possession** | Exclude everyone else — the action is trespass |
| **Natural rights** | Support, right to light, etc. |
| **Alienate** | Sell or transfer |
| **Devise** | Leave it to someone in a will |
| **Create a lesser interest** | Grant a lease, easement, mortgage |
| **Share** | Invite someone onto the property |
| **Receive income** | Take rent and other income from the land |

> **"Right to create a lesser interest" is the one that unlocks Assessment
> 1.** Every encumbrance on a title is a lesser interest that some fee
> simple owner had the right to create — and once created, it binds
> successors. A lease, easement or covenant is not an intrusion on the
> bundle from outside; it is a stick from the bundle that has been handed
> to someone else. Frame encumbrances that way and the "rights and
> obligations" part of the CRA writes itself.

### The bundle of sticks

"Ownership of land is compared to a **bundle of sticks**, each stick
representing a right." The deck's list of sticks: the right to use, the
power to alienate, the power to assimilate, to pass by succession, to claim
title to.

And then the limits, in the same breath:

- Land is subject to **many other pieces of legislation**.
- The **Crown can acquire land compulsorily**. Compensation on "just terms"
  is deemed necessary by **s 51(xxxi)** of the Constitution — with no
  equivalent in the State constitutions.

So the answer to "is it possible to own land?" is: you hold a bundle of
rights, granted by the Crown, from which sticks can be removed by statute
and by your own dealings.

## 3.7 What affects property rights

Three sources, and Assessment 1 needs all three:

| Source | How it operates |
|---|---|
| **Common law** | Where no statute covers an issue, a judge hears the case and issues a verdict. That record becomes a **precedent**, so later judges may take the earlier decision into account. Previous judgments therefore form the basis of common law |
| **Statute law** | Legislation passed through Parliament. "Statute" and "legislation" are used interchangeably |
| **Easements, encumbrances and other interests** (including to the Crown) | Registered interests that burden or benefit the land, plus leases |

## 3.8 Leases: two completely different animals

This distinction is small in the deck and large in Assessment 1.

```
A lease            ≠           Leasehold tenure
(freehold owner leases                 (Crown leases)
 land to another party)
```

| | Commercial lease | Crown lease |
|---|---|---|
| Grantor | A **fee simple (freehold) owner** leases land to a tenant | The **Crown (State government)** leases to the lessee |
| Precondition | The land is freehold | **No freehold grant has been made** over the land |
| Effect | Gives the tenant rights **in contract** and **against third parties**, because it is an interest in land | Creates a leasehold **tenure** — a form of holding from the Crown |
| Native title | Native title generally extinguished by the underlying freehold | Native title may survive; depends on whether exclusive possession was granted |

> **This is the distinction that makes 189 Lutwyche Road a good exam
> question.** The Airport Link Tunnel is held under a **lease in perpetuity
> from the State** — a Crown lease, i.e. a *leasehold tenure*, not a
> commercial lease granted by the surface owner. The surface owner did not
> grant it and cannot terminate it. Get this backwards and the tenure
> analysis collapses. See note 04, §4.3.

## 3.9 Crown / State leases in Queensland

Facts from the deck worth quoting:

- **62% of land is held by the State of Queensland** in one form or another.
- Held for a variety of purposes; may be leased to companies or
  individuals.
- **Most of the State falls under the generic category of crown leasehold
  land.**
- Crown leasehold land is referred to in the *Land Act* as **"unallocated
  State land"**.
- **All of this land could potentially be subject to native title claim.**
- There is **no equivalent in the UK** to crown leasehold interests.

> **Careful with the terminology on that fifth bullet.** The slide says
> crown leasehold land "is referred to in the Land Act as 'unallocated State
> land'." Under the *Land Act 1994* (Qld), **unallocated State land is State
> land that is *not* subject to a lease or other allocation** — it is the
> residual category, and land under a Crown lease has by definition been
> allocated. Learn the deck's framing for the assessment, but if you use the
> term in a report, define it from the Act and check it with your tutor
> before relying on the slide's wording.

## 3.10 Native title

### Where it came from

The deck's legislative journey, in order:

```
1788    Colonisation — principle of terra nullius
1992    Mabo v Queensland (No. 2) (1992) 175 CLR 1
1993    Native Title Act 1993 (Cth)
1996    Wik Peoples v Queensland (1996) 187 CLR 1
1998    Native Title Amendment Act 1998 (Cth)
2002    WA v Ward (2002) 191 ALR 1
2002    Yorta Yorta v Victoria (2002) 194 ALR 538
2003    Daniel v Western Australia [2003] FCA 666
2003    De Rose v State of South Australia (2003)
```

The pre-Mabo position is captured by **New South Wales v Commonwealth
(1923)**, quoted in the deck:

> "That originally the wastelands in the colonies were owned by the British
> Crown is not in doubt… In either event, the consequence is the same, the
> lands of Australia became the property of the King of England."

### What native title is

| Feature | Detail |
|---|---|
| First recognised | **Mabo No. 2** |
| Origin | The common law **did not create** native title — it **recognised** the existence of Indigenous customary law |
| Form | Consistent with Indigenous customary law; each incidence is unique, ***sui generis*** |
| Subject matter | May be over **land or sea** |
| Alienability | **Inalienable** — it cannot be traded |

### How it is extinguished

Three routes in the deck:

- A **break in connection**
- A **valid exercise of sovereign power**
- **Permanent extinguishment through the grant of a freehold title**

And the framing question for any tenure: **"Is there a grant of exclusive
possession which extinguishes native title?"** — the line of authority being
*Mabo No. 2*, *Wik Peoples v Queensland*, and *WA v Ward*.

By tenure type:

| Tenure | Native title position |
|---|---|
| **Freehold** | In general, native title **has been extinguished** |
| **Leasehold** (leased by the State) | Depends on whether the lease conferred exclusive possession — *Wik* held that pastoral leases did not necessarily extinguish native title |

### Making a claim

A claim may be made at common law, but claims are **usually made under the
*Native Title Act 1993* (Cth)**. The elements come from **section 223** —
three limbs:

```
(a) the rights/interests are possessed under traditional law or custom
(b) there is a connection with the claimed land or waters
(c) the rights/interests are recognised by the common law
```

Authorities attached to those limbs in the deck: **Members of the Yorta
Yorta Community v Victoria** and **De Rose v South Australia**.

> **Limb (b), connection, is where claims fail.** *Yorta Yorta* is the
> cautionary authority: the traditional laws and customs must have been
> acknowledged and observed **substantially uninterrupted** since
> sovereignty. That is why "a break in connection" appears in the
> extinguishment list — it is not a separate legal act by the Crown but a
> failure of the claimant's own s 223 case. Do not describe it as something
> the Crown does.

## 3.11 Applying this to a title search

A worked reading of the two surface titles in the Assessment 1 pack, which
is the fastest way to see the doctrine in operation:

```
Title Reference:  50783951        Date Title Created: 23/09/2009
ESTATE AND LAND
   Estate in Fee Simple
   LOT 2  REGISTERED PLAN 18495
   Local Government: BRISBANE CITY
REGISTERED OWNER
   Dealing No: 715875123  03/07/2014
   METRO NORTH HOSPITAL AND HEALTH SERVICE
EASEMENTS, ENCUMBRANCES AND INTERESTS
   1. Rights and interests reserved to the Crown by
        Deed of Grant No. 40021306 (Lot 2 on RP 18495)
```

Line by line:

| Line | Doctrine it demonstrates |
|---|---|
| "Estate in Fee Simple" | The holder holds an **estate**, not the land. The largest freehold estate, but still an estate granted from the Crown |
| "Registered Owner… Metro North Hospital and Health Service" | Registration under the *Land Title Act 1994* gives **indefeasibility** — guaranteed by the State because the State is the source of title (§3.4) |
| "Rights and interests reserved to the Crown by **Deed of Grant**" | The **original Crown grant** still sits on the title as encumbrance 1. This is radical title made visible: the Crown granted the estate and reserved rights when it did |
| Freehold tenure | Native title **generally extinguished** on this land (§3.10) |

> **Every freehold title in Queensland carries that Crown reservation, and
> most students skip it as boilerplate.** It is not boilerplate — it is the
> doctrine of tenure printed on the document, and the Assessment 1 CRA asks
> for "a comprehensive, legally accurate explanation of freehold tenure…
> including rights, obligations, and **statutory protections**." Two
> sentences on the Deed of Grant reservation is cheap marks.

## Summary

- Property is about **rights, not objects**; the meaning of property changes
  over time (Macpherson, quoted in *Yanner v Eaton*).
- **Real (*in rem*) vs personal (*in personam*)** property still matters for
  valuation, taxation and sale contracts.
- The **doctrine of tenure**: the Crown holds **radical title**, grants
  **estates**, and individuals hold estates — not the land. Australia was
  treated as **settled**, so English common law applied.
- *Mabo No. 2* changed the Crown's position from **absolute beneficial
  ownership** to **radical title**, allowing native title to coexist.
- The doctrine still matters because it **explains estates**, **underwrites
  the Torrens system** (the State guarantees title because it is the source
  of title), and **shapes government powers** — compulsory acquisition,
  State land management, native title.
- **Land administration is a State power.** Local government is not in the
  Commonwealth Constitution; it is empowered by State law. **s 51(xxxi) just
  terms binds the Commonwealth only** — there is no State equivalent.
- A **fee simple** holds seven rights: exclusive possession, natural rights,
  alienate, devise, create a lesser interest, share, receive income. Every
  encumbrance is one of those sticks handed to someone else.
- **A lease ≠ leasehold tenure.** A commercial lease is granted by a
  freehold owner; a Crown lease is granted by the State over land never
  granted in freehold.
- **62%** of Queensland is held by the State in some form, mostly crown
  leasehold, all potentially subject to native title claim.
- Native title was **recognised, not created**, by the common law; it is
  ***sui generis*** and **inalienable**; extinguished by break in
  connection, valid exercise of sovereign power, or grant of freehold. The
  claim elements are in **s 223** of the *Native Title Act 1993* (Cth).

**Q1. A client says "I own my land outright — I bought it and it's freehold." Correct them using the doctrine of tenure, in three sentences.**

Under the doctrine of tenure all land in Australia is ultimately held from
the Crown, which retains radical title; what you hold is an estate in fee
simple, the largest estate available, granted from the Crown. Your title
search says so — the first encumbrance listed is the rights and interests
reserved to the Crown by the original Deed of Grant. In practical terms the
difference is minimal, but it explains why the State can guarantee your
title under the Torrens system, why it can resume the land compulsorily, and
why statute can restrict what you do with it.

**Q2. What exactly changed in the Crown's title after *Mabo No. 2*, and why does it matter?**

Before *Mabo No. 2* the Crown was treated as the absolute and beneficial
owner of all land in Australia on acquisition of sovereignty. *Mabo No. 2*
reduced that to **radical title** — a bare sovereign title. It matters
because absolute beneficial ownership is inconsistent with anyone else
holding a prior interest, whereas radical title can coexist with native
title. The common law did not create native title; it recognised the
continuing existence of Indigenous customary law.

**Q3. Distinguish a lease from leasehold tenure and give the Assessment 1 example.**

A lease is created when a fee simple (freehold) owner leases land to a
tenant; it gives the tenant rights in contract and, because it is an
interest in land, rights against third parties. Leasehold *tenure* is a
Crown lease — the State leases land over which no freehold grant has been
made. The Airport Link Tunnel beneath 189 Lutwyche Road is the second kind:
a lease in perpetuity granted by the State to itself through the Department
of Transport and Main Roads (PPL 238134, title reference 40069275). The
surface freeholder is not the lessor and has no power over it.

**Q4. Does the just terms guarantee in s 51(xxxi) protect a landowner whose land is resumed by the Queensland Government?**

No. Section 51(xxxi) of the Commonwealth Constitution binds the
Commonwealth, and the lecture is explicit that there is no equivalent
provision in the State constitutions. Compensation for a Queensland
resumption arises under State statute — the *Acquisition of Land Act 1967*
(Qld) — not from a constitutional guarantee.

**Q5. List the three s 223 elements of a native title claim and name the case most associated with the second.**

(a) the rights and interests are possessed under traditional law or custom;
(b) there is a connection with the claimed land or waters;
(c) the rights and interests are recognised by the common law.

The second is most associated with *Members of the Yorta Yorta Community v
Victoria* (2002), where the claim failed because the traditional laws and
customs had not been acknowledged and observed substantially without
interruption since sovereignty.

**Q6. Name three of the seven rights of a fee simple estate that an easement in gross to Energex would affect.**

Exclusive possession — the utility gains a right of access the owner cannot
exclude. The right to create a lesser interest — that stick has already been
exercised, and it binds successors. The right to use — the burdened strip
cannot be built on or obstructed in ways inconsistent with the easement. (A
reasonable answer might also cite the right to receive income, where the
easement sterilises lettable area.)
