## Section A — Concepts and definitions

**Q1.** Give the unit's working definition of land use planning, attribute
it, and explain in one sentence each what the four component phrases
commit a planner to.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

"A formal process regulating the use of land and the development of the
built environment, in order to achieve strategic policy objectives."
— Gurran, N. (2011), *Australian Urban Land Use Planning: Principles,
systems and practice*, Sydney University Press.

- **Formal process** — the intervention must be statutory, procedural and
  reviewable, not ad hoc discretion.
- **Regulating the use of land** — controls on *use*: zones, use
  definitions, tables of assessment.
- **Development of the built environment** — controls on *form*: height,
  setback, site cover, gross floor area.
- **In order to achieve strategic policy objectives** — regulation is
  instrumental; every control must trace to a stated objective, which is
  also what makes a control challengeable if no objective supports it.

</details>

**Q2.** A client's architect argues that because the client holds freehold
title, the council's height limit is an unjustified interference. Set out
the lecture's four-step justification for constraints on liberty and apply
it.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

1. As a general principle we accept that ownership confers certain rights —
   and fee simple is the largest estate available.
2. We also accept that an individual's actions should be limited where they
   negatively impact on others.
3. On Gurran's definition, land use planning will typically limit rights in
   the use of land, for the benefit of others (human or non-human) — a
   height limit protects amenity, solar access, streetscape character and
   the built-form outcome sought by the strategic framework.
4. Those benefits must be accepted as sufficient to justify the
   intervention — and in this case they have been, through the statutory
   plan-making process that produced the zone and the neighbourhood plan.

The architect's premise is also wrong on the law: freehold is a bundle of
rights held from the Crown, never an absolute immunity from regulation.

</details>

**Q3.** Why does the Week 1 lecture spend three slides asking how you feel
viewing a city from a tower, in plan view, and on foot?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

To make the point that plan-view and GIS analysis abstracts the analyst away
from human experience. The deck says it directly: big-picture technical
analysis "can isolate you from human experience," and a focus on land use
without consideration of the underlying values risks implementing
simplistic, preconceived "solutions." It is the values → activities → land
use chain in teaching form, and it is the honest limitation to state about
any desktop suitability analysis.

</details>

**Q5.** True or false: Le Corbusier's Towers in the Park is a
higher-density urban form than the alternatives. Justify.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

**False, on the lecture's own account.** The deck says Towers in the Park
delivers "the same overall density as other city forms, but very high
localised densities." The tower concentrates dwellings on a small footprint
and gives back the rest as open space; it does not by itself increase people
per hectare of city. This is the standard confusion between *building*
density and *urban* density, and it matters because compact-city policy is
about the latter.

</details>

**Q6.** Explain, in the terms the lecture uses, why the blueprint approach
to planning failed — and connect the failure to something in the property
law half of this unit.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Blueprints apply a preconceived "solution" without regard to economic,
political or social realities, and they do not take account of the
constraints and potentials that an orderly planning process would discover.
To implement one, the planner has to become an "omniscient ruler"
constructing a new reality unimpeded by **private interests in land** and by
**the processes of participatory democracy**.

The connection to property law is direct: private interests in land are
precisely what the Torrens system registers and protects. A fee simple
holder's bundle of rights — exclusive possession, alienation, the power to
create lesser interests — is an obstacle to any plan that requires land to
be reassembled at will. So blueprint planning is not merely impractical; it
is incompatible with the tenure system the unit spends Week 3 describing.
Le Corbusier's ideas were only ever implemented in that manner in
state-controlled social housing, "with disastrous results."

</details>

**Q7.** Present the density-versus-sprawl debate in a way that would earn a
7, naming at least four sources.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Concerns about sprawl date from the 1920s — loss of agricultural land,
travel-time inefficiency, and the cost of servicing far-flung suburbs with
infrastructure. The evidentiary turning point was **Newman & Kenworthy
(1989)**, whose finding that higher-density cities consume less petrol
became the key argument for compact city policy; **OECD (2012)** later
assessed such policies comparatively.

The case is contested. **Ewing & Hamidi (2015)** review US evidence and
argue both that compact form does not necessarily deliver the claimed
sustainability gains and that reshaping entire cities is infeasible relative
to other policy levers. **Angel et al. (2010)** show a persistent global
decline in urban densities — sprawl is winning empirically. Pandemics,
lockdowns and associated mental health effects further challenged
high-density living.

Despite this, compact city policies have been adopted by almost every major
OECD nation. Australia is no exception: metropolitan policies typically
create a **network of compact activity centres**. Whether it is working is,
as the lecture puts it, a different matter.

The mark comes from holding both halves at once — a near-universal policy
consensus resting on contested evidence.

</details>

**Q8.** What is the "missing middle", and why is it the right frame for

<details>

The missing middle is the housing band between detached dwellings and

</details>

## Section C — Property law, tenure and native title

**Q9.** Explain the doctrine of tenure in three sentences, then state what
*Mabo No. 2* changed about it.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

The doctrine of tenure holds that all land is ultimately owned by the Crown
and that private individuals hold rights granted from the Crown rather than
owning land absolutely. When Australia was colonised, English common law was
applied on the footing that the continent was "settled", and the Crown
became the title holder to all the land; individuals hold a *tenure* — a
recognised legal interest granted by the Crown — in the form of an estate.
Its modern practical effect is minimal, but it explains the structure of
freehold and leasehold, underwrites the Torrens system, and shapes
government powers.

*Mabo No. 2* (1992) changed the Crown's position from **absolute and
beneficial ownership** to **radical title**. Absolute beneficial ownership
excludes any prior interest; radical title is a bare sovereign title capable
of coexisting with native title, which the common law recognised (rather
than created).

</details>

**Q10.** Why does the doctrine of tenure explain the *State guarantee* of
title under the Torrens system?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Because the State can guarantee title only because it is the ultimate
source of title. Every registered estate derives from a Crown grant, so
indefeasibility under the *Land Title Act 1994* is not an arbitrary
statutory promise — it is the sovereign standing behind interests it
created. This is also why the first encumbrance on almost every Queensland
freehold title is "rights and interests reserved to the Crown by Deed of
Grant No. …": the original grant, and its reservations, remain on the face
of the register.

</details>

**Q11.** A Queensland landowner's property is resumed by the State. They
tell you the Constitution guarantees them just terms. Are they right?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

No. **Section 51(xxxi)** of the Commonwealth Constitution requires
acquisition of property on just terms, but it binds the **Commonwealth**.
The Week 3 lecture is explicit that there is **no such provision in the
State constitutions**. Compensation for a Queensland resumption arises under
State statute — principally the ***Acquisition of Land Act 1967*** (Qld) —
not from a constitutional guarantee. Land administration is a State power,
being one of the residual powers not enumerated in s 51.

</details>

**Q12.** List the seven rights of a fee simple estate, and say which one
explains the existence of every encumbrance on a title.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Exclusive possession; natural rights (support, light); the right to
alienate; the right to devise; **the right to create a lesser interest**;
the right to share; the right to receive income from the land.

The fifth. Every lease, easement, mortgage and covenant on a title is a
lesser interest that some fee simple owner had the right to create — a
stick from the bundle handed to someone else, which then binds successors in
title. Framing encumbrances that way turns a list into an analysis.

</details>

**Q13.** State the three s 223 elements of a native title claim, name the
authority most associated with each of the extinguishment routes, and
explain why "break in connection" is different in kind from the other two.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Section 223 of the *Native Title Act 1993* (Cth):
(a) the rights and interests are possessed under traditional law or custom;
(b) there is a connection with the claimed land or waters;
(c) the rights and interests are recognised by the common law.

Extinguishment routes and authorities: a **valid exercise of sovereign
power** (*WA v Ward*); **permanent extinguishment through the grant of
freehold** (*Mabo No. 2*, with *Wik Peoples v Queensland* establishing that
a non-exclusive pastoral lease does not necessarily extinguish); and a
**break in connection** (*Members of the Yorta Yorta Community v Victoria*;
*De Rose v South Australia*).

"Break in connection" is different in kind because it is not an act of the
Crown at all — it is a failure of the claimant's own s 223(b) case, where
the traditional laws and customs have not been acknowledged and observed
substantially without interruption since sovereignty. The other two are
things the sovereign does; this one is something that happens to the claim.

</details>

**Q14.** Distinguish a *lease* from *leasehold tenure*, then classify the
Airport Link Tunnel interest.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

A **lease** is created when a fee simple (freehold) owner leases land to a
tenant; it gives the tenant rights in contract and, being an interest in
land, rights against third parties. **Leasehold tenure** is a Crown lease —
the State leases land over which no freehold grant has ever been made. The
two are frequently confused because both are called leases.

The Airport Link Tunnel is **leasehold tenure**: PPL 238134, title reference
40069275, a *perpetual* lease with the registered lessee being "The State of
Queensland (represented by Department of Transport and Main Roads)", granted
for the purpose "TRANSPORT FACILITY" and commencing 5 December 2014. The
surface freeholder is not the lessor. It cannot vary or terminate the
interest.

</details>

**Q19.** The heritage listing was entered on the Queensland Heritage
Register on 24 September 1999, but the administrative advice was lodged on
the titles on 21 June 2016. Why does that gap matter?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Because the statutory effect flows from entry in the Register under the
*Queensland Heritage Act 1992*, not from the notation on the title. The
administrative advice is a notification device. A purchaser who searched the
title in, say, 2005 and found nothing would still have taken subject to the

the planning scheme property report, and the overlays.

## Section E — GIS and site constraint analysis

**Q23.** Your Set B Q1 answer differs from a classmate's by several
thousand. Neither of you has made an arithmetic error. What are the two
most likely causes?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

**(1) A different spatial relationship in Select By Location.** "Intersect",
"Within" (which permits boundary contact), "Completely within" (which does
not) and "Have their centre in" give materially different counts, because
zone boundaries follow cadastral boundaries and a very large number of lots
therefore sit on a zone edge and share it. The brief says "completely
within".

**(2) A different treatment of the selecting features.** Select By Location
tests against each selecting polygon individually, so a lot straddling two
adjacent polygons of the same zone is not completely within either. If one
of you dissolved the zoning layer first and the other did not, the counts
will differ.

Both are documentation problems, not skill problems, which is why the
dialogue screenshots belong in an appendix.

</details>

**Q24.** Compute the geodesic/planar area scale factor for MGA Zone 56 near
the central meridian, and explain when the difference matters.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

MGA is a transverse Mercator projection with a central scale factor
k₀ = 0.9996. Grid distances near the central meridian are 0.9996 of true
distance, so grid *areas* are 0.9996² of true area:

```
area scale (grid → geodesic) = 1 / 0.9996²
                             = 1 / 0.99920016
                             = 1.00080048
```

Geodesic areas come out about **0.08% larger**. It matters only where a lot
sits within 0.08% of a threshold — which on the 1,300 m² MCA boundary means
within about 1 m². 3301 Moggill Road is 1,307.65 m² geodesic and 1,306.60 m²
planar, so it survives either way; a lot at 1,299.4 m² planar would flip
from 0 to 1 on `MCA_L` under the geodesic option. State which option you
used.

</details>

**Q27.** A student writes: "The lot is 1,372 m², which exceeds AO3.1's 800
m² minimum, so PO3 is satisfied." Correct them.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

That confuses the two halves of performance-based planning. The
**performance outcome** is the mandatory standard; the **acceptable
outcome** is one prescribed, deemed-to-comply way of achieving it, and
applicants may instead propose an alternative solution that achieves the PO.
Meeting an AO is normally sufficient for that AO's aspect, but PO3 asks for
something broader — that development "retains the open semi-rural character
of the area by maintaining large lot sizes **and** providing sufficient
frontage for buildings to address the street." A multiple dwelling can
satisfy the numeric lot-size AO and still fail the character outcome through
its bulk, siting or frontage treatment. The correct answer identifies the
tension, notes that the assessment manager will weigh built form against the
character outcome, and recommends pre-lodgement advice.

</details>

**Q29.** Why does the brief's MCA table leave a gap, how many lots does it
affect, and what should you do about it?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

The table awards 1 point for "Unit Number **0**" and 0 points for "Unit

literal expression — `1 if UNIT_NUMBE == 0 else 0` — because Set B is marked
on matching the brief, **state the assumption explicitly** in the
methodology, and confirm the intended treatment with your tutor. If a
one-unit lot appears in your shortlist, the ambiguity decides the
recommendation.

### The traps, in one list

1. **A lease ≠ leasehold tenure.** The tunnel is a Crown lease; the surface
   owner did not grant it.
2. **s 51(xxxi) just terms binds the Commonwealth only.** No State
   equivalent. Queensland compensation comes from the *Acquisition of Land
   Act 1967*.
3. **Land administration is a State power.** Citing Commonwealth statutes
   for tenure is a jurisdictional error and the CRA punishes it.
4. **Holding report ≠ lot report.** Three zones on a holding report do not
   all apply to every part of the site.
5. **Heritage effect comes from the Register, not the title notation.** The
   listing preceded the title advice by 17 years.
6. **"Completely within" excludes boundary-touching lots** and tests each
   selecting polygon separately.
7. **Set B Q2 asks for the flood-affected count**, not the survivors.
8. **`UNIT_NUMBE` and `MCA_ToT`** — not `Unit_Number`, not `MCA_TOT`.
9. **Use ≥ and ≤** in the size expression, as the brief specifies.
10. **Geodesic ≠ planar ≠ title area.** Three numbers; say which you used.
11. **Multiple dwellings round parking DOWN**; everything else rounds up.
12. **AOs are not the standard — POs are.** Meeting AO3.1 does not
    automatically satisfy PO3.
13. **Old imperial areas are acres·roods·perches**, not decimals.
14. **Don't attribute a section to a document that doesn't contain it.**
    The state tenure search names no granting provision.
15. **Disclose GenAI use in detail.** Both CRAs score it, and
    non-disclosure caps the criterion.
