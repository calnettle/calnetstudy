# TOPIC 2 — Evolution of Land Use Planning and Property Development

Week 2. How planning got invented, why the great visionaries' blueprints
failed, where sustainability entered the picture, and the density-versus-
sprawl argument that still drives every Australian metropolitan strategy.

## 2.1 Where planning came from

Humans have always controlled land uses. What changed was scale:

- The **industrial revolution** produced unprecedented urban population
  growth.
- Those rapidly growing urban areas "proved to be incapable of providing
  for basic human needs, **especially for sanitation**."
- "The concept of land use planning that we know today came in response to
  the conditions created from uncontrolled urban development."

The practical foundation is specific and dateable:

```
From the 1850s  London begins formalised land use interventions
                for the purpose of improving human health
                → initially clean water supply
                → soon expanded to control building standards
```

> **Planning's origin is public health, not aesthetics.** This matters for
> essay framing: the first justification for restricting land use rights was
> cholera, and the modern lineage runs straight from there to flood
> overlays, acid sulfate soils overlays and transport noise categories. If
> you are asked why planning intervention is legitimate, the historical
> answer is that it was invented to stop people dying.

## 2.2 The "seers" — five blueprints

Highly urbanised environments plus new transport modes provoked a
generation of thinkers who drew "blueprints of what they considered to be
the optimal urban form." The deck names five. **Some of their ideas are
still commonly used in planning today** — which is why they are examinable
rather than merely historical.

| Thinker | Model | Core idea | Still visible in |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Ebenezer Howard** | The Garden City | Separation of conflicting land uses; **green belts** limiting urban expansion; networks of urban settlements; built from a consideration of human need and desire | Use-based zoning; urban growth boundaries; the whole idea of a settlement network |
| **Clarence Perry** | The Neighbourhood Unit | Functional neighbourhoods at a set scale containing the essential services for an idealised population unit — shops, schools, community centres, open space accessible within the unit, **physically contained by major roads** | Neighbourhood plans; walkable catchment standards; hierarchical road networks |
| **Frank Lloyd Wright** | Broadacre City | The private motor vehicle "liberates" the city from geographic constraints; dispersed settlement, allotments large enough to grow your own food, highways connect everything and **petrol stations form the hubs** | Post-war car-oriented suburbia; large-lot rural residential zoning |
| **Arturo Soria y Mata** | The Linear City | Populations expand along new mass-transit lines, so build long continuous cities to maximise the transit investment while keeping open space and farmland | Transit-oriented development; corridor neighbourhood plans (e.g. the Lutwyche Road corridor) |
| **Le Corbusier** | Towers in the Park | Go up, not out. **Same overall density** as other city forms but very high *localised* densities; green space preserved between buildings; mass transit and highways link them; strictly stratified with identical apartments and furniture for the same classes | High-rise residential zoning; podium-and-tower typologies |

> **The Le Corbusier point that gets missed.** *Towers in the Park is not a
> higher-density model overall* — the deck is explicit that it produces the
> "same overall density as other city forms, but very high localised
> densities." The tower buys you open space at ground level; it does not by
> itself house more people per hectare of city. If an exam question asks
> whether high-rise equals compact urban form, the answer is no, and this
> slide is why.

## 2.3 The problem with blueprints

The deck's critique is the most argumentative content in Weeks 1–3, and it
is where the essay marks live.

The danger is treating plans for human settlements as **blueprints to be
stamped onto the world without consideration of economic, political, or
social realities**. To implement one, the planner has to become an
**"omniscient ruler"** who constructs a new reality:

- unimpeded by **private interests in land**, and
- unimpeded by the **processes of participatory democracy**.

Both of those are exactly what property law and statutory planning exist to
protect. So the blueprint model is not merely impractical — it is
incompatible with the legal and political system the rest of this unit
describes.

Le Corbusier's ideas, "though never holistically implemented, were applied
in this manner in social housing projects with disastrous results." The deck
quotes Hall:

> "The sins of the Corbusians lay… in mindless arrogance whereby they were
> imposed on people who could not take them and could never… ever have been
> expected to take them."
> — Hall, P. (2002), *Cities of Tomorrow*, pp. 260–261.

The generalised diagnosis: **blueprints apply a preconceived "solution"
that does not take into account the constraints and potentials discovered
through an orderly planning process.**

That sentence is the bridge to Geddes, and it is also the bridge to your
own assessments — both of which are, structurally, exercises in
*discovering constraints before proposing a solution*.

## 2.4 Geddes and the method that survived

**Patrick Geddes'** contribution "was methodological." He came from biology
and understood the necessity of understanding an area's nature before
devising a plan for it.

```
SURVEY  →  ANALYSE  →  PLAN
```

The deck's dry aside is the useful bit: "Seems so basic, but you would be
surprised how easy it is to jump to a 'solution'." An evolved version of
Survey–Analyse–Plan is still used in practice, even though planning theory
has moved on.

> **SAP is the skeleton of both ABB155 assessments, and marking it that way
> earns marks.** Assessment 1 = survey the titles, plans and overlays →
> analyse tenure, tunnel, heritage, planning → *then* propose a highest and
> best use. Assessment 2 = survey the four datasets → analyse against core
> criteria and MCA → *then* recommend two lots. If your Assessment 1
> conclusion could have been written before your analysis, you have written
> a blueprint, and the CRA has a band for that: "conclusions are
> unsupported, poorly reasoned, or inconsistent with the analysis."

### What came after Geddes

The deck's "Fast Forward…" slide compresses about sixty years of theory
into four moves:

| Move | What it added |
|---|---|
| **Rational comprehensive method** | Added **implementation** to survey–analyse–plan |
| **Systems approach** | **Process over plan** — the city as a system to be steered, not an object to be drawn |
| **Post-modernism and the communicative "turn"** | Questioned how impartial the earlier methods actually were; established the need for **those affected by planning to have a say** |
| Result | Planning for idealised urban forms lost favour — until sustainability revived it |

Note the shape of that sequence: it is the same "plan vs process" axis from
Week 1, resolving progressively toward process — and then sustainability
brings the idealised urban form back through the side door.

## 2.5 Sustainable development

The Brundtland definition, quoted exactly:

> "Sustainable development is development that meets the needs of the
> present without compromising the ability of future generations to meet
> their own needs."
> — World Commission on Environment and Development, 1987

The deck presents the familiar three-circle Venn — **Environment, Social,
Economic** — and then does something more useful with it: it labels the
three **overlaps as conflicts**, not as harmonies.

```
                     Environment
                    /            \
   Development     /              \    Resource
   Conflict       /                \   Conflict
                 /                  \
            Social ──────────── Economic
                    Property Conflict
```

| Overlap | Conflict named |
|---|---|
| Environment ∩ Economic | **Resource conflict** |
| Environment ∩ Social | **Development conflict** |
| Social ∩ Economic | **Property conflict** |

> **Learn the three conflicts, not just the three circles.** Every student
> can draw the Venn. The examinable content is that the intersections are
> *sites of conflict* — and that the social–economic overlap is named
> **property conflict**, which is the whole reason this diagram is in a
> property law unit. Assessment 1 is a property conflict in miniature: a
> publicly-owned heritage asset (social value) with high redevelopment
> potential (economic value), and the resolution is legal.

### Yigitcanlar's own frameworks

Two of the lecturer's published models appear:

**Smart City — Input–Process–Output–Impact** (Yigitcanlar, 2018):

```
INPUT     (Assets)    Community · Technology · Policy
PROCESS   (Drivers)   Governance & Planning
OUTPUT    (Outcomes)  Productivity & Innovation (Economy)
IMPACT    (Results)   Sustainability & Accessibility (Environment)
                      Liveability & Wellbeing (Society)
```

with four surrounding domains: Sustainable Urban Development, Governance,
Knowledge-Based Urban Development, and Indigenous Knowledge and
Perspectives.

**Knowledge-Based Urban Development (K-BUD)** (Yigitcanlar, 2014) — four
development types, each with a "climate" and a product:

| Development | Climate | Producing | Quality dimension |
|---|---|---|---|
| Economic | Good business climate | **Prosperity** | Economic quality |
| Socio-cultural | Good people climate | **Equity** | Social quality |
| Spatial | Good spatial climate | **Sustainability** | Environmental quality |
| Institutional | Good governance climate | **Enablers** | Governance quality |

The layout diagrams for both are dense and the extracted text scrambles
them; the four-way structure above is what survives cleanly and is what you
would be asked to reproduce. If you need the precise arrangement, go to the
slide image rather than trusting a text transcript.

## 2.6 Density versus sprawl

The deck is careful to present this as a **live debate**, not a settled
policy fact — and that even-handedness is the marking key.

### The case against sprawl

Concerns are old, not new:

- **From the 1920s**, significant concern that urban development was
  destroying **agricultural land**, and that new suburbs created **travel
  time inefficiencies**.
- It was also getting **expensive to service far-flung suburbs** with
  essential infrastructure and services.
- The later sustainable development focus "brought new aspects into
  consideration."

### The case for density

**Newman & Kenworthy (1989)**, *Gasoline Consumption and Cities*, Journal
of the American Planning Association 55(1), 24–37 — "seminal research
showed cities with higher densities had reduced petrol consumption." The
deck says plainly that this "was a key argument in favour of policies to
create more compact cities."

A second exhibit comes from OECD (2012), *Compact City Policies: A
Comparative Assessment*.

### "Or perhaps not…"

The counter-case, in the deck's own words:

- Others argue compact urban forms **do not necessarily achieve these
  things**.
- These authors believe that **trying to change entire cities' urban form
  is not feasible**, and that other policy interventions could better
  achieve sustainability gains. (See Ewing & Hamidi (2015), *Compactness
  versus Sprawl*, Journal of Planning Literature 30(4), 413–432.)
- **Angel et al. (2010)** show urban densities are *actually decreasing* —
  "the persistent decline in urban densities: global and historical
  evidence of 'sprawl'."
- **Pandemics, lockdowns and corresponding mental health issues challenged
  high-density living.**

And yet: "compact city policies have been adopted by almost every major
OECD nation. Australia is no exception: metropolitan level policies
typically include policies to create a **network of compact activity
centres**. As to whether or not this is working is a different matter."

> **The compact city is a policy consensus resting on contested evidence.**
> That single sentence is the highest-value thing in Week 2. An answer that
> says "compact cities are more sustainable" scores lower than one that
> says "compact city policy is near-universal in the OECD, was driven by
> Newman & Kenworthy's petrol-consumption finding, is contested by Ewing &
> Hamidi and undercut by Angel's evidence that densities are still falling
> — and is implemented anyway through activity-centre networks."

### The missing middle

One slide, titled **"Missing Middle: Not Just Tall vs Sprawl."** The framing
matters: the density debate is usually posed as towers versus detached
houses, and the missing middle — townhouses, terraces, small apartment
buildings — is the excluded third option.

> **Assessment 2 is a missing-middle exercise, and saying so scores.** The
> brief asks for *small-scale residential multiple dwelling* on lots of
> 1,050–2,000 m² in **Low-medium density residential (2 storey mix)** and
> **(2 or 3 storey mix)** zones. That is precisely the missing middle: not a
> tower, not a detached house. Framing your introduction that way ties the
> GIS exercise to Week 2's theory and answers "why does this zone exist?"

### How the system delivers it

"The land use planning system plays a key role in attempts to make more
sustainable urban forms. Compact urban forms are considered to be more
sustainable, and are therefore common strategic policy objectives. Land use
planning is then used to try to implement the compact city by **managing
urban growth and development**."

Note the causal chain, and note that it is exactly Gurran's definition
running in the field:

```
strategic policy objective  →  formal regulatory process  →  built form
   (compact city)               (zone, code, overlay)        (outcome)
```

## 2.7 The evolution of property development

The second half of the lecture runs the same historical exercise for the
development industry.

**Property development as a process:** land assembly → finance → design →
construction → marketing → management. Developers are the "key actors
linking land markets, capital markets, and urban form."

| Era | What defined it |
|---|---|
| **Pre-industrial** | Land ownership, agrarian estates, feudal systems, incremental urban growth. Early cities driven by trade routes, ports and defensive needs |
| **Industrial revolution (18th–19th C)** | Rapid urbanisation and housing demand; emergence of **speculative development and private landlords**; infrastructure-led development (railways, factories, worker housing). **Key shift: land as a commodity and property as an investment asset** |
| **Early 20th C** | Zoning, planning regulations, professionalisation of development |
| **Post-WWII** | Mass housing, suburbanisation, automobile-oriented development; large-scale developers and institutional finance; public-sector involvement in housing and urban renewal. Modernist ideals of efficiency, **separation of land uses**, standardised building forms. Implications: scale, speed, **financial leverage** |
| **Late 20th – early 21st C** | Shift from industrial to service and knowledge economies; mixed-use, CBD renewal, waterfront regeneration; **financialisation** — REITs, global capital flows, portfolio investment, property as a global financial asset class; growth of risk management, feasibility analysis and development appraisal |
| **Contemporary** | Sustainable and resilient development — climate risk, **ESG**, green buildings; digital transformation — data analytics, **proptech**, AI in development decision-making; shifts in demand toward density, affordability, flexible uses, lifestyle |

The three lessons the deck draws from that history:

1. Property development is **cyclical and path-dependent**.
2. **Regulation, finance and technology** strongly shape outcomes.
3. Successful development **balances economic return with long-term urban
   value**.

> **"Path-dependent" is doing real work in Assessment 1.** 189 Lutwyche Road
> is a 1916 subdivision plan, a WWII-era military hospital, a 1999 heritage
> listing, a 2012 volumetric survey for a toll tunnel and a 2014 perpetual
> lease — every one of which constrains what can be built in 2026. That is
> path dependence in a single title. If you want one sentence to open the
> report's constraints section, this is it.

## 2.8 Conclusion, as the lecture puts it

- Urban planning and property development have evolved over the past century.
- The **blueprint approach proved not only difficult to implement, but can
  also lead to undesirable results**.
- **Plans should be the result of a process** that seeks to understand
  existing constraints and possibilities and guide direction toward common
  goals.
- **Sustainability goals have become widely accepted**, and making sprawling
  urban forms more compact is seen as a way to help achieve them.
- This has become a commonly prescribed objective in land use planning and
  property development.
- Plus the AI and digital revolution pushing further digitalisation of both
  sectors.

## Summary

- Planning was invented in response to **uncontrolled industrial
  urbanisation**; formalised interventions begin in **London from the
  1850s**, driven by **sanitation and public health**.
- Five "seers" drew optimal urban forms: **Howard** (garden city, green
  belts, use separation), **Perry** (neighbourhood unit contained by major
  roads), **Wright** (Broadacre, car-liberated dispersal), **Soria y Mata**
  (linear city along transit), **Le Corbusier** (towers in the park — same
  overall density, very high local density).
- **Blueprints fail** because implementing them requires an "omniscient
  ruler" unimpeded by private interests in land or participatory democracy,
  and because they impose preconceived solutions instead of discovering
  constraints.
- **Geddes' Survey → Analyse → Plan** survived because it is a method, not
  a picture. Later theory added implementation (rational comprehensive),
  process over plan (systems), and participation (communicative turn).
- **Sustainable development** (Brundtland 1987) puts environment, social and
  economic in tension; the three overlaps are **resource, development and
  property conflicts**.
- The **compact city** is near-universal OECD policy resting on contested
  evidence — Newman & Kenworthy for, Ewing & Hamidi and Angel against, and
  the pandemic against high-density living. Australia implements it through
  **networks of compact activity centres**.
- Property development history runs from **land as commodity** (industrial),
  through **mass suburbanisation and leverage** (post-war), to
  **financialisation** (REITs, global capital) and now **ESG and proptech**.
  It is **cyclical and path-dependent**.

<summary>✅ Checkpoint 2 — evolution, blueprints and the density debate</summary>

**Q1. Why does the lecture say the blueprint approach requires the planner to become an "omniscient ruler"?**

Because implementing a fixed ideal form requires constructing a new reality
unimpeded by two things that actually exist: private interests in land, and
the processes of participatory democracy. Real land is owned in fee simple
by people with enforceable rights, and real plans must go through
consultation and statutory process. A blueprint can only be delivered by
someone able to override both — which is why the Corbusian projects that
were built were built by states over people who had no say.

**Q2. State the density debate in a balanced way, naming at least three sources.**

Newman & Kenworthy (1989) found higher-density cities consumed less petrol,
which became the key argument for compact city policy, and OECD (2012)
assessed those policies comparatively. Against this, Ewing & Hamidi (2015)
review evidence that compact form does not necessarily deliver the claimed
gains and argue that reshaping whole cities is infeasible relative to other
policy levers, while Angel et al. (2010) show urban densities are in
persistent global decline. Pandemic lockdowns further challenged
high-density living. Despite the contested evidence, compact city policy has
been adopted by almost every major OECD nation, Australia included, usually
via networks of compact activity centres.

**Q3. What is the "missing middle" and why is it relevant to this unit's assessment?**

The missing middle is the range of housing between detached dwellings and
high-rise — townhouses, terraces, small apartment buildings — excluded when
the debate is framed as "tall vs sprawl." It is relevant because Assessment
2 screens for *small-scale residential multiple dwelling* in Low-medium
density residential zones on 1,050–2,000 m² lots, which is exactly the
missing-middle product. The zone exists because policy is trying to deliver
that band of density.

**Q4. Draw the sustainable development Venn and name the three conflicts.**

Three circles — Environment, Social, Economic.
Environment ∩ Economic = **resource conflict**.
Environment ∩ Social = **development conflict**.
Social ∩ Economic = **property conflict**.

**Q5. Apply Survey–Analyse–Plan to Assessment 1 in one line each.**

*Survey* — obtain and read the three title searches, both survey plans, the
BCC property holding and lot reports, and the Queensland Heritage Register
entry.
*Analyse* — establish surface freehold and the State's volumetric perpetual
lease, the registered encumbrances, the heritage controls, and the zoning
and overlay envelope, and work out how they interact spatially.
*Plan* — only then propose a preliminary highest and best use, showing that
it follows from the constraints rather than preceding them.

**Q6. Which of the five seers' ideas is most visible in the Lutwyche Road corridor neighbourhood plan, and why?**

Soria y Mata's linear city. The plan concentrates intensification along a
transit corridor to maximise the return on transport investment while
managing the land either side of it — long, continuous, corridor-shaped
development structured by a movement line. Perry's neighbourhood unit is
also visible in the precinct structure (NPP-002, with sub-precincts), which
subdivides the corridor into service catchments bounded by major roads.
