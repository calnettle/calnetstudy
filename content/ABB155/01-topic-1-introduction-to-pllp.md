# TOPIC 1 — Introduction to Property Law and Land Use Planning

Week 1. What the two halves of this unit actually are, why society lets
planners restrict what you do with your own land, and the chain that runs
from human values to a line on a zoning map.

## 1.1 The two subjects in the unit title

ABB155 welds together two disciplines that are usually taught apart. The
Week 1 deck states the relationship in one sentence, and it is worth
memorising because it is the cleanest thing you can put in an introduction:

> "Statutory planning is commonly used to implement land use plans, where
> property law defines who owns what and how property can be used,
> transferred, and protected."

So:

| | Property law | Land use planning |
|---|---|---|
| **Answers** | *Who* holds what rights in this land? | *What* may be done on this land, and *why*? |
| **Operates through** | Torrens registration, estates, interests, encumbrances | Planning schemes, zones, overlays, codes, development assessment |
| **Instrument** | The title | The planning scheme |
| **In Queensland** | *Land Title Act 1994*, *Property Law Act 1974*, *Land Act 1994* | *Planning Act 2016*, *Planning Regulation 2017*, Brisbane City Plan 2014 |
| **Assessed in** | Assessment 1 | Assessment 2 |

Neither answers the other's question. That is the point of putting them in
one unit: a site can be legally yours and still undevelopable, or zoned for
twelve storeys and still unbuildable because of what sits under it. Note 04
is a site where both are true at once.

## 1.2 What is (real) property law?

The lecture's definition:

> "(Real) Property law governs the ownership, use, control, and transfer of
> land and other assets on it. It defines legal rights and obligations
> between individuals, organisations, and the state, regulating how real
> property is acquired, shared, protected, and resolved in cases of dispute
> to ensure certainty, fairness, and social order."

Four verbs to hold onto — property law **defines**, **regulates**,
**protects**, **resolves**.

In Queensland specifically, the deck states that property law operates
under the **Torrens title system**, primarily through:

```
Land Title Act 1994 (Qld)      — registration, indefeasibility, dealings
Property Law Act 1974 (Qld)    — leases, easements, covenants, co-ownership
```

and that it "regulates ownership, registration, transfer, leases,
easements, and mortgages, recognises native title where applicable, and
provides mechanisms for resolving land-related disputes within a statutory
and common law framework."

### Why we need it

The deck's answer is essentially an argument from chaos:

- **Define** ownership
- **Protect** rights
- **Regulate** use and transfer
- Provide **legal certainty**
- **Prevent disputes**
- Ensure **fairness**
- **Balance individual interests with public welfare**

That last one is the hinge into planning. Property law already contains the
idea that private rights are limited by public welfare; planning is just the
systematic, forward-looking version of that limitation.

> **Do not write "property law protects owners" and stop there.** Half the
> function in the lecture's own list is *limiting* owners — regulation,
> fairness, balancing against public welfare. A report that treats title as
> a pure grant of freedom will get the Assessment 1 tenure criterion wrong,
> because on a site like 189 Lutwyche Road the interesting content is
> entirely in the limits.

## 1.3 What is land use planning?

The unit adopts Gurran's working definition, and it is the single most
quotable line in the first half of the semester:

> "A formal process regulating the use of land and the development of the
> built environment, in order to achieve strategic policy objectives."
> — Gurran, N. (2011), *Australian Urban Land Use Planning: Principles,
> systems and practice*, Sydney University Press.

Pull it apart, because each phrase does work:

| Phrase | What it commits you to |
|---|---|
| **formal process** | Statutory, procedural, reviewable — not ad hoc discretion |
| **regulating the use of land** | Zoning, use definitions, tables of assessment |
| **development of the built environment** | Built form controls: height, setbacks, site cover, GFA |
| **in order to achieve strategic policy objectives** | Regulation is a means, not an end; there must be a *plan* behind the rule |

The deck flags immediately that this is a definition of how planning **is
commonly practised**, and that there is longstanding debate about how it
*ought* to be defined. The debate is, at heart, about the relative weight of
**"the plan" vs "the process"** of plan-making — which is exactly the
argument Hall's chapter 1 sets out, and which Topic 2 then traces
historically.

> **"The plan vs the process" is the exam-friendly framing of Week 1 and 2
> together.** Week 1 gives you the tension; Week 2 gives you the historical
> evidence — the blueprint makers were plan-people and they failed, and
> Geddes was a process-person and his method survived. If a question asks
> you to evaluate a planning approach, this is the axis to evaluate it on.

## 1.4 Justifying constraints on liberty

This is the ethical core of the lecture and it is a four-step argument.
Learn the steps, because they are how you *justify* a planning control in
writing rather than just describing it.

```
1. As a general principle, we accept that ownership confers certain rights.
2. We also accept that an individual's actions should be limited where they
   negatively impact on others.
3. On Gurran's definition, land use planning will typically limit rights in
   the use of land, for the benefit of others (human or non-human).
4. Therefore those benefits must be accepted as sufficient to justify the
   intervention.
```

Step 4 is the one students skip. Every planning control is a live claim
that some public benefit outweighs a private right. If you cannot name the
benefit, you cannot defend the control.

**Worked application — the Transport noise corridor overlay.**
189 Lutwyche Road carries the *Noise corridor — Brisbane: Queensland
Development Code MP4.4 Noise Category 3* sub-category. Run the four steps:

```
1. The owner has a fee simple right to build residential floorspace.
2. Residents exposed to sustained road traffic noise suffer sleep
   disturbance and health effects — an impact on others, being the future
   occupants.
3. The overlay limits the right by requiring acoustic construction
   standards, the noise categories being established under Chapter 8B of
   the Building Act 1975 (Qld).
4. The benefit — habitable amenity for hundreds of future dwellings — is
   accepted as outweighing the added construction cost.
```

Written that way it is an argument. Written as "the site is affected by a
transport noise corridor overlay" it is a list item. The Assessment 1 CRA
is explicit that a 3 or below "tends to describe issues rather than
critically examine their implications."

> **Note the source of the noise category.** The Transport noise corridor
> overlay is a City Plan overlay, but the noise categories themselves are
> established under **Chapter 8B of the *Building Act 1975* (Qld)** — state
> legislation, mapped by the State for state-controlled roads. Attributing
> the standard to Brisbane City Council is a jurisdictional error, and the
> Assessment 1 CRA sends "jurisdictional errors" straight to the 2/1 band.

## 1.5 Values → activities → land use

The deck's second conceptual thread. The chain is:

```
values  →  activities  →  land use
```

- **All human activity involves the use of land (space).** Nothing you plan
  for is spaceless.
- There is a connection between what people **value**, the **activities**
  they therefore undertake, and the **land uses** those activities require.
- "A focus on land use without a consideration of the underlying values
  risks the implementation of simplistic, preconceived 'solutions'."

The lecturer then makes the point experientially, with three slides asking
how you feel viewing a city (a) from the top of a tall building, (b) in
plan view from City Plan 2014, and (c) on foot. The pedagogical point is
that **GIS and plan-view analysis abstract you away from human
experience**, and the deck says so directly: "We're going to be looking at
big picture issues and doing lots of technical analysis using GIS. This
perspective can isolate you from human experience."

> **This is not filler — it is the answer to a likely "reflect on the
> limitations of your GIS analysis" question.** Assessment 2 asks you to
> screen 420,934 lots on four attributes. Nothing in that method can see
> whether a street is pleasant to walk down, whether the neighbours will
> object, or whether the existing dwelling is loved. Naming that limitation
> explicitly in your Section 5.2 is worth more than another due-diligence
> bullet, and it ties your GIS report back to Week 1.

Worth knowing because it tells you where the marks are hiding.

## Summary

- The unit joins two questions: **property law asks who holds what rights;
  planning asks what may be done and why.** Property law defines who owns
  what; statutory planning implements land use plans.
- Queensland property law runs on the **Torrens system**, primarily the
  ***Land Title Act 1994*** and ***Property Law Act 1974***.
- Land use planning is "**a formal process regulating the use of land and
  the development of the built environment, in order to achieve strategic
  policy objectives**" (Gurran, 2011). Every clause of that definition is
  load-bearing.
- Planning restricts liberty, so it needs justification: rights exist →
  actions may be limited where they harm others → planning limits land use
  rights for others' benefit → **those benefits must be shown**.
- There is a chain from **values → activities → land use**. Skip the values
  and you get preconceived "solutions" — the failure mode Topic 2 documents.
- Plan-view and GIS analysis is powerful and dehumanising at the same time.
  Say so in your reports.

<summary>✅ Checkpoint 1 — concepts and definitions</summary>

**Q1. Give Gurran's definition of land use planning and explain what work the final clause does.**

"A formal process regulating the use of land and the development of the
built environment, in order to achieve strategic policy objectives."

The final clause — *in order to achieve strategic policy objectives* —
makes regulation instrumental rather than an end in itself. It means every
control must trace back to a stated objective in the strategic framework,
and it is what distinguishes planning from arbitrary restriction. It is also
what makes a control challengeable: if no objective supports it, the control
is unjustified.

**Q2. A client asks why the council can stop them demolishing their own building when they hold freehold title. Answer in the lecture's four steps.**

1. Ownership confers rights — freehold is the largest estate and includes a
   right to use and to create lesser interests.
2. But we accept that an individual's actions may be limited where they
   negatively impact others.
3. Land use planning (and, here, heritage legislation) limits the use of
   land for the benefit of others — in this case the public interest in
   cultural heritage, which is a benefit to people who do not own the site.
4. That benefit has been accepted by Parliament as sufficient to justify the
   intervention, through the *Queensland Heritage Act 1992*. The right was
   never absolute; it was always a bundle held from the Crown.

**Q3. Why does the deck insist that "all human activity involves the use of land"?**

Because it makes land use planning unavoidable rather than optional. If
every activity consumes space, then allocating space allocates the ability
to undertake activities — so planning decisions are decisions about which
human activities are possible, not merely about maps. It is also the
premise for the values → activities → land use chain: to plan land use
properly you have to reason backwards to the activities and the values.

**Q4. Name two Queensland statutes that the Week 1 deck identifies as the primary vehicles of property law, and one thing each does.**

*Land Title Act 1994* (Qld) — establishes the Torrens register: registration
of title and of dealings, and indefeasibility of the registered interest.

*Property Law Act 1974* (Qld) — governs leases, easements, covenants and
co-ownership; the general law of dealings in land outside the register
mechanics.

(Accept also *Land Act 1994* (Qld) for State land and leasehold tenure,
which the Week 3 lecture adds.)

**Q5. Your GIS analysis produces two "most suitable" lots. Using Week 1's argument, name a limitation of that result that no amount of extra spatial data would fix.**

Suitability was defined entirely by measurable attributes — zone, tenure,
flood, area, unit count. The analysis has no access to the underlying
*values* that make a place good to live in or a development welcome:
neighbourhood character as experienced on foot, community attachment to the
existing dwelling, the quality of the street. Plan-view analysis abstracts
away from human experience by design, so adding more layers deepens the
abstraction rather than curing it. The fix is a site visit and consultation,
not more data.
