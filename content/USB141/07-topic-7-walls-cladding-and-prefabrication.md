# Topic 7 — Walls, Cladding and Prefabrication

Week 7. Formwork, the portal frame and its components, tilt-up panels,
façades and cladding, and then prefabrication, modular construction and mass
timber.

## Formwork

```
In concrete construction, formwork is the term given to either temporary
or permanent moulds into which concrete is poured.
```

"What is formwork?" is on the revision deck as a question in its own right —
so learn that sentence, including **"temporary or permanent"**.

**Requirements of formwork** — eight, each a single word plus a clause:

| Requirement | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Strength** | The ability to resist all applied loads |
| **Stiffness** | The ability to withstand deformation under load |
| **Accuracy** | Should correctly form component dimensions |
| **Water tightness** | Should not leak |
| **Robustness** | The ability to withstand repeated use |
| **Ease of stripping** | Able to be easily removed without damaging the formwork or the concrete |
| **Standardisation** | Avoids unnecessary cutting and wastage |
| **Safety** | Provides a safe working environment for site personnel |

**Types of formwork:** ground forms · soffit forms · beam forms · column
forms · wall forms · stair forms

**Form materials:** solid sawn timber sections · manufactured timber sections,
e.g. plywood · steel, aluminium · cardboard

> **Strength and stiffness are not the same requirement.** Strength resists
> *failure* under load; stiffness resists *deformation* under load. Formwork
> can be strong enough not to break and still bow enough to ruin the finished
> face of the concrete. Both are on the list, and a good answer distinguishes
> them.

## The portal frame

The industrial structure, revisited from Week 1 with its components — which
is where the label-the-diagram mark lives.

**Characteristics**

- Generally constructed from **structural steelwork**
- Provides **clear open space** inside the building
- **Quick and easy** construction
- **Cranes can be included in the design**
- Wall types — **tilt-up concrete or steel cladding**
- **Flexible building dimensions** to suit the client's requirements

**Truss portal frame**

- Trusses can be used in the **rafters and ridge** of a portal frame to
  achieve **the largest spans of any type of portal frame**
- Typically used in **commercial warehouses**

### Portal frame components

| Component | Function |
|---|---|
| **Rafter** | Transfers load from roof sheeting and self weight to **column** to **foundations** |
| **Ridge** | A **moment-resisting connection** provided at the crown to resist **lateral and gravity loadings** |
| **Knee joint** | The main design area — to **resist the moment of wind** |
| **Column** | A **vertical** frame member; transfers load from roof to column then foundations |
| **Haunch** | Haunching the rafter is done **in lieu of a bolted connection**; increases the **section strength** — and the major function is **increasing the bending resistance of a rafter** |
| **Mullions** | Support the **end wall girts** where the frame span is too large for the girt to span between main columns |
| **Purlins** | **Roof** members used to support **roof sheeting**; run **perpendicular to and are supported on rafters** |
| **Girts** | **Wall** members used to support **wall cladding**; run **perpendicular to and are supported on columns and mullions** |

> **Purlins are roof, girts are wall — and both run perpendicular to what
> supports them.** Purlin → rafter → roof sheeting. Girt → column/mullion →
> wall cladding. This is the single most reliable two-mark pair in the topic
> and it is trivially easy to invert under time pressure. **P**urlin =
> **P**erpendicular on rafters, roof; **G**irt = wall.

> **The knee joint and the ridge both resist moments, for different loads.**
> The **knee** is designed for the **moment from wind**; the **ridge** is a
> moment-resisting connection at the crown resisting **lateral and gravity**
> loading. The **haunch** is what makes the knee work without a large bolted
> connection, by deepening the rafter section where the bending moment peaks.

### Bracing

- Required to **transmit forces to foundation level**
- Usually provided **in the roof and in the walls**:
  - **Bracing in the roof** — required to transmit forces from the **tops of
    the mullions to the knees**
  - **Bracing in the walls** — required to transmit forces **at the knees
    (from above) to foundation level**
- **As soon as the structure is able to pick up lateral loads, bracing is
  required**:
  - **Temporary bracing** may be required during the construction phase,
    particularly with a system of concrete and masonry panels
  - **Even unclad structures have a sizeable surface area** on which to
    collect lateral wind load

> **The load path for wind is the answer to "why brace?"** Wind hits the end
> wall → the mullions carry it up → **roof bracing** takes it from the tops
> of the mullions to the **knees** → **wall bracing** takes it from the knees
> down to the **foundations**. Two bracing planes, one continuous path.
> Write the path, not just the word "bracing".

## Roof cladding and gutters

**Roof cladding**

- External shell to the building providing **protection from the elements**
- For industrial buildings this is usually **steel cladding**
- Generally the **roofing sub-contractor** supplies and installs the whole
  cladding system including **flashings, gutters, downpipes and other
  accessories**

**Gutters — design consideration:** roof slope and other penetrations that
reduce the capacity of the profile to carry rain efficiently to the gutters.

## Tilt-up panels

```
Tilt-up = the construction of concrete walls in a horizontal, slab-like
condition, which are then lifted (or tilted) into the final vertical
position. Generally pre-cast.
```

### Tilt-up versus masonry

The competitor comparison, straight from the slide:

| | Tilt-up | Masonry |
|---|---|---|
| Cost | Lower | **Generally more costly** |
| Speed | Faster | **Generally takes longer to build** |
| Scaffolding | Minimal | **Requires scaffolding when the height gets restrictive** |
| Method | Cast flat, then tilted; generally pre-cast | Laid unit by unit |

### Pre-cast panels

- Made **off-site** and trucked to location
- **Size limitations** (transport)
- Can be **quicker and stronger**, and **not weather dependent**
- **Same installation method** as site-cast tilt-up

> **"Tilt-up" and "pre-cast" are not synonyms, though the deck says tilt-up is
> generally pre-cast.** The distinction that matters is *where it was cast*:
> site-cast tilt-up panels are cast on the slab of the building itself and
> lifted straight up; pre-cast panels are cast in a factory and trucked in,
> which lifts quality and removes weather risk but imposes transport size
> limits. The lifting and bracing method is identical.

### Lifting problems

Potential problems encountered during lifting — eight of them, and they are
the reason the safety slides follow:

1. Panel may **crack**
2. Panel may **stick to the slab** (or to the panel below)
3. **Crane capacity may be inadequate**
4. **Tangled cables**
5. Panel does not **hang plumb**
6. Panel does not **fit into position**
7. Panel does not **sit level**
8. **Lift inserts break loose**

### Bracing of tilt-up panels

- **Prior to tilting**, temporary braces are attached to the panel
- Once erected, the panels **must be temporarily braced against wind and
  other lateral forces until all final structural connections are completed**
- **Telescoping pipe braces** are commonly used

### Safety — the 6 October 2016 incident

From the deck, in the words of the report:

- Three panels were already in place and Mr D'Alessandro was on site
  overseeing the fourth being lowered into position.
- *"One panel began to fall forward and the two workers managed to scale the
  ladder on that wall and ride it down and jumped on to its back as it
  fell."*
- *"The failure of this wall caused another wall to immediately fall forward
  as it was no longer supported."*
- *"The two workers were unable to escape the second wall falling and were
  crushed to death between the two panels."*

Related outcomes cited: a **manslaughter prosecution** for concrete wall
panel deaths (2016); **Axedale Holdings fined $160,000** over the death of two
construction workers in WA; a builder fined over a tilt-up collapse in Perth.

> **Progressive collapse is the mechanism, and it is what the bracing
> prevents.** One unbraced panel falls; it was propping the next; the next
> falls too. The panels are only self-supporting once **all final structural
> connections are completed** — until then, telescoping pipe braces are
> structure, not scaffolding. If a question asks why tilt-up panels are
> braced, "wind" is half the answer and "until final connections are made"
> is the other half.

### Architectural tilt-up

Most people associate tilt-up with big warehouses and retail box stores, but
panels offer decorative possibilities:

- An unlimited array of **colourings added to the concrete mix**, or textured
  paints
- **Textures** from improvised techniques and **form-liner cast surfaces** —
  striated, fractured fin, stone, brick, woodgrains
- **Exposed aggregate** and mechanical tooling surface treatments
- Simple forming on the casting surface can produce **three-dimensional
  surface effects**

Examples: Bishop Gadsden Church, Charleston SC — tilt-up panels with a tabby
finish and detailed quoins to match historic details on the existing church,
avoiding the expense of hand-laid masonry and oyster shell stucco. The Sibaya
Casino, Durban, South Africa.

## Façades

Three dictionary definitions from the Australian Oxford, quoted on the slide:

- **Fenestration** — arrangement of windows in a building
- **Glazing** — fitting (a window) with glass, or (a building) with windows
- **Glazier** — one whose trade is to glaze windows

**Fundamentals of fenestration** — seven, a ready-made list:

1. Thermal performance and solar control
2. Water and wind resistance
3. Visual aesthetics
4. Acoustics
5. Safety
6. Security
7. Durability and maintenance

### Wall cladding

- Cladding is an **extra layer on the outside of a building**
- Types: **stone cladding, brick cladding, vinyl cladding**
- Offers a **wide range of colours and styles**
- Makes a building exterior more attractive (**also for renovation projects**)
- **Mainly used to stop wind and rain from entering the building**
- Can also provide **sound and thermal insulation as well as fire
  resistance**

### Aluminium composite panels (ACP)

- **Sandwich-type panels consisting of two aluminium faces and a core
  material**
- Marketed as architectural building panels
- A number of different products on the market **appear outwardly similar**
- **The core material affects the fire performance of the panel**
- Materials with a **higher proportion of mineral core** are generally
  considered to have **better fire performance** than those with a
  **polyethylene core** or a low proportion mineral core

**Grenfell Tower, London (2017)**

- Claimed the lives of **72 people**
- Highlighted the serious fire risk posed by combustible aluminium cladding,
  **particularly when retrofitted to older buildings**
- The primary cause of the fire's swift spread is said to have been the
  external cladding's composite panels, which **despite being banned in the
  UK contained polyethylene material which is highly flammable**

**Princess Alexandra Hospital, Brisbane** — the external cladding project was
completed in **January 2021** after **17,500 m²** of non-compliant aluminium
cladding was removed and replaced.

> **The danger is invisible from the outside, and that is the examinable
> point.** Two ACPs that "appear outwardly similar" can behave completely
> differently in a fire because the difference is the **core**: mineral core
> performs, polyethylene core burns. You cannot assess ACP risk by looking at
> a building — you need the product specification. That is why the PA
> Hospital had to strip 17,500 m² rather than inspect it.

## Prefabrication

**Terms used:** offsite construction · offsite manufacturing · modular
housing · prefabricated housing/building · **volumetric** building

**Definition:** offsite manufacture for construction is a
**manufacturing-based approach** which involves the **production of
components of buildings in a factory for installation on site**.

It is increasingly associated with **greater use of digital technologies**:
**BIM**, Geographic Information Systems (**GIS**) and **3D printing** allow
virtual access to complex information about a building's specific components
and installation requirements, meaning **problems in the manufacturing
process can be identified and addressed early**.

### History

- Modular housing is often viewed negatively by consumers, builders,
  investors and insurers alike, based on an enduring image of **low-quality
  post-World War II prefabricated housing**.
- Those units were widely perceived as **poor quality and design, not built to
  last**.
- There were many cases of 'factory-made' houses found to have **defects**, and
  some **collapsed**, causing **risk to life and increased homelessness**.

Two historic examples:

- **Lustron House, 1949** — all **3,000 parts** could be shipped from the
  factory in Columbus, Ohio in a **single 35-foot tandem trailer** and be
  **site-assembled in 350 hours**.
- **Million Homes Programme, Sweden** — built **1965–1974** across the whole
  of Sweden, named for the objective of providing **one million new rental
  apartments** for a growing population.

### Prefabricated versus modular

The line the revision deck reproduces verbatim:

```
All modular construction is prefabricated,
but not all prefab construction is modular.
```

| | Prefabrication | Modular |
|---|---|---|
| What it is | Something **constructed off-site with the intent to be put together somewhere else** — window panels, walls, staircases, floors, bathroom pods | A building **constructed off-site using these prefabricated pieces**, allowing it to be installed on site **with little extra construction work** |
| On-site work | Still significant assembly | Minimal — units are craned in |
| Relationship | The superset | The subset |

### Modular construction

- The building's components (**modules**) are **constructed in a factory
  before assembly on site**
- This includes **framing, cladding, services, fittings and finishes**
  assembled in factory conditions

**What modular is for:**

- To **minimise the on-site building activity / use of trades**
- To **minimise the potential for rework on site**
- The optimum mix and degree of these objectives **varies from project to
  project**
- Where the concept is **maximised to deliver structurally complete building
  units to site, it is described as volumetric**

**Why modular — the sustainability case:**

Buildings expend about **30% of the world's resources** in construction,
consume approximately **40% of global energy** and produce approximately
**40% of total greenhouse gas emissions** (Green Building Council of
Australia, 2008). This is because such structures are still built on-site
using **traditional craft-based, labour-intensive methodology**, resulting in
ever-increasing costs and unacceptable consequences for quality, safety and
environmental impact — while almost all other industries have been advanced
with **automated processes**.

### Case study: Holiday Inn Express, Trafford, Manchester

- **220-bed** hotel constructed from purpose-built **steel containers**
  complete with factory-finished interior fixtures and fittings, all installed
  **before delivery to site**
- Modules were **stacked on top of each other without additional supporting
  structures**, enabling fast installation
- Each module consists of **two bedrooms and a section of corridor**
- All **220 bedrooms placed on site in less than three weeks**, ready for
  external cladding to commence
- The external envelope was applied **once the modules were in place**, using
  **pre-finished rainscreen cladding and single-ply roofing systems** fixed to
  specific areas of the module to maintain an **airtight seal**

### Opportunities and risks

| **Opportunities** | **Risks and challenges** |
|---|---|
| Faster, cheaper to build | **Higher costs of investment** |
| Tackle labour shortages | Embracing new business models |
| Minimise onsite reworking | Reduction in local economic benefits |
| Higher quality | Lack of evidence of benefits |
| Health and safety benefits in production and asset management | **Consumer perceptions** |
| Reduced local disruption | **Issues of customisation** |
| New skills | **Risk-averse insurers and lenders** |
| New geographies of production | Resistance to change |
| Collaborative business models | **Lack of regulation, insurance and standards** |
| New aesthetics and greater diversity | **Storage, transport to site and onsite fit issues** |
| Reduced carbon emissions | |

> **"Faster and cheaper to build" sits opposite "higher costs of
> investment", and both are true.** The *unit* is cheaper and quicker; the
> *factory* is a large upfront capital commitment that only pays back at
> volume. That tension — plus risk-averse insurers, the customisation
> problem and the post-war reputation — is why Australian uptake lags. A
> question asking for advantages *and* disadvantages wants that pairing, not
> ten items from one column.

## Mass timber

### First Tech Federal Credit Union, Portland, Oregon

- Mass timber components are pre-manufactured to suit the building's design,
  so **on-site construction time can be reduced by up to 25%** compared with
  traditional concrete and steel methods
- In **2023** it was the **largest glue-laminated timber (glulam) and
  cross-laminated timber (CLT) building in the United States**
- Construction time was reduced by **four months** compared with a
  conventional steel-framed structure
- The construction site was also **safer**, since most work was done in a
  factory, using a **wider range of skills** and offering improved career
  paths

### 25 King Street, Bowen Hills, Brisbane

The local case study, and the one to quote.

- Showcases **timber from roof to floor**
- **Nine storeys plus ground** superstructure
- Uses a combination of **cross-laminated timber (CLT)** and **glue-laminated
  timber (glulam)**
- **Glulam for the structural beams and columns; CLT for the floors, lift
  shafts and escape stairs**
- The engineered timber has a **lower carbon footprint** and is **sourced from
  certified sustainably-managed forests**
- Allows **precise offsite prefabrication and safer onsite construction**
- **Timber was chosen for its lightweight properties**
- Delivers a development that is low carbon, low waste, aesthetically
  attractive, highly energy efficient, cost effective, and promotes occupant
  wellbeing

**Process implications:**

- Requires **early documentation to enable prefabrication**, a different
  approach to resolving the design, coordinating procurement and services, and
  finalising details **ahead of construction**
- Produced a significantly **cleaner, quieter and safer** construction site
  with minimal waste and very little disturbance to neighbouring high-density
  residents
- **On-floor workers were typically reduced from 60 to 12**, with entire
  levels built in as little as **eight days**, and the building constructed in
  **15 months** — **69 fewer days of impact**, a **reduction of 20%** compared
  with comparable steel/concrete buildings

**Sustainability ratings achieved:**

| Rating | Result |
|---|---|
| Green Star Design and As Built v1.1 | **6 Star** |
| Climate Active | **Carbon Neutrality** |
| NABERS Energy | **6 Star** |
| NABERS Water | **4.5 Star** |
| NABERS Waste | **3.0 Star** |

Performance: saves **74% embodied carbon** compared with equivalent concrete
buildings, with a **46% reduction in energy** and a **29% reduction in potable
water** consumption.

> **Glulam is linear, CLT is planar — that is how you remember which goes
> where.** Glulam = **beams and columns** (laminations run one way, so it
> works as a stick). CLT = **floors, lift shafts, escape stairs** (layers
> cross at right angles, so it works as a plate). Getting these the wrong way
> round in a 25 King Street answer is an easy mark to drop.

> **The 60-to-12 figure is the one to quote.** Reducing on-floor workers from
> 60 to 12 is the clearest single illustration of the whole DfMA/prefab
> argument: labour moves off site into the factory, the site gets quieter and
> safer, and the programme shortens by 20%.

### Health benefits of timber

- Natural insulation properties **reduce internal heat gain in summer and
  loss of warmth in winter**
- Timber can **store and release moisture**, helping regulate **humidity and
  air quality** indoors
- The **natural visual appeal** of timber in the workplace helps **lower blood
  pressure and stress levels**
- Adding natural elements is associated with **increased wellbeing and higher
  productivity**
- A survey of Australian workers in workplaces with exposed timber found
  **higher levels of satisfaction, concentration and improved mood**

<summary>3. Trace the wind load path through a braced portal frame.</summary>

Wind on the end wall → carried by the **mullions** → **roof bracing** takes

takes it from the knees down to **foundation level**. Note also that bracing

<details>
<summary>4. Give four differences between tilt-up panels and masonry.</summary>

Tilt-up is **cheaper**, **faster to build**, needs **minimal scaffolding**, and
is **cast flat then tilted** (generally pre-cast). Masonry is more costly,
slower, **requires scaffolding as height becomes restrictive**, and is laid
unit by unit. A fifth: tilt-up carries a significant **lifting safety hazard**

<details>
<summary>5. Two aluminium composite panels look identical on a building. Why might one be safe and the other not?</summary>

sandwich panels of two aluminium faces around a core; **higher-proportion
mineral cores** perform better in fire, while **polyethylene cores** are
highly flammable. Grenfell Tower (2017, 72 deaths) spread rapidly because of
polyethylene-cored panels; Princess Alexandra Hospital removed and replaced
**17,500 m²** of non-compliant cladding by January 2021.
</details>

<details>
<summary>6. State the relationship between prefabrication and modular construction, and give one advantage and one disadvantage.</summary>

**All modular construction is prefabricated, but not all prefabricated
construction is modular.** Prefabrication is any component made off-site
(wall panels, staircases, bathroom pods); modular is a building assembled
from those pieces into complete volumetric units installed with little extra
site work. Advantage: faster, higher quality, safer, less on-site rework and
disruption. Disadvantage: higher upfront capital investment, plus consumer
perception, customisation limits, risk-averse insurers and lenders, and

</details>

## Summary

- **Formwork** = temporary or permanent moulds into which concrete is poured;
  eight requirements, of which strength and stiffness are distinct.
- **Portal frame**: steel, clear span, quick, flexible; components are rafter,
  ridge, knee joint, column, haunch, mullions, **purlins (roof)** and **girts
  (wall)**. Trusses in the rafters give the longest spans.
- **Bracing** carries wind from mullions → roof bracing → knees → wall bracing
  → foundations, and temporary bracing is needed during construction.
- **Tilt-up** is cast horizontally then tilted; cheaper, faster and needs less
  scaffolding than masonry, but must be **temporarily braced until all final
  structural connections are complete** — the 2016 progressive collapse killed
  two workers and produced a manslaughter prosecution.
- **Cladding** stops wind and rain and adds thermal, acoustic and fire
  performance; **ACP fire performance depends on the core**, mineral good,
  polyethylene dangerous — Grenfell (72 deaths) and PA Hospital (17,500 m²
  replaced).
- **All modular is prefab; not all prefab is modular.** Modular minimises
  on-site activity and rework; maximised to complete units it is called
  **volumetric**.
- **Mass timber**: glulam for beams and columns, CLT for floors and shafts.
  25 King Street — 9 storeys plus ground, 15 months, 60 to 12 on-floor
  workers, 20% faster, 6 Star Green Star, 6 Star NABERS Energy, 74% embodied
  carbon saving.
