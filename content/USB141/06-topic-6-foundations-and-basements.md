# Topic 6 — Foundations, Retaining Walls and Basements

Week 6. The terminology block that generates definition questions, the three

## What foundations do

From Marshall et al. (2013), quoted in the deck:

> The foundations of houses must carry the dead loads of the walls, the roof,
> the floors, etc., together with the imposed loads of occupants and
> furniture, as well as snow, and transmit them safely into the ground. They
> must be designed so that settlement is sufficiently controlled to keep any
> distortion (and possibly cracking) within acceptable limits.

Two jobs, then: **transmit load safely into the ground**, and **control
settlement** so distortion and cracking stay within acceptable limits.

## Terminology

The Week 12 revision deck reproduces this list twice. It is the highest-yield
definition block in the unit — assume at least one of these is worth two
marks.

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| **Bearing capacity** | The load which the sub-soil **can carry** |
| **Bearing pressure** | The pressure **on** the sub-soil caused by the building load |
| **Compaction** | The act of increasing the density and strength of a **material** by the application of **impact forces**, e.g. a heavy roller |
| **Consolidation** | The act of increasing the density and strength of the **sub-soil** by the **expulsion of water and air** under **self-weight and constant external loading** |
| **Dead load** | The load exerted on the sub-soil by the **structure** of a building plus its **permanent fixtures and fittings** |
| **Imposed load** | The load exerted on the sub-soil by the **contents** of a building |
| **Heave** | **Expansion** of the soil due to climatic and other factors, e.g. frost or removal of trees |
| **Settlement** | **Downward movement** of the ground, or any structure on it, due to **soil consolidation as a result of the load applied by the structure** |
| **Subsidence** | **Downward movement** of the ground, or any building on it, due to **changes within the sub-soil** |
| **Soil creep** | The **slow movement of rock and soil down a slope** under the influence of gravity, often triggered by high water content |

> **Capacity versus pressure is the pairing to get right.** Bearing
> **capacity** is a property of the *soil* — what it can take. Bearing
> **pressure** is a property of the *building* — what it is applying. Design
> is simply: keep the pressure below the capacity. Answering "bearing
> capacity is the pressure the building applies" loses the mark outright.

> **Compaction versus consolidation is the other pairing, and there are three
> differences.**
>
> | | Compaction | Consolidation |
> |---|---|---|
> | Cause | Mechanical **impact force** (roller) | **Self-weight and constant external load** |
> | Timing | **Immediate** | **Over time** |
> | Mechanism | Densifies the **material** | Expels **water and air** from the **sub-soil** |
>
> Compaction is something you *do* to a material; consolidation is something
> that *happens* to the sub-soil under load.

> **Settlement versus subsidence: same direction, different cause.** Both are
> downward movement. **Settlement is caused by the load the building applies**
> (consolidation under that load). **Subsidence is caused by changes within
> the sub-soil** — mine workings, dissolution, groundwater withdrawal,
> desiccation — and would happen with or without your building. And **heave is
> the opposite direction**: expansion, from frost or from removing a tree that
> had been drying the clay out.

## Residential foundations — three basic types

```
1. Isolated    — pads, piers and piles
2. Continuous  — strip, beam
3. Integrated  — raft slabs, pier and beam
```

Three families, seven members. Memorise the families first; the members hang
off them.

### 1. Isolated footings

- **Separated one from the other** to form a series of support points for a
  building
- Used under isolated brick piers and stumps, timber posts and poles, steel
  columns and reinforced concrete columns
- Forms: **pads, bored piers or driven piles**
- Footings **rely on the foundation material around them** to keep their
  position relative to one another
- **If that material is not stable, the footings may move around**, causing
  material failure that manifests as cracks in walls — or, at worst, collapse
- Suitable where loads reach the foundations **through separate columns**
- The **simplest and most cost-effective** footing for vertical support and
  transfer of building loads to the ground
- Most are reinforced concrete, but masonry units (brick, stone, concrete
  block) are sometimes used, **particularly in NSW**

**Tying piers or stumps to the footings** — three methods:

1. Steel brackets and bolts
2. Steel reinforcing starter bars cast into the concrete footings
3. Casting the pole, post or column directly into the concrete footing

### 2. Continuous footings

Generally used in conjunction with **masonry walls**.

**Strip footings**

- Continuous reinforced concrete footings that are generally **wide and deep**
- The reinforcement **counters any tendency for the footing to move due to
  local settlement**
- A **very basic form** of footing used in Australia
- Can also be built using masonry units on stable foundation areas
- A strip of concrete under **all of the load-bearing walls**
- Depth and width are determined by the **nature of the ground and the load of
  the building**

**Beam (or deep beam) footings**

- Designed to **resist bending forces**
- Designed to a **grid pattern**, capable of continuing to give stable support
  when the **foundation strata may be unstable**
- **Deeper than strip footings**
- The footing may vary in depth and width

### 3. Integrated footings

**Raft slab** — the most common in domestic construction:

- The **slab-on-ground system with an integral edge beam**, also known as the
  **raft slab**
- Footing beams are **poured integrally with the floor**
- Where column loads are considerable, or the bearing capacity of the soil is
  poor, independent column bases become so large as to nearly touch one
  another — it then becomes **economical to link the bases into one
  continuous slab or raft**
- More economical because: excavation work is simpler; the amount of **edge
  formwork is reduced**; fixing steel reinforcement is simplified

**Pier and beam system**

- Suitable where there is **no adequate foundation-bearing strata near the
  ground surface**
- Pier holes are **bored at 2,000–3,000 mm centres for residential**
- A reinforced concrete **beam is cast on top of the piers** to provide a
  system on which continuous masonry walls can be built

> **The raft becomes economical at the point where isolated pads stop being
> isolated.** The logic on the slide is worth reproducing in an answer: heavy
> column loads or poor bearing capacity → each pad has to get bigger to keep
> bearing pressure below bearing capacity → the pads grow until they nearly
> touch → at that point one continuous slab is cheaper, because you excavate
> once, form only the edge, and lay the reinforcement continuously.

## High-rise foundations

Not straightforward for "big buildings". **General factors that affect the
choice of foundation:**

- **Building load**
- **Soils** — ground and soil investigation, trial pits

### Piled foundations

Situations where a strip or trench-fill foundation would be **unsuitable**:

- Where existing or new **trees** are close to proposed houses, or where trees
  have been chopped to clear a site (tree roots present)
- Where the **uppermost sub-soil is too soft** to build on

**Two families of pile:**

| | **Replacement piles** | **Displacement piles** |
|---|---|---|
| Method | A **bored** pile that **extracts the soil** by boring or auguring and **replaces it with reinforced concrete** | **Driven** into the ground, **displacing** rather than removing soil |
| Ground | General | Used in **granular soils** such as **compact sands and gravels** |
| Arisings | Produces spoil to remove | **Little or no arisings** (excavated material) |
| Disadvantage | Spoil handling, slower | **Noise and vibration** as they are driven |

**Pile types listed for advantages and disadvantages:** bored piles · screw
piles · driven piles · Franki piles · micropiles.

> **Replacement = you take soil out. Displacement = you push soil aside.**
> The names describe what happens to the ground, not to the pile. Replacement
> (bored) is quiet but generates spoil; displacement (driven) generates no
> spoil but makes noise and vibration — which is why displacement piles are a
> problem on a restricted inner-city site next to occupied buildings.

## Retaining walls

**Three distinct types:**

```
1. Inclined or stabilised banking
2. Gravity retaining wall
3. Cantilever retaining wall
```

### How a retaining wall fails

**Two main causes** — and this is a named exam question in the revision deck:

1. The pressure acting on the wall is so great that **the wall is pushed over
   — it fails by overturning**.
2. The pressure acting on the wall is **not evenly distributed or restrained**,
   and one part of the wall **fails by shearing**. This is **common in
   unreinforced walls**.

**Controlling the overturning forces:**

- The **filling used to bridge between the natural bank and the retaining
  wall should be selected to minimise the pressure on the wall** — usually
  **crushed rock** gives the best results.
- **Water that is likely to flow from the natural bank into the filling must
  not be allowed to build up pressure on the wall, and therefore must be
  drained away from the area.**

> **Water is the cause of most retaining wall failures, and it is the one
> thing you control by drainage rather than by structure.** A wall designed
> for soil pressure alone will be overwhelmed if hydrostatic pressure builds
> behind it. That is why every retaining wall answer needs three things:
> **free-draining backfill, a drain at the base, and a way for water to get
> out** (weep holes or an agricultural drain).

### The three types

**Inclined or stabilised banking**

*Revetment*

- Forming an embankment at an angle at which the soil will **stand
  permanently by itself**
- Soil **battered** to the required safe slope
- Soil surface covered: grass, sprayed or cast in-situ concrete, bricks, rock
  rubble, boulders
- **Soil is not retained: only protected from surface erosion**

*Improved angle of slip-sloping type*

- Retain the natural bank by **augmenting its natural angle of slip**
- Systems include: pre-cast concrete planks; timber sleepers; interlocking
  timber and concrete patented systems; natural stones

**Gravity retaining wall**

- Common in **domestic construction**
- Depend on **their own mass** to resist the overturning forces exerted on
  them
- Can be constructed from any masonry unit or poured concrete
- Three profiles shown: **A** parallel and vertical faces; **B** one vertical
  face and one sloping face; **C** one vertical face and one stepped face

**Cantilever retaining wall**

- Makes use of the **extra strength obtainable by using reinforced concrete**
- Can be poured over pre-cast units
- **Requires a structural or civil engineer's design**

**Option: walls of the building.** Using the walls of the building as
basement retaining walls — usually constructed from masonry (concrete blocks)
or reinforced concrete.

> **A revetment does not retain anything.** It protects a slope from surface
> erosion at an angle the soil will already hold by itself. If a question
> asks for a system that *retains* a bank steeper than its natural angle, a
> revetment is the wrong answer — you need the improved-angle-of-slip type,
> a gravity wall or a cantilever wall.

### Retaining wall materials

| | Masonry | Cast in-situ concrete |
|---|---|---|
| Formwork | **Not required** | **Required** |
| Construction time | More | Less |
| Scaffolding | More | Less |
| Expansion joints | More | Less |
| Wall heights | **Limited** | **Greater height possible** |

**Reinforced concrete block retaining wall** — the hybrid:

- Special **large cavity block** specifically manufactured for this use
- Concrete is poured into the block cavities and **vibrated to remove air
  pockets** and ensure contact between steel reinforcing and concrete
- **Vertical bars are spliced to starter bars**
- **Horizontal bars are wired to vertical bars and laid in the groove**
- The **starter bar is cast into the footings**

> **Masonry wins on formwork and loses on everything else.** The table is
> five rows and every other row favours cast in-situ. So masonry is the
> choice for low domestic walls where avoiding formwork matters; in-situ
> concrete is the choice for height. The reinforced concrete block wall
> exists to get in-situ strength without conventional formwork — the block
> *is* the formwork.

### Water and backfilling

**Reducing water pressure**

- The easiest way is to **form holes in the wall near its base** and allow
  water to flow through an **open drain** (weep holes).
- If that is not possible — **in basements** — an **agricultural drain must be
  installed adjacent to the heel of the footing**. Water is collected and
  piped to a convenient disposal point, and **the wall also must be made
  waterproof**.

**Backfilling**

- **All retaining walls are required to be backfilled.**
- Backfill material should be **free-draining, well-graded gravel**:
  - **Free draining** to allow unimpeded water movement to drainage points
  - **Well graded** to limit the ingress of natural soil fines and prevent
    clogging
- An **agricultural drain or proprietary system** is placed behind the wall
  and at the base of the footing.
- The drainage system is usually **covered with a geofabric** to prevent
  clogging.
- Backfill should be placed **carefully to prevent damage to the drainage
  system**.

> **You cannot put weep holes in a basement wall.** A weep hole would let
> water into the habitable space. That is exactly why basements get an
> agricultural drain at the heel of the footing plus tanking/waterproofing —
> and why a basement wall is more expensive per square metre than a garden
> retaining wall doing the same structural job.

## Basements

```
A basement is a storey which is below ground and is therefore
constructed below ground.
```

**Deep basement excavation issues:**

- Excavations are going deeper
- Identify **old services**
- Look at **ground water and the water table**
- **Adjacent buildings and foundations**
- **Always be prepared for the unexpected**
- Give **flexible lead times**
- Understanding of the process

### Case study: Infinity Tower, Dubai Marina (2007)

- Nearly **100 workers escaped** as excavation on the 80-floor building was
  nearing completion.
- Workers were assisting with foundation works **20 m below ground level**.
- The supervision team **noticed a slight movement and cracks in the
  diaphragm wall** adjacent to the Dubai Marina and alerted management, who
  ordered an **immediate evacuation**.
- The diaphragm wall **collapsed and crumbled within 2 minutes** of being
  noticed.
- The whole site was **totally flooded within 4 minutes**.

> **The Infinity Tower is a case study in observation, not engineering.** The
> wall failed regardless; what saved 100 lives was that someone was watching
> the wall, recognised cracking as a warning, and evacuated. Two minutes from
> observation to collapse. Use it as the example when a question asks why
> monitoring and inspection matter on deep excavations.

## Other retaining systems for deep excavation

**For soil / sand:**

- Soldier pile and panel wall
- Contiguous wall
- Sheet piling
- Trench wall
- Shotcreting and ground anchors
- Steel soldiers with timber infills
- Secant piling
- Driven piles
- Bored piers

**For rock:** rock bolts

The revision deck narrows this to three you should know properly.

### Soldier pile and panel wall

- The piles are **fitted wider apart**, with the soil between piles
  **subsequently retained** using **timber lagging, precast concrete panels
  or shotcrete** applied to the exposed earth face during excavation
- **Generally used for temporary works**
- **Cost-effective and quick** to construct
- Can be designed as cantilever walls or with **additional lateral support
  from anchors or bracing**

**Major advantages:**

- Fast to construct
- Cheaper compared with other systems
- Versatile installation — adjustments can be made in the field easily to
  accommodate changes
- Lagging construction can be very quick
- Does **not require very advanced construction techniques**

**Major disadvantages:**

- Primarily **limited to temporary construction**
- **Cannot be used in high water table conditions** without extensive
  dewatering
- Poor backfilling and associated ground losses can result in **significant
  surface settlements**
- **Not as stiff** as other retaining systems
- Because **only the flange of a soldier pile is embedded beneath subgrade**,
  it is very difficult to control basal soil movements

### Contiguous pile wall

- A row of concrete soldier piles installed so that **each pile is in contact
  or near contact** with the piles on either side of it
- A **more permanent** retaining wall
- Suitable for a variety of soils **where groundwater lies below the maximum
  excavation depth**

### Sheet piling

- Constructed by **driving prefabricated sections into the ground**
- Sections can be **vibrated into ground or hammer-driven**
- The full wall is formed by **connecting the joints of adjacent sheet pile
  sections** in sequential installation
- Provide structural resistance by **utilising the full section**

**Generally used for:**

- Waterfront structures — wharfs, quays and piers
- Diversion dams, such as cofferdams
- River bank protection
- Retaining the sides of cuts made in earth

> **The three systems are separated by water and permanence.** Soldier pile
> and panel: **temporary**, cheap, fast, **fails in a high water table**.
> Contiguous pile: **permanent**, but still needs **groundwater below the
> excavation depth**. Sheet piling: **the water one** — wharfs, cofferdams,
> river banks — because the interlocking joints form a continuous barrier.
> If the question mentions water, the answer is sheet piling (or secant
> piling, which interlocks for the same reason).

<details>
<summary>4. A site has soft upper sub-soil and mature trees that were recently removed. Which footing system, and why?</summary>

**Piled** — specifically a **pier and beam** system if it is a house, or bored
replacement piles for something heavier. The deck names both triggers
explicitly: trees close to the building or recently removed (roots and clay
heave/shrinkage) and uppermost sub-soil too soft to build on. Pier holes at
**2,000–3,000 mm centres** for residential, with an RC beam cast on top to
carry the masonry walls.
</details>

<details>
<summary>5. What are the two ways a retaining wall fails, and how do you resist each?</summary>

**Overturning** — the pressure pushes the wall over. Resist with mass
(gravity wall), reinforced concrete geometry (cantilever wall), and by
reducing the pressure: crushed rock fill and drainage of water away from the
wall. **Shear** — pressure is not evenly distributed or restrained and part of
the wall shears off; this is common in **unreinforced** walls, so the answer
is reinforcement and even distribution of load.
</details>

<details>
<summary>6. Why can't a basement retaining wall use weep holes, and what replaces them?</summary>

Weep holes would discharge water into the occupied basement. Instead an
**agricultural drain** is installed adjacent to the **heel of the footing**,
collecting water and piping it to a disposal point, and **the wall must also
be waterproofed**. Backfill remains free-draining, well-graded gravel with
geofabric over the drainage system.
</details>

<details>
<summary>7. A deep excavation on a site with the water table above the base of the dig. Which retaining system, and which one would you rule out?</summary>

**Rule out soldier pile and panel wall** — it cannot be used in high water
table conditions without extensive dewatering, and it is a temporary system
anyway. **Contiguous piling** is also ruled out on its own terms (it is
suitable where groundwater lies *below* the maximum excavation depth). Use
**sheet piling** or **secant piling**, both of which interlock to form a
continuous water-resisting barrier — sheet piling is the system named for
cofferdams and waterfront work.
</details>

## Summary

- Foundations do two things: **transmit dead and imposed loads safely into the
  ground**, and **control settlement** so distortion stays acceptable.
- Definitions to hold: capacity (soil can carry) vs pressure (building
  applies); compaction (impact, immediate, material) vs consolidation
  (sustained load, over time, expels water and air); settlement (caused by
  the building's load) vs subsidence (caused by changes in the sub-soil);
  heave (expansion, frost or tree removal); soil creep (slow downslope
  movement).
- **Three footing families**: isolated (pads, piers, piles), continuous
  (strip, beam), integrated (raft slab, pier and beam). Raft slab is the most
  common domestic system and becomes economical when isolated pads grow until
  they nearly touch.
- **Replacement piles** are bored, remove soil and produce spoil;
  **displacement piles** are driven into granular soils, produce no arisings
  but cause noise and vibration.
- **Retaining walls fail by overturning or by shear**; crushed rock fill,
  free-draining well-graded backfill, an agricultural drain at the heel and
  geofabric are the standard controls. Masonry avoids formwork; in-situ
  concrete goes higher.
- **Deep excavation systems**: soldier pile and panel (temporary, cheap, fast,
  fails in high water table), contiguous pile (permanent, needs groundwater
  below dig level), sheet piling (interlocking, for water — wharfs,
  cofferdams, river banks), rock bolts for rock.
- Infinity Tower Dubai: cracks in the diaphragm wall were spotted, the site
  was evacuated, the wall collapsed in **two minutes** and the site flooded in
  **four**.
