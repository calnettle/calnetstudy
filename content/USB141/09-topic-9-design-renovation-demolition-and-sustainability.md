## Part A — Design, Renovation and Demolition

## Building design

```
Building design:
  - to create a graphic representation of a structure
  - the graphic architectural concept of a structure
  - to make a preliminary sketch, drawing, or outline
```

### The client's requirements

Also called the **design brief**, architectural brief or client's requirements
document.

- Any requirement a client may have that an architect designs to meet, usually
  by creating a building to accommodate the requirement(s)
- A brief is a **written document** that might be anything from a **single
  page to a multiple-volume set of documents**
- It is often **formulated by or with the architect**, and includes
  **opportunities assessed, constraints identified, and needs for further
  investigation clarified**

### Design process consultants

Ten to be able to name:

- Town planner
- Quantity surveyor
- Building certifier
- Architect, interior designer and/or landscape designer
- Civil, structural, traffic engineers
- Services engineers — **hydraulics, electrical, mechanical, fire, lifts,
  communications**
- Geotechnical engineer
- Land (cadastral) surveyor
- Façade engineer
- Facilities management consultant

**Responsibilities of the consultants:** attendance at meetings; liaison with
other consultants and the client; co-ordination of sub-consultants;
buildability and construction details; workshop drawing approvals;
responsibility and standard of reporting; meeting time, cost and quality
requirements; **analysis of alternatives and life cycle costings**;
responsibility for site tests and design approvals; supervision and quality
control; commissioning and acceptance procedures.

### The design process — five stages

| Stage | What happens |
|---|---|
| **Pre-design** | The architect talks to the client about **expectations, project requirements and budget**. This information forms the **design brief** |
| **Concept / schematic design** | The architect analyses the design brief and the **building site conditions**, determines the **best location and orientation**, then develops ideas through rough plans, sketches and models, brought together into concept / schematic design drawings |
| **Design development, documentation and building approvals** | The architect **compares the concept drawings with the design brief** and develops technical detail with the project team. **Detailed drawings and specifications** are prepared for the builder. Drawings are lodged to obtain **local authority building approval** |
| **Construction** | The architect works with the builder and project team to ensure the project is constructed **in accordance with the drawings and specification** |
| **After construction** | Projects have a warranty period called the **defects liability period**. It is the architect's responsibility to **follow up any relevant issues or outstanding work** with the client and the builder |

> **The design brief is created at pre-design and *checked against* at design
> development.** That loop is the examinable structure — the brief is not
> written once and forgotten; it is the benchmark the concept design is tested
> against before documentation. And the architect's involvement does not end
> at handover: the **defects liability period** is a named stage.

## Drawings

Recommended standards for working drawings are set out by **Standards
Australia**, covering **line work, dimensioning, sheet layout and size, scale,
symbols and lettering**.

**Drawings should be:**

- Technically correct and up to date, reflecting current good building
  practice and current statutory requirements
- **Well coordinated, with no conflicts or ambiguities** within themselves or
  with other drawings and measured information
- Developed throughout the project lifecycle to become an essential part of
  the **as-built information**
- **Specific to the project with no irrelevant material**
- **Comprehensive**, covering every significant aspect of quality to a degree
  of detail appropriate to the importance and nature of the work
- **Practicable** requirements, having regard to the nature of the project and
  available knowledge and resources
- **Constructive** — helpfully specific, so that all parties know what is
  expected

**Architectural drawing media covered:** hand sketches (Mario Botta's
Wellness Centre "Bergoase", Arosa, Switzerland 2003–2006; Alvar Aalto's
Viipuri Public Library, 1933–35; Jørn Utzon's Sydney Opera House), **CAD**,
and **BIM**.

### Drawing numbers and titles

The purpose of numbering is to give each drawing a **unique place in the set
to facilitate filing and retrieval**. Numbers usually fall into three parts:

1. A **project identification number** (master site number) — useful for
   office purposes but normally ignored for communications within the project,
   e.g. `38170`
2. A **sequential number** giving unique identification within the
   classification group number, e.g. `34598/…`
3. A **classification group identifier**, e.g. `A` for Architectural — `A01.2`

**Drawing classifications:**

```
A  Architect              C  Civil Engineer
S  Structural Engineer    H  Hydraulics Engineer
E  Electrical Engineer    L  Landscape Architect
M  Mechanical Engineer    F  Fire Services Engineer
```

### Final working drawings versus workshop drawings

| | Final working drawings | Workshop drawings |
|---|---|---|
| Prepared by | The **design consultants** — architect and engineers | The **construction contractor or subcontractor** (supplier, manufacturer) |
| Content | Architectural (floor plans, interior and exterior elevations and sections), structural, mechanical, electrical and HVAC details, site and landscape plans | **Particular parts of the building which they are to supply**, or products to be assembled in their shops — architectural embellishment or interior design, special cabinet work, elevator or heating systems |

> **Workshop drawings are made by the people who will build the thing.** That
> is the whole distinction. Working drawings say *what is required*; workshop
> drawings say *how this supplier will make it*, and they go back to the
> consultant for **workshop drawing approval** — which is on the consultants'
> responsibility list.

## Safety in design

```
Safe design = the integration of control measures EARLY in the design
process to eliminate or, if this is not reasonably practicable, minimise
risks to health and safety THROUGHOUT THE LIFE of the structure being
designed.
```

- The safe design of a structure will always be **part of a wider set of
  design objectives**, including **practicability, aesthetics, cost and
  functionality**
- These sometimes competing objectives need to be balanced **in a manner that
  does not compromise the health and safety** of those who work on or use the
  structure over its life

**Safe design begins at the concept development phase**, when making decisions
about:

- The design and its **intended purpose**
- **Materials** to be used
- Possible methods of **construction, maintenance, operation, demolition or
  dismantling and disposal**
- What **legislation, codes of practice and standards** need to be considered
  and complied with

**Benefits of safety in design:** prevention of injury and disease; improved
usability of structures; improved productivity; reduced costs; better
prediction and management of production and operational costs over the
lifecycle; compliance with legislation; **innovation (safe design demands new
thinking)**.

> **"Throughout the life of the structure" includes demolition.** Safe design
> is not only about the construction phase — the definition explicitly covers
> maintenance, operation, **demolition or dismantling and disposal**. A
> building designed so the façade can be cleaned safely and taken apart safely
> in fifty years is what safe design means.

## Value of good design

Three dimensions, then four benefit categories.

**The three dimensions:**

| Dimension | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Build quality** | The **engineering performance** of a building — structural stability, integration and robustness of systems, services, finishes and fittings |
| **Functionality** | The **arrangement, quality and interrelationship of space**, and the way the building is designed to be **useful** |
| **Impact** | The building's ability to **create a sense of place** and have a positive effect on the local community and environment; also the wider effect on the arts of building and architecture |

**Four benefit categories:**

- **Financial** — lower operation and maintenance costs, higher book value,
  higher market value, higher whole-life value
- **Internal business process** — flexibility/adaptability, functional
  efficiency, process innovation, organisational structure
- **Human resource** — personal well-being, higher quality of work life,
  improved productivity, cultural expression
- **Stakeholder** — public image, organisational expression, brand identity,
  recognition

## Design for Manufacture and Assembly (DfMA)

```
DfMA is a design approach that focuses on ease of manufacture and
efficiency of assembly.
```

- By **simplifying the design** of a product it is possible to manufacture and
  assemble it more efficiently, in the **minimum time and at a lower cost**
- Construction contractors have begun to adopt DfMA for the **off-site
  prefabrication** of construction components such as **concrete floor slabs,
  structural columns and beams**

### Advantages of DfMA — six

| Advantage | Why |
|---|---|
| **Speed** | Significantly **reduced programme on-site** through the use of prefabricated elements |
| **Lower assembly cost** | **Fewer parts**, decreased labour, **fewer unique parts** |
| **Higher quality and sustainability** | A highly automated approach enhances quality and efficiency at each stage; **less waste in the construction phase**, greater efficiency in site logistics, **fewer vehicle movements** transporting materials |
| **Shorter assembly time** | Uses **standard assembly practices** such as **vertical assembly and self-aligning parts**; ensures the transition from design to production is smooth and rapid |
| **Increased reliability** | **Lowering the number of parts decreases the chance of failure** |
| **Safety** | Removing construction activities from the site into a **controlled factory environment** can have a significant positive impact on safety |

> **DfMA's mechanism is always "fewer parts".** Fewer parts → lower assembly
> cost, higher reliability, shorter assembly time. If you can only remember
> one sentence, remember that simplification is the lever and everything else
> follows from it. Compare with Topic 7: DfMA is the *design philosophy* that
> makes prefabrication and modular construction work.

## Renovation

```
Building renovation is the process of fixing or replacing existing parts of
the building to improve its performance, either to its original state or
better.
                          — Jensen & Maslesa (2015), Building and Environment
```

At the same time, renovation provides the possibility to **change building
design/layout, functions and architectural expression** to match users'
current and/or future needs.

**Why renovate?**

- Building stock is **ageing** and becomes less attractive
- **Indoor climate** — we spend most of our time indoors
- **Higher energy prices** — older buildings are less energy efficient
- Renovations usually address **several challenges at the same time** and
  therefore contribute **multiple benefits**

**Barriers to renovation** — seven:

1. Too little consciousness about the **value creation** by renovation
2. **Weak economic incentive structures — including the landlord/tenant
   dilemma**
3. Lack of **life cycle cost perspective**
4. Lack of **standard solutions/concepts**
5. **Overview and common direction** is lacking among the stakeholders
6. **No overview of potential and priority**
7. Renovation has an **image problem** compared with new building activities

### Façade renovation and re-imaging

**Façade re-imaging (building wrap)** — a way to transform the entire exterior
appearance of a commercial facility **without performing any lengthy,
disruptive and expensive construction**. It usually involves:

- **Window film** to glass windows and doors
- **Resurfacing the spandrel** (the glass panels between floors)
- **Repainting mullions** (the vertical bars between glass panes)

> **Re-imaging is cosmetic and that is the point.** No structural work, no
> tenant displacement, a rapid change of the building's market perception at
> a fraction of the cost of re-cladding. In a Grade B building fighting new
> stock, it is a real strategy — Topic 10 shows why the cost of it must be
> weighed against the capital value it protects.

## Retrofitting

> Whether to demolish or retrofit is a question which cannot be answered
> simply and necessitates a multidimensional and holistic investigation.

**Definitions:**

- Oxford Dictionary (2014): **the adding of components and accessories to
  something that it did not have when manufactured**
- In construction (Wilkinson, 2011): **the induced modernisation and
  improvement of an existing structure/building due to its degradation and
  the need to improve energy efficiency, architectural appeal and indoor
  environment quality for the benefit of the community**

> **Renovation versus retrofit is a named exam question — "what is the
> purpose? what are the differences?"**
>
> | | Renovation | Retrofit |
> |---|---|---|
> | Core act | **Fixing or replacing existing parts** | **Adding components and accessories it did not have** |
> | Target state | Back to **original state or better** | **Modernisation and improvement** beyond original |
> | Driver | Ageing stock, indoor climate, energy prices | **Degradation plus the need for energy efficiency, architectural appeal and indoor environment quality** |
> | Beneficiary framing | The building and its users | Explicitly **"for the benefit of the community"** |
>
> Renovation restores; retrofit adds what was never there.

### Retrofitting strategy

- Buildings must **adapt to new standards** and retrofit is an option
- Considerations: **financing available, rental returns, value growth**
- Characteristics: energy type used, age, size, occupancy characteristics,
  heating and cooling systems, location, orientation, building envelope

> The effectiveness of retrofitting scenarios on the energy performance of an
> office building depends on specific characteristics related to its
> architectural structure, its operational features and relation to the
> surrounding environment.

**Two named strategies:**

| Strategy | Description |
|---|---|
| **The Stabilisation Strategy (STA)** | A set of **incremental interventions that do not fundamentally modify either the substance or the appearance** of the building. Alterations of minor elements such as **light fittings, mechanical ventilation systems** |
| **The Double-Skin Facade Strategy (DSF)** | Involves **"a complete metamorphosis of the building's appearance"** whilst **preserving "a large portion of the original substance"**. Major alterations including changes to major structural elements such as the **roof and facade** |

**Benefits of retrofitting**

- Reducing energy consumption
- Reducing greenhouse gas emissions
- Enhanced **corporate image, corporate social responsibility**
- Improved **employee productivity**

> **NABERS** is a significant factor promoting energy efficiency retrofits in
> Australia and has been proven to be an extremely effective **marketing
> tool** in the commercial office building sector.

**Barriers to retrofitting**

- **Risky**
- **Long payback period with interruptions to existing tenancies**
- **Less incentive to building owners or investors** — part of the return to
  energy efficiency improvements consists of relatively predictable energy
  savings, but **under standard lease contracts and in multi-tenant buildings
  these typically flow to the occupants**

By category:

- **Financial** — tenants' pre-commitment required
- **Technical** — **asbestos** (adds enormous costs, reputation); **heritage
  buildings**; quality of structures and materials
- **Market conditions** — companies moving around and looking to downsize;
  ideal location with good demand (generally CBD or some fringe location)

> **The split-incentive problem is the single best answer to "why don't
> owners retrofit?"** The owner pays the capital cost; under a standard lease
> the **energy savings flow to the tenant** who pays the outgoings. The owner
> gets no direct return, so nothing happens. This is the same "landlord/tenant
> dilemma" listed under renovation barriers, and it is why NABERS matters —
> a rating turns an invisible efficiency gain into a **marketable** attribute
> the owner can charge for.

### Retrofitting the building envelope — three strategies

| Strategy | When it is used |
|---|---|
| **Replacement** | Demolition and replacement with new façades. Used when the **deterioration of the façades is critical and repair requires too much work** |
| **Add-in** | An **insulation layer inside**. For façades that **do not require repairs or have architectural value** |
| **Wrap-it** | **New and more efficient layers overlap with the existing envelope**. Many technical solutions, from simple protected insulation to double screens with ventilation |

**Adding volume** — three approaches, and the first one pays for the rest:

- **Storey extension**, an additional storey above the existing roof — **offers
  the possibility to fund the renovation/retrofit** (often modular or timber
  construction)
- **Additional volume** to increase living spaces — for example, balconies
- **Boxes connected to the envelope** — buffer zones, greenhouses or common
  areas; same funding logic but **technically more challenging**

> **Add-in is the only one that preserves the façade's appearance.** If a
> heritage or architecturally valuable façade must stay, you insulate from
> the inside (add-in) — accepting the loss of internal floor area and the
> thermal bridging at the slab edges. Replacement and wrap-it both change the
> external appearance.

### Community retrofit

**Community** is defined as more than just a collection of households within a
geographical area, but as **people linked by shared resources and needs** —
whether residents, employees or business owners.

**Community retrofit** therefore means the retrofit of **all community
infrastructure**: housing, transport and social infrastructure, lighting,
heating, green space and others. It develops and delivers a vision addressing
the full needs of the community including transport, utilities provision,
building efficiency, public spaces and economic activity.

**Why** — the decline spiral the deck describes:

1. Quality of apartment buildings gets lower
2. The **wealthiest residents move into higher quality apartments**
3. The other owners/tenants **don't have sufficient funds for routine
   maintenance**
4. As a result the buildings often become **dilapidated housing for
   low-income families**

Necessary energy refurbishment required by current regulations provides an
**opportunity to modernise housing to meet new needs and regenerate suburban
areas** — new housing models, small temporary apartments associated with
multifunctional spaces, shared community areas.

**Outdoor spaces:** rethink collective spaces and green areas; redesign green
spaces and create equipped gardens; **active participation in the maintenance
of these spaces, strictly managed by residents, has helped to stop the
process of vandalism and degradation**. Quality of green space matters
alongside size, boundaries and functions.

Example: **Wyandanch Village, Long Island, New York**, where asphalt parking
was turned into a great public space.

### Retrofitting cities in Australia

> Established cities will achieve the largest GHG emissions savings by
> replacing, repurposing, or retrofitting the building stock, strategic
> infilling and densifying, as well as through modal shift and the
> electrification of the urban energy system.
>
> — *Climate Change 2022: Mitigation of Climate Change* (Technical Summary,
> IPCC AR6)

**Benefits of urban retrofit:** reduce greenhouse gases; adaptation and
improved resilience to floods, fires and extreme weather; improve energy,
waste and water management; health benefits; **improves rental yield**;
**preventing obsolescence**.

New construction can provide some of these benefits **but generally requires
more upfront (embodied) emissions**.

**Challenges preventing widespread retrofit:** new skills and education
required to work in existing buildings and brownfield sites; **economic
hurdles** — perceived as too challenging or too costly; **policy barriers** —
policy usually focuses on deep retrofits and on new construction; lack of
research and innovation.

## Demolition

**Three main ways to demolish:**

| Method | Used for | How it works | Considerations |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Implosion** | **Big / high buildings** | Explosives loaded on several different levels so the building structure **falls on itself at multiple points**. For a 20-storey building: blow the columns on the **first and second floors, and the 12th and 15th** — the building collapses | The main challenge is **controlling which way it falls**. Consider surroundings: **dust, noise, vibration** |
| **Giant wrecking balls** | **Large but lower** buildings | Swinging mass | **Not common nowadays**; still used when other methods are not practical; the **ball is difficult to control**; consider surroundings and safety |
| **Munching** | **Smaller or lower** buildings | Everything re-usable is removed first; **asbestos check and removal**; recyclable materials and parts removed (windows, doors); leftover structure **'munched'** (concrete beams, columns) | — |

**Demolition example — Queen's Wharf, Brisbane** (the buildings were
'munched'):

- **Asbestos check**
- **Internal demolition** — remove furniture, carpets and fittings
- **Building scaffold stays high** while internal elements are demolished
  downward
- **Debris tipped into lift cores**, running through a chute to waiting trucks
  on William Street
- Construction debris **crunched up and reused as fill** — one destination was
  Brisbane Airport

> **Munching is the only one of the three that recovers materials, and that
> is why it is the modern default.** Implosion and the wrecking ball destroy
> everything indiscriminately. Munching sequences the job: hazardous materials
> out first, re-usables out second, recyclables out third, structure last —
> which is the **circular economy** applied to demolition, and the link to
> Part B of this topic.

## Part B — Sustainability

## Sustainable buildings

The United Nations (1987) definition, quoted verbatim on the slide:

> Sustainable development is development that meets the needs of the present
> without compromising the ability of future generations to meet their own
> needs.

### Linear versus circular economy

```
LINEAR (the Industrial Revolution mindset — our dominant mindset)

    TAKE  →  MAKE  →  USE  →  DISPOSE

CIRCULAR (the next revolution — what must become our dominant mindset)

    MAKE  →  USE  →  REUSE / RECYCLE  →  (back to MAKE)
```

Waste is "disposed of" **back to the source**: **air** (gases and particles),
**water** (liquids, solids and particles), **soil** (liquids, gases, solids
and particles).

**What is a circular economy?**

- An **industrial system that is restorative or regenerative by intention and
  design**
- It **replaces the end-of-life concept with restoration**, shifts towards
  the use of **renewable energy**, **eliminates the use of toxic chemicals**,
  and aims for the **elimination of waste through the superior design of
  materials, products, systems and business models**
- **Nothing that is made in a circular economy becomes waste**, moving away
  from the current linear 'take-make-dispose' economy
- The potential for innovation, job creation and economic development is
  huge: estimates indicate a **trillion-dollar opportunity**

> **"Restorative or regenerative BY INTENTION AND DESIGN" is the phrase to
> quote.** Circularity is not recycling bolted on at the end — it is designed
> in from the start, which is why it belongs in the same topic as DfMA and
> safe design. All three are "decide it early or you cannot have it".

## Why build sustainably

**Commercial reasons:**

- **Reduce your running costs**
- **Rising power and water costs — future waste costs**
- **New legislation**
- **Positive impact on the environment**
- **A-grade development with a competitive edge**

**Health and wellbeing of people:**

- Improved physical and emotional wellbeing
- Increased connection with their surroundings
- Occupants are keen to help the environment and see the benefits of working
  in a facility that encourages them to 'live green'
- Happier outlook
- **Improved productivity**
- **Reduction in sick leave**

**The investment benefits** — ten, and this is the list that connects
sustainability to Topic 10's capital value formula:

- Potential **higher occupancy rates**
- **Higher future capital value**
- **Reduced risk of obsolescence**
- Less need for refurbishment in the future
- **Ability to command higher lease rates**
- **Higher demand from institutional investors**
- **Lower operating costs**
- **Mandatory for government tenants**
- **Lower tenant turnover**
- Costs less to maintain and operate

> **Every item on that list moves a term in `CV = NI / CR`.** Higher lease
> rates and occupancy → higher gross income. Lower operating costs → lower
> outgoings. Both raise net income. Lower tenant turnover, reduced
> obsolescence risk and institutional demand → a **lower cap rate**. Numerator
> up and denominator down, so capital value rises twice over. That is the
> single strongest argument you can make in an Assessment 2 recommendation.

## Sustainable design

- **Sustainable Design = Good Design**
- Going back to the roots of architecture and construction, **using the
  techniques that were common before the industrial age**
- Using one's knowledge of **climate and materials** to design a building able
  to create a **constant comfortable internal environment without mechanical
  aid**

**Sustainable development — how?** Ten measures:

- Siting and orientation of new buildings
- Site ecology (flora, fauna, erosion)
- **Shading of openings to reduce solar radiation**
- **Human comfort through daylighting and natural ventilation**
- Waste management
- Materials — both construction and interior
- **Drought tolerant and low maintenance landscaping**
- Building management to reduce wasted energy
- **Energy and water efficient fixtures and equipment**
- **Rainwater collection and re-use**

### Overcooling — the worked example of wasted energy

- Why are many public spaces set to **18 °C**?
- If you must use air conditioning, **set the thermostat between 24 °C and
  27 °C** (or higher if comfortable)
- **Increasing your thermostat by just 1 °C in warm weather can reduce the
  running cost of your appliance by about 10%**

> **Worked example — the 1 °C rule.** A building spends $180,000 a year on
> cooling and currently runs at 21 °C. Lift the setpoint to 24 °C:
>
> ```
> Saving per degree ≈ 10% of running cost
> 3 degrees, applied successively:  180,000 x 0.9^3 = $131,220
> Annual saving                                     = $48,780
> ```
>
> Now feed that through the cap rate. If the outgoing is **non-recoverable**
> and the cap rate is 6%:
>
> ```
> Capital value created = 48,780 / 0.06 = $813,000
> ```
>
> A thermostat setting. That is why "building management to reduce wasted
> energy" is on the sustainable development list — and note the qualifier
> *non-recoverable*: if the tenant pays the outgoing under the lease, the
> saving flows to the tenant, not the owner. Straight back to the
> split-incentive problem.

Overcooling also has an equity dimension: research (Parkinson, Schiavon, de
Dear et al., *Scientific Reports* 11, 2021) shows **overcooling of offices
reveals gender inequity in thermal comfort**. Overcooling and overheating
buildings emits as much carbon as **four million cars**.

## Building ratings

**Why rate buildings at all?**

- Provides **market recognition and competitive advantage**
- Provides a **credible, independently verified means for benchmarking**
- **Encourages best practice and innovation in design**

And the reason it matters:

- The built environment has a significant global environmental impact and
  contributes to climate change
- Buildings are responsible for **40% of the world's carbon emissions** and
  use approximately **40% of the world's energy** and **30% of the world's
  available drinking water**

### NABERS

**National Australian Built Environment Rating System.**

- Provides **simple, reliable and comparable sustainability measurement**
  across building sectors including **hotels, shopping centres, apartments,
  offices and data centres**
- **NABERS ratings are valid for twelve months.** This **annual model** helps
  ensure that a rating represents a building's or workplace's **current
  operational performance**
- *"What gets measured gets managed."*

### Green Star

- An **internationally-recognised Australian** sustainability rating and
  certification system
- There are **four Green Star rating tools**

**Green Star Buildings — key features:**

- **Help future proof your asset.** A holistic tool that extends beyond the
  environment to address the issues that will define the next decade of the
  built environment. The tool's **8 categories** enable owners and developers
  to act on the areas of sustainability that matter most
- **A clear path to decarbonisation.** Includes the **Climate Positive
  Pathway** — targets aligned with IPCC recommendations to deliver a Climate
  Positive building that is **fossil fuel free, powered by renewables, highly
  efficient, built with lower carbon materials and offset with nature**
- **Access sustainable finance with ease.** Any building meeting the Climate
  Positive Pathway **automatically complies with the Climate Bonds
  Initiative**
- **Fast, simple, streamlined reporting.** Designed to align with the **UN
  Sustainable Development Goals, GRESB, IPCC recommendations and the Task
  Force for Climate Related Financial Disclosure**
- **Meet tenant demands for better, healthier buildings.** Sets minimum
  standards for **fresh air, adequate light, acoustic comfort and exposure to
  toxins**
- **Be recognised for your leadership**

**Build for better returns** — the certified-asset performance figures:

| Metric | Green Star certified assets |
|---|---|
| Capital value per m² | **16.4% higher** |
| Annual return | **13.5% higher** |
| WALE (weighted average lease expiry) | **23% longer** |
| Electricity | **66% less** |
| Water | **51% less** |

> **NABERS versus Green Star is the comparison question.**
>
> | | NABERS | Green Star |
> |---|---|---|
> | Measures | **Operational performance** — energy, water, waste, indoor environment, as actually metered | **Design and construction** (and communities, performance, interiors) |
> | Validity | **12 months — must be renewed annually** | Certification, not annually renewed |
> | Reach | **Australian**, sector-benchmarked | **Internationally recognised** |
> | Scale | Star rating | Star rating, **four tools**, 8 categories in Green Star Buildings |
>
> The trap: both use stars, so a "6 Star" claim is meaningless until you say
> **6 Star what**. 25 King Street holds **6 Star Green Star Design & As Built
> v1.1** *and* **6 Star NABERS Energy** — two different achievements.

> **NABERS expiring after twelve months is a deliberate design choice.** A
> design rating can be earned once and coasted on; an operational rating has
> to be re-earned every year against actual metered consumption. That is why
> the deck pairs it with "what gets measured gets managed", and why NABERS —
> not Green Star — is described as the effective **marketing tool** driving
> energy efficiency retrofits.

## The cost of being green

In Australia:

- Construction costs of green buildings are about **5% higher** than
  conventional homes
- The difference in costs **gets covered within the first 3–4 years**, along
  with a great reduction in operational costs

> **Worked example — the 5% green premium.** A $12 million conventional
> building costs $12.6 million if built green (+5% = $600,000). If the payback
> is 3–4 years:
>
> ```
> Implied annual operational saving = 600,000 / 3.5 ≈ $171,400 per year
> ```
>
> Capitalised at a 6% cap rate, that recurring saving is worth:
>
> ```
> 171,400 / 0.06 = $2.86 million of capital value
> ```
>
> for a $600,000 spend. Note this is an *implied* figure derived from the
> deck's own two claims — the deck does not state an annual saving. But it
> shows why the Green Star performance table (16.4% higher capital value per
> m², 13.5% higher annual return) is plausible rather than marketing.

- A type of concrete made with **sustainable materials**, designed to reduce
  the environmental impact of construction projects
- Made with **recycled glass, fly ash, and blast furnace slag**
- Can also be made with **alternative cements**, such as those made from

**Recycled concrete aggregate**

- Concrete recycling **reclaims waste concrete from demolition sites and
  other sources** to turn it into new construction materials
- Recycling of concrete aggregate is a **well-established practice** in the
  building industry, but **has not been widely used to reduce carbon
  emissions from cement production**
- With increasing demand for sustainable materials there is now more incentive

## The Tegel project, Berlin

The circular-economy case study at city scale.

- The former **Berlin-Tegel Airport** is making room for Berlin
- Tegel Airport has been **closed since 8 November 2020**; all flight
  operations moved to Berlin Brandenburg Airport
- New apartments and a future-oriented technology park will be built on the
  site:
  - **Berlin TXL – the Urban Tech Republic**, a research and industrial park
    for urban technologies — **202 ha** for research, innovation and
    technology
  - **Schumacher Quartier**, a new residential district
  - **200 hectare landscape zone**

**Schumacher Quarter — sustainability for everyone:**

- A **model project for climate-adapted district planning**, applying the
  **"sponge city" principle** so it can be better protected against excessive
  rain and heat
- Minimises climate impact as much as possible — **CO₂ reduction, energy
  efficiency, and the use of renewable energy sources** are specific building
  blocks on the path towards **climate neutrality**
- The interaction of **climate protection and climate adaptation** becomes
  visible in **water-retaining open spaces, photovoltaic systems, timber
  construction, and shaded roofs**
- All partners have agreed to **seven guiding principles** in a charter of
  rights

> **The deck states the Tegel site area two ways.** The project overview slide
> says the Urban Tech Republic sits alongside a **200 hectare** landscape
> zone; the following slide says the Urban Tech Republic itself is **202 ha**.
> Those are two different things (the tech park and the landscape zone), not
> a contradiction — but they are one slide apart and easy to conflate. Quote
> them separately.

> **Climate protection and climate adaptation are not the same thing, and
> Schumacher Quarter is the example that separates them.** *Protection* =
> reducing your contribution to climate change (CO₂ reduction, renewables,
> timber construction). *Adaptation* = coping with the change that is already
> coming (sponge city water retention, shaded roofs against heat). A good
> sustainability answer names both.

## Checkpoint

<details>
<summary>1. Name the five stages of the design process.</summary>

**Pre-design** (client discussion → design brief) → **concept/schematic
design** (site analysis, orientation, sketches and models) → **design
development, documentation and building approvals** (concept checked against
the brief, detailed drawings and specifications, lodged for approval) →
**construction** (architect ensures compliance with drawings and
specification) → **after construction** (**defects liability period**).
</details>

<details>
<summary>2. What is the difference between working drawings and workshop drawings?</summary>

**Working drawings** are prepared by the design consultants (architect,
structural, mechanical, electrical, hydraulic, landscape) and cover the whole
building. **Workshop drawings** are prepared by the **construction contractor
or subcontractor, supplier or manufacturer** for the particular parts they
will supply or assemble — special cabinet work, lifts, heating systems — and
go back to the consultant for **workshop drawing approval**.
</details>

<details>
<summary>3. Give four advantages of DfMA.</summary>

**Speed** (reduced on-site programme); **lower assembly cost** (fewer parts,
less labour, fewer unique parts); **higher quality and sustainability** (less
waste, better site logistics, fewer vehicle movements); **shorter assembly
time** (standard practices, vertical assembly, self-aligning parts);
**increased reliability** (fewer parts, less chance of failure); **safety**
(work moved to a controlled factory environment).
</details>

<details>
<summary>4. Distinguish renovation from retrofit.</summary>

**Renovation** = fixing or replacing existing parts of a building to improve
performance, **either to its original state or better**. **Retrofit** =
**adding components and accessories that the building did not have when it
was made** — induced modernisation and improvement driven by degradation and
the need to improve energy efficiency, architectural appeal and indoor
environment quality, for the benefit of the community. Renovation restores;
retrofit adds.
</details>

<details>
<summary>5. An owner refuses to fund an energy retrofit of a fully leased multi-tenant office. Why, and what changes the calculation?</summary>

The **split-incentive / landlord-tenant dilemma**: under standard leases in
multi-tenant buildings the predictable energy savings flow to the **occupants
who pay the outgoings**, not to the owner who pays the capital cost. The
payback is long and disruptive to existing tenancies. What changes it: a
**NABERS rating**, which converts an invisible efficiency gain into a
marketable attribute the owner can capitalise through higher rents, higher
occupancy, lower tenant turnover and a lower cap rate — plus government
tenancy requirements that make ratings mandatory.
</details>

<details>
<summary>6. Name the three ways to demolish and say which recovers materials.</summary>

**Implosion** (big/high buildings; explosives on several levels so the
structure falls on itself; the challenge is controlling which way it falls),
**giant wrecking ball** (large but lower buildings; not common now; hard to
control), and **munching** (smaller/lower buildings). **Munching** is the one
that recovers materials — re-usables removed first, asbestos checked and
removed, recyclables (windows, doors) taken out, then the structure is
munched and the debris crushed for reuse as fill.
</details>

<details>
<summary>7. Give three differences between NABERS and Green Star.</summary>

| | NABERS | Green Star |
|---|---|---|
| What it measures | **Operational performance** as metered | **Design and construction** (plus communities, performance, interiors) |
| Validity | **12 months**, renewed annually | Certification |
| Reach | Australian, sector-benchmarked | **Internationally recognised**, four rating tools |
</details>

<details>
<summary>8. A building spends $260,000 a year on cooling at a 20 °C setpoint. What is the approximate saving from moving to 24 °C, and what capital value does it create at a 6.5% cap rate if the outgoing is non-recoverable?</summary>

```
4 degrees at ~10% each:  260,000 x 0.9^4 = $170,586
Annual saving                            = $89,414
Capital value = 89,414 / 0.065           = $1,375,600
```

State the assumption that the outgoing is **non-recoverable** — if the tenant
pays it, the owner captures none of the value.
</details>

## Summary

- **Design process**: pre-design → concept/schematic → design development,
  documentation and approvals → construction → defects liability period. The
  brief is written at the start and **checked against** at design development.
- **Safe design** integrates control measures **early**, to eliminate or
  minimise risk **throughout the life of the structure**, including
  demolition.
- **Value of good design** = build quality + functionality + impact, producing
  financial, business process, human resource and stakeholder benefits.
- **DfMA** simplifies design for ease of manufacture and efficiency of
  assembly. Six advantages, all flowing from **fewer parts**.
- **Renovation** fixes or replaces what is there; **retrofit** adds what never
  was. Retrofit's biggest barrier is the **split incentive** — savings flow to
  tenants, cost falls on owners.
- **Envelope retrofit**: replacement (critical deterioration), add-in
  (preserve appearance / architectural value), wrap-it (new layers over the
  old). A **storey extension can fund the retrofit**.
- **Three demolition methods**: implosion, wrecking ball, munching — only
  munching recovers materials.
- **Circular economy** replaces take-make-dispose; it is restorative and
  regenerative **by intention and design**.
- **NABERS** = operational, **12-month** validity, Australian.
  **Green Star** = design and construction, internationally recognised, four
  tools. Green Star certified assets: 16.4% higher capital value/m², 13.5%
  higher annual return, 23% longer WALE, 66% less electricity, 51% less water.
- Green construction costs about **5% more** and pays back in **3–4 years**;
  every sustainability benefit moves a term in `CV = NI / CR`.
