## The Australian residential market

Source: KPMG, January 2026, as cited in the Week 4 deck.

**Prices**

| | 2025 actual | 2026 forecast |
|---|---|---|
| National house prices | **+8.6%** | **+7.7%** |
| National unit prices | **+7.3%** | **+7.1%** |

Momentum is expected to remain solid through 2026, supported by stabilising
financial conditions and tightness in supply, with **more balanced house
price growth in 2027**.

**House price growth by city, 2025:** Perth **+16.4%** strongest, then Darwin
**+14.8%**, then Brisbane **+13.4%**.

**Unit price growth by city, 2025:** **Brisbane led the nation at +18.3%**,
then Perth **+16.0%**, then Darwin **+14.8%**.

**Brisbane specifically** (NAB, January 2026):

| | Month | Quarter | Year | Median |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Houses | +1.5% | +4.9% | **+15.1%** | **$1,149,589** |
| Units | +2.0% | +6.1% | **+18.3%** | **$824,764** |

> **Brisbane units outperformed Brisbane houses in 2025 on every horizon.**
> +2.0% vs +1.5% monthly, +6.1% vs +4.9% quarterly, +18.3% vs +15.1%
> annually. This is the opposite of the long-run pattern and it is the single
> most quotable fact in the topic — units are cheaper, use land more

> **The lecture's national and Brisbane house numbers differ, and both are
> right.** KPMG says national house prices rose **8.6%** in 2025; NAB says
> Brisbane house values rose **15.1%**. National versus city — not a
> contradiction. But note the unit figure **18.3%** appears in both the KPMG
> slide (Brisbane leading the nation on units) and the NAB slide (Brisbane
> annual unit growth). Same number, two sources, so it is safe to quote.

> **One slide in the deck is mis-dated.** The housing-affordability slide is
> sourced "KPMG, **Jan 2025**" while every other slide in the same run says

> January 2026.

**Trends affecting the property process** — six of them, each with a number:

1. **Housing approvals have gained momentum, but the supply response remains
   patchy.** Improvement in approvals has not translated into a consistent
   lift in completions; growth remains volatile. The disconnect reflects
   labour shortages, elevated construction costs and project delays.
2. **Construction costs remain high but growth slowed in 2025.** Building
   material prices rose **2.1%** over the year to September 2025. Timber rose
   as suppliers passed on costs; other metal products rose on higher raw
   material costs, particularly aluminium and copper; plaster, flooring,
   paint and insulation rose on supplier adjustments. Partly offset by lower
   concrete, cement and sand prices on stronger competition — although those
   materials still recorded the largest annual increase.
3. **Housing affordability hit a new low.** The Housing Affordability Index
   fell to **59.3** in the September quarter 2025. The repayment-to-income
   ratio sits at **50.6%**, slightly lower than 54.2% a year earlier.
4. **Rate cuts have eased mortgage stress** — financial stress among mortgage
   holders eased modestly, supported by **three interest rate cuts in 2025**
   and a resilient labour market.
5. **Lending activity surged.** Investment lending reached its highest level
   since March 2022 on falling borrowing costs and tight rental markets,
   pushing total new dwelling loan values to a record high.
6. **Migration slowed to a three-year low**, and **foreign investment remains
   below pre-pandemic levels**. From **1 April 2025**, foreign persons —
   including temporary residents and foreign-owned companies — are
   **temporarily banned from purchasing established dwellings** in Australia
   **until 31 March 2027**. Exceptions apply for large-scale developments of
   **20 or more properties**.

**Rental market.** Record highs, but growth is moderating. Tenants now
allocate a record **33.4%** of income to rent, well above the 20-year average
of **29.2%** (Cotality). The core driver is the structural imbalance between
strong recent population growth and limited housing supply — so conditions
stay tight even as population growth eases.

> **Point 3 contains a genuine oddity in the source.** Affordability is
> described as hitting "a new low" (index 59.3), yet the repayment-to-income
> ratio **improved** from 54.2% to 50.6%. Those move in opposite directions.
> The most likely reading is that the index is a broader measure (including
> deposit and price-to-income effects) while the repayment ratio only
> captures servicing cost, which fell with the three 2025 rate cuts. If you
> use this in a report, present both and say what each measures — do not
> claim affordability worsened on every measure, because it did not.

## Apartments

- The composition of homes built in Australia has changed substantially over
  the past decade. Detached house volumes last year were roughly in line with
  the 30-year average, so the shift is in what has been *added* on top.
- The shift to higher density brings Australia more into line with
  international norms — existing stock is heavily concentrated in detached
  houses and cities have relatively low density.
- **Apartments are typically much more affordable than houses.** The median
  apartment price is approximately **30% lower** than for houses, mainly
  because apartments are smaller, but also because they **use land more
  intensively**.
- **Rental yields for apartments are higher than for houses.**
- Three types of buyer: **owner-occupiers, domestic investors, foreign buyers
  (non-residents)**.
- Location and lifestyle: new apartments are generally in **established
  suburbs close to employment and services**; new houses are often in
  **greenfield areas awaiting infrastructure and amenity**.
- Demographics: **larger households** want larger dwellings (houses);
  **smaller households** — single adults, older downsizers — may prefer
  apartments.

**Apartment prices in high-rise buildings**

- Several studies find a **positive relationship between floor level and
  price**: positive with the highest floor, negative with the first floor.
- **Discount for ground floor units.**
- The unit price per m² of **super high-rise** residential buildings is about
  **40% higher** than other buildings.

> **"30% lower" and "40% higher" describe different things — do not merge
> them.** Apartments overall are about **30% cheaper than houses** (total
> price, driven by size). Super high-rise apartments carry about a **40%
> premium per square metre** over other buildings (rate, driven by
> construction complexity and view). Both can be true at once: a smaller
> dwelling at a higher rate per square metre.
>
> ```
> If a house median is $1,000,000, an apartment median is ~$700,000.
> If a standard apartment is $10,000/m2, super high-rise is ~$14,000/m2.
> ```
>
> Note also that the "30% lower" claim is a *median* comparison, so it is
> partly a mix effect, not a like-for-like discount.

## Building types and features

- **Building types:** residential or mixed use
- **Unit types:** 1, 2, 3, 4 bed; 1 bed plus study; penthouse
- **Three levels** — no lifts, usually natural ventilation, parking often
  underneath with two living levels
- **Over three levels** — lifts, natural or mechanical ventilation
- High population density brings **more stringent fire, noise and parking
  requirements**
- Contracts often involve extensive external works: landscaping,
  entertainment areas, footpath improvements, surrounding public areas and
  road networks

families**, often featuring recreational areas such as parks and reserves,

## Build-to-rent (BTR)

Sometimes called **multi-family housing**. BTR apartment complexes are
designed and constructed by a developer who **retains ownership** when
complete. The apartments are then rented to tenants by the developer, which
also **manages and maintains** the complex.

The AHURI definition (Australian Housing and Urban Research Institute),
quoted in the deck:

> 'Build-to-rent' (BTR) is the process whereby developers and their financiers
> build multi-unit buildings and, instead of selling the units, retain them to
> rent to tenant households. Rents may be set at market rent or, for
> affordable and social housing, at an appropriate discount to market rents.

**What institutional ownership of long-term rental delivers:**

- Giving long-term renters **security of tenure** and avoiding 'no-cause'
  evictions
- Improving **responsiveness to maintenance requests** and ensuring long-term
  capital expenditure is kept up
- Helping lift the level of **amenities and services** offered to renters,
  creating a more customer-oriented rental product

**Why the Australian BTR sector is small** compared with the United States,
Europe and Japan — three reasons:

1. **Relatively low yield**, making residential unfavourable to institutional
   investors relative to other commercial real estate segments
2. A **taxation structure that favours capital gains**
3. **Lengthy planning approval processes**

However, this is beginning to change: in recent years commercial investment
yields have declined, **making BTR yields relatively more attractive**.

> **The BTR argument is a relative-yield argument, and the direction matters.**
> BTR was unattractive because residential yields were low *compared with*
> commercial. Commercial yields then fell (compressed). The gap narrowed, so
> BTR became competitive — nothing had to change about residential yields at
> all. A question asking "why is BTR growing in Australia?" wants that
> relative movement, plus the tax and planning constraints as the remaining
> brakes.

> **Do not define BTR as "apartments built to be rented".** The definitional
> core is that the **developer retains ownership** and becomes the long-term
> landlord and manager. No strata sell-down, no individual investor owners,
> one institutional owner across the whole building.

### Example: Brisbane Skytower

- Brisbane's tallest residential tower, completed **2019**
- **270 m** high, **90 storeys**
- **1,141** one, two and three-bedroom apartments plus sub-penthouse and
  penthouse apartments
- Recreation deck on the **89th floor** with **Australia's highest
  infinity-edge swimming pool**

## High-rise residential methods and materials

**Structure**

- Reinforced concrete
- Steel framed (**rarely**)

**External envelope**

- Precast, curtain wall, lightweight composite cladding, glazing, balconies
- Sunscreening

**Partition walls**

- Blockwork
- Lightweight framing with insulation

**Roof**

- Reinforced concrete slab
- Steel roof cladding
- A combination of both

**Fit-out**

- Tailored to suit market and price point (high quality vs affordable
  housing)
- Fit-out generally **by the building developer**
- In some countries, units are sold as **'cold wet shells'** for fit-out by
  buyers

> **Residential high-rise is reinforced concrete; steel framing is rare.**
> This is the reverse of industrial (steel portal frame) and different again
> from office (RC post-tensioned, but 111 Eagle Street shows a steel frame is
> possible). Concrete suits residential because of acoustic separation
> between dwellings, fire separation, and the repetitive floor plate.

## Office versus residential high-rise — the comparison table

This is the one the unit asks for by name ("Differences — Office vs.
Residential"). Six differences, straight from the slide, and worth writing as
a table in the exam:

| Feature | Office / commercial | Residential |
|---|---|---|
| Façade | **Curtain walling** | **Balconies** |
| Floor to floor | **3,600 mm** (more in-ceiling services) | **3,000 mm** |
| Fit-out | Generally by the **tenant** | Generally by the **builder** |
| Location driver | Needs to be **close to the CBD** | Needs an **aspect and a view** |
| Public space finishes | Level and type of finish differs | Level and type of finish differs |
| Locations | Vary between the two | Vary between the two |

> **The floor-to-floor difference has a reason, and the reason is the mark.**
> Commercial is 3,600 mm because of **more in-ceiling services** — ducted
> HVAC, sprinklers, cable trays, lighting grid. Residential gets away with
> 3,000 mm because each dwelling has its own small split system and far less
> in the ceiling void. Quoting the two numbers is one mark; explaining
> in-ceiling services is the second.

> **The Week 3 office deck says 3,500 mm floor to floor; the Week 4 deck says
> 3,600 mm.** Both appear in this unit's own slides. The Week 3 figure sits
> next to the ceiling heights (2,700 mm and 3,000 mm) as a construction
> detail; the Week 4 figure sits in the comparison table against residential's
> 3,000 mm. In an exam answer, use **3,600 vs 3,000** for the comparison
> question because that is the slide the question is drawn from, and note the
> range 3,500–3,600 mm if you are writing a report. Worth confirming with
> Dr Chia which figure he wants.

<details>
<summary>2. Give five differences between an office high-rise and a residential high-rise.</summary>

Curtain walling vs **balconies**; **3,600 mm** vs **3,000 mm** floor to floor

**builder/developer**; location driven by **CBD proximity** vs by **aspect and

structure is commonly post-tensioned RC (or occasionally steel), residential
is almost always RC with steel framing rare.
</details>

<summary>3. Define build-to-rent, and give two reasons the Australian sector is small.</summary>

BTR is where developers and their financiers build multi-unit buildings and,
**instead of selling the units, retain them to rent** to tenant households —
managing and maintaining the complex themselves. Small in Australia because
of **relatively low residential yields** compared with other commercial

<details>
<summary>4. Brisbane median house $1,149,589 and median unit $824,764. Is the unit discount consistent with the national "approximately 30% lower" claim?</summary>

```
Discount = 1 - 824,764 / 1,149,589 = 1 - 0.7175 = 28.25%
```

**Yes — 28.3%, close to the stated ~30%.** Note the base: the discount is
expressed against the *house* median. Turned the other way round, houses are
1,149,589 / 824,764 = **39.4% more expensive** than units, not 30%. State
which base you are using.
</details>

<details>
<summary>5. Tenants allocate 33.4% of income to rent against a 20-year average of 29.2%. By how much has the burden risen, relative to average?</summary>

```
Absolute gap   = 33.4 - 29.2 = 4.2 percentage points
Relative       = 4.2 / 29.2  = 14.4% higher than the long-run average
```

Say "4.2 **percentage points** above the 20-year average, or 14.4% higher in
relative terms". Writing "4.2% higher" is wrong and it is a marked error.
</details>

<details>
<summary>6. A super high-rise apartment sells at $13,000/m². What would you expect a comparable non-super-high-rise apartment to fetch per m²?</summary>

The lecture says super high-rise is about **40% higher** per m² than other
buildings, so the base is the *other* buildings:

```
Base = 13,000 / 1.40 = $9,286 /m2
```

Not 13,000 × 0.6 = $7,800. Dividing by 1.40 and multiplying by 0.60 give
different answers — the premium is stated on the lower base, so you divide.
</details>

## Summary

- **High-rise = four storeys or greater** in Australia.
- Census 2021: 10,852,208 private dwellings, 70% separate houses, 13%
  townhouses, 16% apartments, 1,043,776 unoccupied.
- 2025: national house prices +8.6%, units +7.3%; Brisbane houses +15.1% to a
  $1,149,589 median and **Brisbane units +18.3% to $824,764 — the strongest
  unit growth in the nation.** KPMG forecasts +7.7% and +7.1% nationally for
  2026.
- Constraints: patchy completions despite better approvals, material costs
  +2.1% y/y, affordability index at 59.3, migration at a three-year low, and
  a **foreign purchase ban on established dwellings from 1 April 2025 to
  31 March 2027** (exceptions for developments of 20+ properties).
- Apartments are ~30% cheaper than houses in total price but super high-rise
  carries a ~40% premium per m²; ground floor units trade at a discount and
  price rises with floor level.
- **Build-to-rent** = the developer retains ownership and rents, manages and
  maintains. Small in Australia because of low relative yields, capital-gains-
  favouring tax and slow planning — now improving as commercial yields
  compress.
- Residential high-rise is **reinforced concrete**, precast/curtain wall/
  balcony envelope, blockwork or lightweight partitions, developer fit-out.
- The exam comparison: **curtain wall vs balcony, 3,600 vs 3,000 floor to
  floor, tenant vs builder fit-out, CBD vs aspect, different public finishes.**
