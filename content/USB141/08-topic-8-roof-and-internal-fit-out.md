# Topic 8 — Roof and Internal Fit-Out

Week 8. Roof covers, roof elements, the roof-type catalogue, interior linings,
windows, doors and stairs. This is the topic most likely to produce a
**label-the-diagram** question.

## Roof covers — the five

```
1. Tile roofs
2. Sheeted roofs
3. Membrane roofs (build-up roofs)
4. Concrete roofs
5. Green roofs
```

### 1. Tile roofs

| | Terracotta tiles | Concrete tiles |
|---|---|---|
| Finish | **Has a glaze that cannot be painted over** | Available in all colours; **can be painted over** |
| Cost | — | **More budget friendly** |
| Life | **Long lifespan — 20 years** | — |

Other tile materials shown: **slate · fibrocement · timber shingles · pressed
metal tiles**.

> **The terracotta/concrete difference is a paint question.** Terracotta is
> glazed, so the colour is fired in and **cannot be painted over**; concrete
> tiles come in all colours **and can be repainted**, which is why they are
> the budget option and why an ageing concrete tile roof can be refreshed but
> a terracotta one cannot.

### 2. Sheeted roofs

Six advantages, all relative to tiles:

- **Light** — ceramic tiles are heavy
- **Quick installation** — pre-crafted sheets
- **Easy to replace** — modular in nature
- **Durable** — chemically treated to resist erosion
- **Wide colour variety**
- **Cheaper**

### 3. Membrane roofs / build-up roofs

- **Solid base of plywood or concrete with multi-layers of a waterproof
  membrane**
- **Not common in Australia**, but used extensively in Europe and North
  America
- Require **bitumen as sealant, which is not suitable in higher summer
  temperatures in Australia**

### 4. Concrete roofs

- **"Walk on" roofs**
- **Expensive to build and maintain**

### 5. Green roofs

- Reduce environmental pollution
- **Improve the energy efficiency of solar panels by reducing the roof
  temperature**
- Healthier environment to live and work in
- **Increase productivity of employees**
- **Mitigate the urban heat island effect**
- **Increase the value of the property**

> **The membrane roof exclusion is climate-driven, and that is the mark.**
> Membrane roofs are not rare in Australia because of cost or technology —
> they are rare because **bitumen sealant is unsuitable at Australian summer
> temperatures**. If a question asks why a common European roof type is
> uncommon here, that is the answer.

> **Green roofs help solar panels, which is counter-intuitive.** Photovoltaic
> output falls as cell temperature rises. A vegetated roof lowers the surface
> temperature around the array, so the panels run cooler and more
> efficiently. The deck lists it explicitly, and it is the sort of detail an
> MCQ likes.

## Roof elements and frames

The deck reproduces three labelled diagrams: **roof elements**, **gable roof
elements**, and **hip roof elements**. The revision deck reproduces all three
again, unlabelled, which is a strong signal.

The conventional roof frame elements to be able to name:

- **Ridge** (or ridge beam) — the apex member the rafters bear against
- **Rafters** — the sloping members carrying the roof battens and covering
- **Hip rafter** — the sloping member at an external corner where two slopes
  meet (hip roofs)
- **Valley rafter** — the sloping member at an internal corner where two
  slopes meet
- **Ceiling joists** — horizontal members carrying the ceiling and tying the
  rafter feet
- **Top plate** — the horizontal member at the head of the wall frame that the
  rafters and ceiling joists sit on
- **Battens** — fixed across the rafters to carry tiles
- **Trusses** — prefabricated triangulated frames replacing the separate
  rafter/ceiling joist arrangement
- **Load-bearing walls** — the walls carrying roof load down to the footings
- **Fascia and eaves** — the trim at the rafter ends and the overhang

> **The gable and hip diagrams differ by two members.** A **gable** roof
> slopes in two directions and has a **vertical gable end wall** that must be
> clad. A **hip** roof slopes on all sides, so it has **hip rafters** at the
> corners and no gable end. Add a valley where two hipped wings meet and you
> have hip and valley. If you can draw and name ridge, rafter, hip rafter,
> valley rafter, ceiling joist and top plate, you can answer any version of
> this question.

## Common roof types

Ten types, with the properties that distinguish them.

| Type | Description | Points to quote |
|---|---|---|
| **Hip** | Traditional form of roof in brick veneer houses | **Cheap to construct**; advantage of **continuous gutter** and **one height walls** |
| **Hip and valley** | Shares general characteristics of a hip roof | Addition of the **valley(s)** may be used to cover **multi-sided plans** |
| **Broken hip and valley** | Shares characteristics of a hip and valley roof | **Allows variation in the width of the wings** |
| **Gable** | Used in many historic period styles | Requires **as much roof covering material as a hip roof**, but also has **exposed gables that have to be clad** |
| **Boxed gable** | The gable end is **extended out beyond the building wall** | **The roof and walls become clearly separated components** |
| **Dutch gable** | Combination of a **hip roof with the addition of mini gables** | **Provides ventilation to the roof space**; **reduces the number of truncated trusses**; adds character |
| **Jerkin head** | Combination of a **gable roof with a hip added at the apex** of the gable | **Suited to a house with an attic room**; **roof line is softened** |
| **Bellcote** | **Hip roof with a continuous veranda around its perimeter** | — |
| **Early Australian lean-to / veranda** | **Gable roof with a skillion roof over the verandah** | **Common style in rudimentary cottages** |
| **Flat roof** | — | **Easier to construct than hip or gable**; **less than 3 degrees fall** |
| **Skillion / lean-to** | Single sloping plane | **Highlight windows can be created**; **requires skilled designers and architects** |
| **Clerestory** | **Combination of gable and skillion roofs** | Possibility of a **window along the roof ridge**; **allows natural light and ventilation** |

> **Hip versus gable is the comparison the unit asks for.** Both use **about
> the same amount of roof covering material** — that is the counter-intuitive
> part. The difference is that the **gable has exposed gable ends that must be
> clad**, an extra material and an extra weathering detail; and the hip gives
> a **continuous gutter** on all four sides and **walls all one height**,
> which simplifies both the wall framing and the drainage.

> **Flat roofs are not flat.** The definition on the slide is **less than 3
> degrees fall** — there is still a fall, because standing water is what
> destroys a roof. A "flat" roof with genuinely zero fall is a defect.

> **Three roof types exist mainly to let light and air in.** Dutch gable
> (ventilation to the roof space), skillion (highlight windows), clerestory
> (window along the ridge, natural light and ventilation). If a question asks
> which roof form improves daylight or ventilation, those are the three.

## Interior linings

| Lining | Points |
|---|---|
| **Plasterboard / fibrous plaster** | **Universal wall and ceiling lining materials**; flush finish at **low cost**; can be painted or wallpapered; **10 mm thick on 450 mm stud spacings**; **13 mm thick on 600 mm stud spacings**; sheets are **1,200 mm and 1,350 mm wide**; **fixed horizontally** |
| **Fibre cement cladding** | **Does not contain asbestos any more**; used with a flush joint just like plasterboard; can have featured patterns from tiles to wood grain; **little affected by water**; **cannot burn**; **good base for tiled walls** |
| **Timber lining boards** | — |
| **Medium density fibreboard (MDF)** | *Advantages*: smooth surface, easy to process, strong stability, affordable, highly decorative, sound insulation performance. *Disadvantages*: **absorbs moisture and swells**, intolerant of heavy pressure, **not suitable for outdoor use**, **not resistant to high temperatures** |
| **Plywood** | Popular lining in the **1920s and 1930s**; could be used to line **all the surfaces of the home including the floor and ceiling** |
| **Masonry** | May be left in **manufactured colours**, built into **particular patterns**, or **painted and rendered** |

> **The plasterboard thickness/stud spacing pairing is a calculation-style
> fact.** **10 mm on 450 mm studs; 13 mm on 600 mm studs.** Wider stud
> spacing means a longer unsupported span, so the sheet has to be thicker.
> If an MCQ offers "13 mm on 450 mm" it is testing whether you memorised the
> pair or the logic — either works, but the logic is safer.

> **Fibre cement's three selling points are all about water and fire.**
> "Little affected by water", "cannot burn", "good base for tiled walls" —
> which is why it is the wet-area lining. MDF is its opposite on every count:
> absorbs moisture and swells, not for outdoor use, not resistant to high
> temperatures. A "which lining for a bathroom?" question is really a fibre
> cement versus MDF question.

## Windows

**Purpose:** allow daylight into a building; a source of natural ventilation.

**Windows should be:**

- Reasonably strong and secure
- Reasonably waterproof and airtight
- Able to provide resistance to the passage of heat
- Durable and maintenance-free
- Able to provide reasonable sound insulation

**A trend to quote.** In **2021**, the Swiss company **Air-Lux** produced the
first-ever **sliding window system for skyscrapers**. Until then, high-rise
experts broadly held that sliding windows were **not suitable for high-rise
buildings** because of the demands of **air- and water-tightness** and their
**limited ability to accommodate building movement**.

**Thermal windows** — design features:

- **Multiple panes of glass** (double or triple glazing)
- **Special coatings on the glass (e.g. low-e)** — designed to **slow radiant
  heat transfer**
- **Gas filled air gaps**
- **Warm edge spacers**
- Frames with **multiple internal chambers**
- **Centre sealing systems**
- **Reducing or eliminating thermal bridges**

### Common window types

| Type | Frame | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Fixed light** | Timber or aluminium | — |
| **Horizontal sliding** | Generally aluminium | Produced in **standard size**; **not all are weather tight** |
| **Top hung awning** | Generally timber | Standard size; some have clips and friction stays, others use positive action window openers |
| **Side hung casement** | Generally timber | **Purpose made**; stay-openers or winders may be used |
| **Double hung** | Generally timber | **Purpose made** |
| **Louvre** | Aluminium, steel or timber | **Purpose made**; generally fixed to a timber sub-frame; **not suitable in cold climates due to poor sealing** |
| **Bottom hung hopper** | Timber | Purpose made; **hard to achieve 100% water tightness**; **open sashes interfere with curtains** |

> **"Standard size" versus "purpose made" is the hidden classification.**
> Horizontal sliding and top hung awning are **standard size** products
> (cheap, off the shelf, generally aluminium or timber). Casement, double
> hung, louvre and hopper are **purpose made**. That distinction drives cost
> and lead time and is a neat answer to "what would you specify for a budget
> project?"

> **Three window types have a named weakness.** Horizontal sliding — not all
> are weather tight. Louvre — **poor sealing, unsuitable in cold climates**.
> Bottom hung hopper — hard to achieve 100% water tightness, and the open
> sash fouls curtains. Those three weaknesses are the examinable content.

## Doors

**Characteristics** — seven:

1. Strength and stability
2. Weather tightness
3. Security
4. Privacy
5. Thermal insulation
6. Sound insulation
7. Fire and smoke resistance

**Other considerations:** wheelchair access; fire doors.

### Common door opening types

| Type | Notes |
|---|---|
| **Swing** | Can be **single or double**; can open **90° or 180°**; **trouble free**; provides **excellent security** |
| **Sliding — patio door** | Fabricated with **aluminium frames**; can be fitted with an efficient sliding **insect screen**; provides **good access and ventilation** |
| **Sliding — face of the wall** | Useful where **swinging doors would be inappropriate**; **requires expensive track** |
| **Sliding — cavity** | Modern **pre-hung system built into the house during construction**; relatively trouble free; **major failure of the track in the cavity can be a major nuisance** |
| **Bi-fold** | Useful to **screen large areas and fit into large openings**; **relatively expensive** |

> **The cavity slider's advantage and disadvantage are the same fact.** The
> door disappears into the wall — which is why it saves space, and why a
> track failure inside the cavity is a major nuisance to repair. Face-of-wall
> sliders are the reverse: ugly and expensive track, but accessible.

## Stairs

The regulated numbers. These are the most likely calculation-adjacent facts
in the topic:

```
Risers per flight     not more than 18, not less than 2
Riser and going       height and depth of treads must be CONSISTENT
                      throughout each flight
Solid treads          required if the stair is higher than 10 m or
                      three storeys (i.e. not mesh or perforated metal)
Open stair risers     riser openings must be LESS THAN 125 mm
Balustrade gaps       gaps between railings, vertical or horizontal, must be
                      LESS THAN 125 mm — no opening through which a
                      125 mm diameter sphere can pass
```

**Design** must follow the current **Building Code of Australia**. For
**Building Class 1** (houses and dwellings) and **Class 10** (non-habitable
buildings), construction should adhere to:

- **BCA Part 3.9.1 Stair Construction** and **3.9.2 Balustrades**
- **Australian Standard AS1657:2018**

> **The 125 mm rule appears twice and it is the same rule.** Riser openings in
> an open stair and gaps between balustrade railings both have to be **less
> than 125 mm**, expressed as "no opening through which a 125 mm diameter
> sphere can pass". It is a child-safety dimension. If you remember one
> number from this topic, remember 125 mm.

> **"Not more than 18 or less than 2 risers" is per flight, not per stair.**
> A tall storey is broken into two flights with a landing, precisely because
> of the 18-riser limit. And the riser and going must be consistent *within
> each flight* — a single odd step is a trip hazard and a code breach.

Stairs are also an architectural element: the deck shows the 1 William Street
stair and several feature designs, making the point that stairs have always
been an important part of design and architecture.

<summary>1. Name the five roof covers and give one distinguishing fact about each.</summary>

**Tile** — terracotta is glazed and cannot be painted over (20-year lifespan);
concrete tiles come in all colours, can be painted, more budget friendly.
**Sheeted** — light, quick, easy to replace, durable, wide colour range,
cheaper. **Membrane/build-up** — plywood or concrete base with multi-layer
waterproof membrane; uncommon in Australia because the **bitumen sealant is
unsuitable in high summer temperatures**. **Concrete** — "walk on" roofs,
expensive to build and maintain. **Green** — reduces pollution and the urban
heat island, improves solar panel efficiency by lowering roof temperature,
increases property value.
</details>

<details>
<summary>2. Give two main differences between a hip roof and a gable roof.</summary>

| | Hip | Gable |
|---|---|---|
| Ends | Slopes inward on all sides | **Vertical gable end that must be clad** |
| Gutter and walls | **Continuous gutter, walls all one height** | Gutter interrupted at the gable ends, wall heights vary |

And the counter-intuitive similarity worth stating: they require **about the
same amount of roof covering material** — the gable's extra cost is the
cladding of the exposed ends, not the roof area.
</details>

<details>
<summary>3. Which roof type combines a gable and a skillion, and what does it enable?</summary>

**Clerestory.** It allows a **window along the roof ridge**, admitting
**natural light and ventilation**. (Do not confuse it with jerkin head =
gable + hip at the apex, or Dutch gable = hip + mini gables.)
</details>

<details>
<summary>4. What thickness of plasterboard on 600 mm stud spacings, and why?</summary>

**13 mm.** Wider stud spacing means a longer unsupported span, so the sheet
must be thicker. On 450 mm studs, 10 mm is sufficient. Sheets are 1,200 mm
and 1,350 mm wide and are **fixed horizontally**.
</details>

<details>
<summary>5. Which lining would you specify for a tiled bathroom wall, and why not MDF?</summary>

**Fibre cement.** It is **little affected by water**, **cannot burn**, and is a
**good base for tiled walls** — and it no longer contains asbestos. MDF is the
opposite: it **absorbs moisture and swells**, is intolerant of heavy pressure,
is not suitable outdoors and is **not resistant to high temperatures**.
</details>

<details>
<summary>6. A stair rises 4.2 m in a single flight with 175 mm risers. Is that compliant?</summary>

```
Risers = 4,200 / 175 = 24 risers
```

**No** — a flight must have **not more than 18 risers**. The stair must be
broken into two flights with a landing (e.g. 12 + 12). Also check that the
riser and going are **consistent throughout each flight**, and if the stair is
higher than 10 m or three storeys, the **treads must be solid** rather than
mesh or perforated metal.
</details>

<details>
<summary>7. Why is a 120 mm gap between balustrade railings acceptable but a 130 mm gap is not?</summary>

Because gaps between balustrade railings — vertical or horizontal — must be
**less than 125 mm**, expressed as: the balustrade cannot have any opening
through which a **125 mm diameter sphere** can pass. The same 125 mm limit
applies to **riser openings in an open stair**.
</details>

## Summary

- **Five roof covers**: tile (terracotta glazed and unpaintable, 20-year life;
  concrete paintable and cheaper), sheeted (light, quick, cheap, durable),
  membrane (rare in Australia — bitumen unsuitable in summer heat), concrete
  (walk-on, expensive), green (pollution, heat island, solar efficiency,
  value).
- **Roof elements** to label: ridge, rafters, hip rafter, valley rafter,
  ceiling joists, top plate, battens, trusses, load-bearing walls.
- **Hip** — cheap, continuous gutter, one wall height. **Gable** — same
  covering material but exposed ends to clad. **Dutch gable** — ventilation
  and fewer truncated trusses. **Jerkin head** — gable plus hip at the apex,
  suits an attic. **Flat** — less than 3 degrees fall. **Skillion** —
  highlight windows. **Clerestory** — gable plus skillion, window at the
  ridge.
- **Linings**: plasterboard 10 mm on 450 mm studs / 13 mm on 600 mm, fixed
  horizontally; fibre cement is asbestos-free, water-resistant, non-combustible
  and the tile substrate; MDF swells and is not for outdoors or heat.
- **Windows** must be strong, weather tight, thermally resistant, durable and
  sound-insulating. Sliding, awning = standard size; casement, double hung,
  louvre, hopper = purpose made. Louvres seal poorly; hoppers leak and foul
  curtains.
- **Doors**: seven characteristics; swing gives the best security, cavity
  sliders save space but hide track failures, bi-folds screen large openings
  at a price.
- **Stairs**: **2–18 risers per flight**, consistent riser and going, solid
  treads above 10 m or three storeys, **125 mm** maximum openings in both open
  risers and balustrades. BCA 3.9.1 and 3.9.2, AS1657:2018.
