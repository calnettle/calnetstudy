# Topic 3 — Office Property

Week 3. How the office evolved, the PCA grades, how office towers are built,
the investor's design checklist, the Brisbane CBD market, and NLA.

## The sector

Commercial / office property: **properties devoted to non-retail business and
service activities.**

History in five beats: the need for administrative and professional space
goes back to ancient times; growth during periods of international trade and
the development of accounting and law in the **1600s–1700s**; more growth
during the **industrial revolution**; **earthquake requirements and the
invention of the elevator lift** had a major impact; and then rising
expectations of office standards — prestige, views, architecture, facilities
— as organisations use the building to **attract and retain staff**.

## The evolution of office design

Source: Oktra, 2023. The driver is a shift in *how and where we work*: new
technologies and tools, changing staff needs and work models, and the shift
**from an industrial to a knowledge-based economy**.

| Decade | What changed |
|---|---|
| **1800s** | Offices popularised as a place for conducting business, mainly because of the **railway** roll-out — freight, logistics, importing and exporting needed much larger workforces. Branching hierarchies: merchants and clerks on the lower floors, owners often living above the office. Furniture quality improved higher up the chain of command. |
| **1900s** | Technology revolutionised form and function. **Electric lighting** opened up whole floor plates. The **typewriter, telegraph and telephone** accelerated communication and let organisations manage offices from afar. Linear rows of desks for typists and admin — paperwork assembly lines, managers in separate rooms. |
| **1950s** | Flatter hierarchies. Germany's **'Bürolandschaft'** (office landscape) movement produced the first open-plan offices. Informal break rooms and lounges introduced as an "escape" from the desk. |
| **1960s** | The **cubicle** invented — the "Action Office" by George Nelson and Robert Propst: a three-sided moveable partition for privacy, convenience and less distraction. In the **1980s**, cheap modular walls produced a sea of cubicles worldwide. |
| **1990s** | Looked much like the 1980s at first — private enclosed workstations, bulky PCs with heavy power and data cabling. As **laptops** got cheaper, open plans and **hot-desking** spread to promote flexibility and collaboration. |
| **2000s** | Work-life balance blended into design; "office as playground". Mobile tech let people work away from the desk and popularised **coworking**. |
| **2010s** | **"Agile work"** environments — people, how they work and the outcomes they produce become the focus. **Activity Based Working** emerges and remains popular. |
| **2020s** | Post-COVID acceleration of changes already under way: **remote working, virtual collaboration, self-organising team structures**. Less than **one-third** of digital workers select the corporate office as their preferred place to work. |

> **The decade table is a likely label-the-image question.** The Week 12
> revision deck reproduces it as two rows of unlabelled images (1800s / 1900s
> / 1950s / 1960s, then 1990s / 2000s / 2010s / 2020s). Learn the one-word
> hook for each: railway · electric light · Bürolandschaft · cubicle ·
> hot-desking · playground · agile/ABW · remote.

> **The cubicle was invented to give privacy, not to take it away.** Propst's
> Action Office was a reaction *against* the open typing pool. The sea of
> 1980s cubicles was the cheap corruption of the idea. A question asking "why
> was the cubicle invented?" wants privacy, convenience and reduced
> distraction.

## PCA office building classes

The **voluntary** standard is set by the Property Council of Australia in its
*Guide to Office Building Quality*.

| Grade | Definition and inclusions |
|---|---|
| **Premium** | A **landmark** office building located in major CBD office markets which is a **trendsetter in establishing rents**. Includes: expansive views and outlook; ample natural light; prestige quality access from an attractive street setting; prestige lobby and lift finishes; premium quality lift ride; premium quality amenities; premium presentation and maintenance |
| **Grade A** | High quality office building including: high quality views, outlook and natural light; high quality access from an attractive street setting; high quality lobby and lift finishes; high quality lift ride; high quality amenities; high quality presentation and maintenance |
| **Grade B** | Good quality office building with a **good standard of finish and maintenance** |
| **Grade C** | **Adequate** quality office space |

> **The grade wording is deliberately graded, and that is the answer.**
> Premium = *prestige / premium*; Grade A = *high quality*; Grade B = *good*;
> Grade C = *adequate*. The list of attributes (views, natural light, access,
> lobby and lift finishes, lift ride, amenities, presentation and
> maintenance) is the same at Premium and Grade A — only the adjective
> changes, plus "landmark" and "trendsetter in establishing rents" for
> Premium. If a short answer asks you to distinguish Premium from Grade A,
> lead with **landmark status and rent leadership**, not the amenity list.

### Two Brisbane examples worth quoting

**One One One Eagle Street**

- A tall **'pure' office** building, **195 m** to roof
- **5.5 star NABERS Energy** rating
- Between the Riverside Centre and Riparian Plaza
- Built by Leighton Contractors; **50 floors**; cost **A$335 million**
- **Steel structural frame** — the organic web of columns mimics local fig
  trees, appearing 'arboreal' at night under LED lighting
- Ceiling heights **3.1 m**, fully glazed exterior to maximise views
- Basement excavated while the floors above were being constructed — the
  **top-down method**

**1 William Street**

- Skyscraper, **259.8 m**
- Modernist style, Brisbane CBD
- Developed for the **Queensland Government** as part of a renewed Government
  Administrative Precinct
- Tower construction cost expected at **$538 million**; total development cost
  **over $650 million**
- Completed **October 2016**, staff moved in over six weekends

> **Top-down construction is the exam-worthy detail at 111 Eagle Street.**
> Excavating the basement while the superstructure rises above compresses the
> programme — it is the method to name when a question asks how a
> constrained CBD site accelerates a deep basement.

## Office methods and materials

**Floor slabs and columns**

- Generally reinforced concrete, **post-tensioned** — because of height
  constraints and to space out internal columns
- Loadings
- Carpet for tenancies, vinyl for tearooms, tiles for lobbies — chosen for
  cleaning and maintainability
- **3,500 mm floor to floor** (2,700 mm and 3,000 mm ceiling heights)
- Column spacings suit workstation sizes and **match the basement grid to
  mitigate the cost of a transfer floor**

**External envelope**

Generally a **curtain wall** system: precast concrete panels; composite
panels; glass; aluminium sunscreen.

**Internal**

- Internal walls are **lightweight partitions with minimal column supports
  for flexibility**
- Ceilings generally **tiled grid drop-in panels** in offices, plaster with
  features in lobbies
- High ceilings are difficult to maintain and clean
- **Finishes — common area by the Lessor (owner), tenancy fit-outs by the
  Lessees (tenants)**

**Roofs**

- Generally concrete roofs with **trafficable membranes, insulation and
  ballast**
- **Green roofs** and green walls as Environmentally Sustainable Development
  (ESD) initiatives

> **A transfer floor is a cost you design out, not a feature.** If the office
> column grid does not line up with the basement car park grid, you need a
> deep transfer slab to redirect the loads. Matching the two grids from the
> start avoids it. This is the classic "why does column spacing matter?"
> answer — workstation efficiency above, basement grid below.

### Green roofs

A growing trend for commercial buildings: as concerns about climate change
and dwindling resources grow, design is geared toward minimising negative
environmental impact. Green or living roofs are established on commercial
buildings with **flat or pitched** roofs, and have **economic, social and
environmental** benefits.

Two case studies from the deck:

- **Queensland Children's Hospital** — a large green sloping roof of **1,400
  individual planting cassettes and 23,000 plants**, six transplanted 30-year-
  old fig trees, play areas and roof gardens for recreation and
  rehabilitation.
- **STH BNK by Beulah, Melbourne** — 'Green Spine', a cantilevered structure
  of two towers each with a glasshouse green roof and balcony planters on
  every level. The taller is a **356 m residential** building crowned by a
  publicly accessible botanical garden; the second, **252 m**, holds offices
  and a hotel.

## Investor / developer design factors

Eight criteria — a ready-made eight-point answer, and the first one carries a
number:

```
Efficiency — net to gross area:   < 75% poor      > 85% good
```

- Efficiency — **net to gross area (<75% poor, >85% good)**
- Efficiency — operating cost of HVAC and other services
- Durability of internal/common areas
- Ease of cleaning — internal/external windows
- Efficient/low-maintenance lifts
- Flexibility of use/partitioning
- Security/vandalism
- Safety/sprinklers/alarms

> **Worked example — building efficiency.** A floor plate has a gross area of
> 1,600 sqm. The core (two stairs, eight lifts, lobby, services), the toilets
> and a riser take up 265 sqm.
>
> ```
> Net area   = 1,600 - 265        = 1,335 sqm
> Efficiency = 1,335 / 1,600      = 83.4%
> ```
>
> **83.4% — between the thresholds.** Not "poor" (<75%) but not "good"
> (>85%) either. A 3 percentage point gain to clear 85% would need the core
> to shrink by about 25 sqm, which is roughly one lift shaft. That is the
> trade-off: more lifts means better lift service and worse efficiency, and
> the investor pays for the non-lettable area either way.

## Brisbane CBD office market

Sources: Cushman & Wakefield 2025 and Knight Frank 2025, as cited in the
Week 3 deck.

**Economy.** Conditions remain steady, supported by solid domestic demand and
a strong labour market, though inflationary pressure has been more persistent
than expected. **Momentum is expected to build through 2026.**

**Supply**

| Timing | Building |
|---|---|
| Current | 205 North Quay |
| Current | 360 Queen Street |
| 2027 | 450 Queen Street |
| 2028 | Waterfront Brisbane, 1 Eagle Street |

**Rents and incentives**

| | Prime | Secondary |
|---|---|---|
| Gross face rent growth, 2025 | **+6.7%** (speed of growth moderating from earlier highs) | **+5.1%** |
| Average incentives | **37.75%** — fell 15 bps in the quarter | **39.5%** — stable q/q |

> **Nearly 40% incentives is the number to remember about Brisbane offices.**
> Prime face rents grew 6.7% in 2025 while incentives sat at 37.75%. On a
> gross face rent of $1,000/sqm with a 37.75% incentive, the effective rent
> is roughly $622/sqm — the tenant is getting more than a third of the lease
> term free or as fit-out contribution. A report that quotes face rent growth
> without the incentive is telling half the story.

> **A falling incentive is a strengthening market.** Prime incentives *fell*
> 15 bps to 37.75%. That is landlords regaining pricing power — the same
> direction of travel as tightening yields. Secondary incentives were flat at
> 39.5%, i.e. secondary landlords are still fighting harder for tenants.

## Net Lettable Area (NLA)

**Applications:** office buildings; office and business parks.

**Method:** NLA is the aggregate of floor lettable space at **each level**,
measured in square metres —

- from the **internal surface** of the dominant portion of external walls,
  firewalls and service areas
- to the **centreline** of inter-tenancy walls or partitions

**Include:** window mullions; window frames; structural columns or piers;
**engaged perimeter columns**; **fire hose reels attached to a wall**;
additional facilities for individual tenants.

**Exclude:**

- Areas set aside for the provision of services (electrical ducting,
  air-conditioning risers, fire hose reel cupboards, plant or motor rooms)
- Areas dedicated as public spaces not for the exclusive use of tenants
  (**foyer and atrium**)
- Lift lobbies, lift shafts, stairs and escalators
- **Toilets**

**Sub-divided floors** (PCA 2008, p.27):

- Measure to the **centre line** of inter-tenancy walls or partitions
- Where walls or partitions adjoin **public areas** (lobbies and corridors),
  measure to the **line of the dominant portion of their public-area faces**

> **The sub-divided floor rule is asymmetric and that is the whole point.**
> Tenant-to-tenant → **centreline** (they share the wall, so they share the
> thickness). Tenant-to-public-corridor → **the corridor face** of the wall.
> The tenant does *not* get half the corridor wall, because the corridor is
> the landlord's. Draw it if you need to: the measured line hugs the far side
> of the wall on a shared boundary and the near side on a common-area
> boundary.

> **A tenant reception desk in the public foyer is still excluded.** The deck
> poses the question — "what if a tenant may want to provide a
> security/reception facility within a building's public space?" — right in
> the middle of the exclusion list. The foyer is not for the exclusive use of
> that tenant, so it stays out of NLA. If the tenant wants that space
> counted, it has to be leased and demised, which changes it from public
> space to tenancy.

> **NLA and GLAR share almost the same include list.** Mullions, frames,
> structural columns, engaged perimeter columns, wall-mounted fire hose
> reels, additional tenant facilities — identical. The differences are in
> where you stop (NLA: centreline of inter-tenancy partitions; GLAR: also
> the mall/shop line rule) and that GLAR names escalators and the under-1.5 m
> rule. Do not learn them as two unrelated lists.

## Checkpoint

<details>
<summary>1. Distinguish a Premium building from a Grade A building in one sentence.</summary>

Premium is a **landmark** building in a major CBD market that is a
**trendsetter in establishing rents**, with prestige/premium finishes
throughout; Grade A is a high quality building with high quality views,
access, lobby, lift ride, amenities and maintenance — but without the
landmark status or rent leadership.
</details>

<details>
<summary>2. A floor plate is 1,850 sqm gross. Core, toilets and risers are 310 sqm. Is the efficiency good, poor, or neither?</summary>

```
Net        = 1,850 - 310   = 1,540 sqm
Efficiency = 1,540 / 1,850 = 83.2%
```

**Neither** — above the "poor" threshold of 75% but below the "good"
threshold of 85%. Say so explicitly; the two thresholds leave a band in the
middle and the exam expects you to notice it.
</details>

<details>
<summary>3. Why do office column spacings need to match the basement grid?</summary>

To avoid a **transfer floor**. If the tower grid does not align with the car
park grid, a deep transfer slab is needed to redistribute the column loads,
which is expensive and eats floor-to-floor height. Column spacing above also
has to suit workstation sizes, so it is a two-sided constraint.
</details>

<details>
<summary>4. Under NLA, where do you measure to on (a) a wall shared with the neighbouring tenancy and (b) a wall facing the common corridor?</summary>

(a) The **centreline** of the inter-tenancy wall or partition. (b) The **line
of the dominant portion of the public-area face** — i.e. the far side of the
wall, on the corridor. The tenant is not charged for half of a wall the
landlord owns on both sides.
</details>

<details>
<summary>5. Brisbane prime office incentives are 37.75% and prime gross face rents grew 6.7% in 2025. What is the gross effective rent on a $1,050/sqm face rent, and what does the incentive movement (down 15 bps) tell you?</summary>

```
Effective = 1,050 x (1 - 0.3775) = 1,050 x 0.6225 = $653.63 /sqm
```

The 15 bps fall in incentives means landlords are clawing back pricing power
— a modest strengthening. Face rent growth of 6.7% understates nothing here,
but if incentives had *risen*, effective growth would have been well below
6.7%.
</details>

<details>
<summary>6. Which of these are excluded from NLA: an engaged perimeter column, an electrical duct, a wall-mounted fire hose reel, a fire hose reel cupboard, the lift lobby, the toilets?</summary>

Excluded: **electrical duct, fire hose reel cupboard, lift lobby, toilets.**
Included: **engaged perimeter column, wall-mounted fire hose reel.** The
reel/cupboard pair is the trap.
</details>

## Summary

- Office = properties devoted to **non-retail business and service
  activities**; the sector's growth tracks trade, the industrial revolution,
  the lift, and now the knowledge economy.
- Design evolution: railway (1800s) → electric light and typewriter (1900s) →
  Bürolandschaft open plan (1950s) → cubicle (1960s) → hot-desking (1990s) →
  playground (2000s) → agile / ABW (2010s) → remote and hybrid (2020s), with
  under a third of digital workers now preferring the office.
- PCA grades are a **voluntary** standard: Premium (landmark, rent
  trendsetter, prestige) → A (high quality) → B (good) → C (adequate).
- Construction: post-tensioned RC floor plates, **3,500 mm floor to floor**,
  curtain wall envelope, lightweight partitions, drop-in ceiling grid,
  concrete roof with trafficable membrane. Column grids must match the
  basement to avoid a transfer floor.
- Investor efficiency test: **net to gross <75% poor, >85% good.**
- Brisbane CBD 2025: prime gross face rents +6.7% with **37.75% incentives**;
  secondary +5.1% with 39.5%. Supply: 205 North Quay and 360 Queen Street
  now, 450 Queen Street in 2027, Waterfront Brisbane in 2028.
- **NLA** measures from the **internal** surface of external walls to the
  **centreline** of inter-tenancy partitions — but to the **public-area face**
  where the partition adjoins a lobby or corridor. Toilets, foyers, atria,
  lift lobbies, shafts, stairs, escalators and service areas are all out.
