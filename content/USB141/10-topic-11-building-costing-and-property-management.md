## Method 1 — Building cost per square metre

**What the rate is and is not:**

- Provides the **average cost range** for a wide selection of typical
  buildings
- **$/m² costs are recommended for use in initial feasibility studies only**,
  but they also provide the **base figure in the valuation of buildings** —
  and when used that way they **should be adjusted** as described in the
  Elemental Building Rates section
- The figures given are for a **typical building on a flat site**, so
  adjustments must be made for: **sloping sites, ground conditions, unusual
  shape, ESD initiatives** and other design considerations

**How the area is measured — two rules that carry marks:**

```
The costs given are based on the TOTAL FLOOR AREA OF ALL LEVELS,
measured between the INNER FACES OF EXTERNAL WALLS.

Rates EXCLUDE parking areas.
Refer to the relevant sections for basement or open parking areas.
```

> **The costing area basis is none of GLA, GLAR or NLA.** It is the total
> floor area of all levels **measured between the inner faces of external
> walls** — that is gross floor area, and it includes the core, the toilets,
> the lift shafts and everything else a lettable-area method throws away.
> Using an NLA figure in a $/m² estimate under-states the cost. This is the
> single most likely way to lose marks on a costing question.

> **Rates exclude parking. Always.** Both the deck and the revision deck
> repeat it. Price the building, then price the parking separately from the
> parking section, then add.

### Worked example — a Brisbane apartment building

*40 basic-standard apartments averaging 77 m², each with an 8 m² reinforced
concrete balcony, plus 40 open car spaces. Brisbane rates.*

**Step 1 — building.** The rate is per m² of total floor area of all levels
measured between inner faces of external walls; the 77 m² average already
includes lettable area, circulation, common/support and plant space, so
`40 × 77` is the right area.

```
GFA = 40 apartments x 77 m2         = 3,080 m2

Low   3,080 x 3,395                 = $10,456,600
High  3,080 x 3,660                 = $11,272,800
Mid   3,080 x 3,527.50              = $10,864,700
```

**Step 2 — balconies (excluded from the base rate).**

```
Balcony area = 40 x 8 m2            = 320 m2
Low   320 x 890                     =    $284,800
High  320 x 1,525                   =    $488,000
```

**Step 3 — parking (excluded from the base rate).** Either route:

```
Per car:  40 x 4,865                =    $194,600   (low)
          40 x 6,690                =    $267,600   (high)

Per m2:   40 x 32 = 1,280 m2
          1,280 x 152                =    $194,560   (low)
          1,280 x 209                =    $267,520   (high)
```

**Step 4 — total.**

```
Low   10,456,600 +   284,800 + 194,600 = $10,936,000
High  11,272,800 +   488,000 + 267,600 = $12,028,400
```

**Answer: approximately $10.94 m to $12.03 m.** Then state the adjustments
still required — sloping site, ground conditions, unusual shape, ESD
initiatives, external works and services.

**Sanity check against the per-apartment row:**

```
40 x 261,300 = $10,452,000     vs building-only low  $10,456,600  ✓
40 x 281,700 = $11,268,000     vs building-only high $11,272,800  ✓
```

The two routes agree to within 0.05% — the small gap is Rawlinsons rounding
the $/m² rate to the nearest $5 and the per-apartment figure to the nearest
$100.

### Worked example — an elemental adjustment

*Price a Brisbane basic-standard apartment building, but with **ducted
air-conditioning** instead of split systems.*

Take the Basic elemental column and substitute the High standard's Mechanical
rate:

```
Basic total (Sydney basis)              = $3,445.00 /m2
Remove Basic mechanical                 -    $52.75
Add High-standard ducted mechanical     +   $552.00
                                          -----------
Adjusted rate (Sydney basis)            = $3,944.25 /m2
```

That is a **14.5% uplift** on the base rate, for one element.

For a 3,080 m² building:

```
Unadjusted   3,080 x 3,445.00 = $10,610,600
Adjusted     3,080 x 3,944.25 = $12,148,290
Difference                    =  $1,537,690
```

Then note two things in your answer: the rate is on a **Sydney** basis, so a
Brisbane job needs the city relativity applied; and the adjustment assumes
everything else — plumbing, electrical, fitments — stays at basic standard,
which in practice a ducted-air building rarely does.

## Three errors in the deck's cost tables

These are verifiable from the deck's own numbers, and they are worth knowing
because they show how to sanity-check a rate table.

**1. The Medium standard's average apartment size does not match its
per-apartment figures.**

The description says the average apartment is **110 m²**. Divide each city's
per-apartment figure by its $/m² rate:

```
Adelaide   354,000 / 3,505 = 101.0     381,500 / 3,780 = 100.9
Brisbane   367,500 / 3,640 = 101.0     396,000 / 3,920 = 101.0
Melbourne  337,500 / 3,340 = 101.1     363,500 / 3,600 = 101.0
Perth      380,500 / 3,765 = 101.1     410,000 / 4,060 = 101.0
Sydney     359,500 / 3,555 = 101.1     387,500 / 3,835 = 101.0
```

**Every city implies about 101 m², not 110 m².** The other three standards
reconcile perfectly (Basic → 77.0, High → 168.1, Prestige → 277.0), so this
is specific to the Medium row. Either the descriptor should read 101 m² or
the per-apartment figures belong to a different unit size.

**Practical consequence: use the $/m² rate and your own measured area.** If
you take the per-apartment figure and divide by 110 you get $3,341/m² for
Brisbane, when the table says $3,640–3,920 — an 8% error.

**2. The Adelaide Prestige per-apartment figure has an extra digit.**

The slide shows Adelaide prestige as **$11,450,000 – $1,563,000**. The upper
bound is smaller than the lower bound, which is impossible, and:

```
11,450,000 / 5,235 = 2,187 m2      (absurd for an apartment)
 1,450,000 / 5,235 =   277.0 m2    ✓ matches the stated 277 m2
```

It should read **$1,450,000**.

**3. The elemental total is Sydney, not the city you are pricing.**

Covered above. It is not an error in the source — the total row says so — but
it is the mistake students make, so treat it as one.

> **Confirm the Medium-standard size with Dr Chia before an exam.** All three
> findings are arithmetic, not opinion, but the first one changes an answer.
> If a costing question hands you the Medium standard and says "110 m²", use
> **110 m² and the $/m² rate** (that is what the question is testing), and add
> one line noting that the per-apartment column implies 101 m². You get the
> mark for the method and the credit for the check.

## Part B — Property Management

## Strategic Asset Management (SAM)

The **RICS** definition, quoted verbatim in both the Week 11 deck and the
revision deck:

> The process which aligns business and property asset strategies, ensuring
> the optimisation of an organisation's property assets in a way which best
> supports its key business goals and objectives.

**What SAM sets out to do:**

- **Maximise the returns to stakeholders**
  - Value of portfolio (**owner**)
  - Business performance (**tenant**)
- **Minimise risk**
- **Managing the certainty of cash flow**
- **Meeting investment objectives**
- **Value adding**

> **SAM serves two different stakeholders with two different metrics.** The
> owner wants **portfolio value**; the tenant wants **business performance**.
> Naming both is what distinguishes a SAM answer from a generic
> "property management" answer.

## The formula

```
Capitalisation rate = Net income / Capital value

Capital value (CV)  = Net income / Capitalisation rate

Net income (NI)     = Gross income - Outgoings
```

The capitalisation rate is used in commercial real estate to indicate **the
rate of return that is expected to be generated on a real estate investment
property**. It is the **risk-rating component**.

### The four value components

In order to maximise the property to its best value, the asset manager must
have a strategy for **all four**:

```
1. Gross rent
2. Outgoings
3. Capitalisation rate — risk rating component
4. Capital value
```

### Gross income strategies

- **Expenditure to maximise rent increases**
- **Tenant mix** to maximise patronage and turnover
- **Promotional activities** to increase patronage
- **Diversify income sources** — signage, licences, car parking and kiosks
- **Timely market reviews**
- **Minimise vacancy periods**
- **Manage delinquencies**
- **Active lease renewal strategies**

### Outgoings strategies

- Employ **new technologies** to reduce utilities and maintenance costs
- **Installation of timers and meters**
- **Individual metering for user pays**
- **Structure lease terms for maximum recoverable**
- **Negotiate long-term service contracts**
- **Tender service contracts**
- **Lobby government for reduced statutory costs**
- **Preventive maintenance regime**

### Capitalisation rate strategies

The cap rate range is **market driven**, but the **quality of the asset is
manageable**:

- Income and outgoings strategies
- **Attractive WALE**
- Frequency and terms of **market reviews**
- **Good quality tenants**
- **Historical low vacancies**
- **Limited arrears**
- **Few disputes**
- **Well maintained**

### Capital value strategies

A function of all the other factors, but also:

- **Protecting the property rights**
- **Monitoring the local environment**
- **Adapting property to changing needs**
- **Acquisition and sales**
- **Conducting regular valuations**
- **Funding considerations**

> **You cannot move the market's cap rate, but you can move where your asset
> sits within the range.** That is the deck's own framing — "cap rate range is
> market driven, but the quality of asset is manageable". Long WALE, good
> tenants, low historical vacancy, limited arrears, few disputes, well
> maintained: every one of those is a risk reducer, and lower risk means a
> lower cap rate means a higher capital value.

### Worked examples

**Example 1 — the basic calculation.**

A commercial property produces gross income of **$850,000** with outgoings of
**$230,000**. The market cap rate for comparable assets is **6.25%**.

```
Net income     = 850,000 - 230,000        = $620,000
Capital value  = 620,000 / 0.0625         = $9,920,000
```

**Example 2 — what is a dollar of saved outgoings worth?**

The multiplier is simply the reciprocal of the cap rate:

```
Value per $1 pa of net income = 1 / CR

at 5.5%  ->  $18.18
at 6.0%  ->  $16.67
at 6.25% ->  $16.00
at 7.0%  ->  $14.29
```

So cutting **$20,000** of **non-recoverable** outgoings at a 6.25% cap rate:

```
20,000 x 16.00 = $320,000 of capital value
```

**Example 3 — the manageable cap rate.**

Take the same property with net income of $620,000. Suppose an active
management programme — re-letting to better-covenanted tenants, lengthening
the WALE, clearing arrears — moves the asset from the weak end of the market
range to the strong end, 6.75% to 5.75%:

```
At 6.75%   620,000 / 0.0675  = $9,185,185
At 5.75%   620,000 / 0.0575  = $10,782,609
Difference                   =  $1,597,424
```

**$1.6 million of value created with no change to the income at all.** That is
what "the quality of the asset is manageable" means in dollars.

> **The cap rate is in the denominator — lower is better.** A "cap rate that
> improved from 6% to 7%" is backwards: 7% is a *higher* required return,
> which means *more perceived risk* and a *lower* value. Compression (falling
> cap rate) raises value; expansion (rising cap rate) destroys it.

> **Only non-recoverable outgoings capitalise into the owner's value.** If the
> lease makes the outgoing recoverable, the tenant pays it and cutting it
> benefits the tenant, not the landlord. That is why "structure lease terms
> for maximum recoverable" sits on the outgoings strategy list, and it is the
> same split-incentive point that blocks energy retrofits in Topic 9.

## Building management

```
Building management is the process required to achieve and sustain defined
levels of building performance throughout the life of the building.
```

Note: **building management, property management, asset management and
facility management are sometimes used interchangeably.**

**Benefits of efficient building management:**

- Effective risk management
- **Extended asset life**
- **Reduced operating costs**
- Compliance with statutory obligations
- Provision of a healthy work environment
- **Performance and investment predictability through a life-cycle approach**
- Alignment of building performance with users' expectations, relevant to
  productivity and service delivery
- Improved user/occupant satisfaction
- Enhanced community perceptions
- Environmental compatibility

**Risks of poor building management:**

- **Degradation of asset base**
- **Premature asset failure**
- **Shorter asset useful life**
- **Higher repair and replacement costs**
- Missed opportunities to identify and implement improvements to reduce
  environmental impacts
- Unnecessary exposure to legal, social and other risks associated with
  deteriorated buildings, statutory non-compliance, workplace health and
  safety, and hazardous materials

### The four aspects of building management

| # | Aspect | What it covers |
|---|---|---|
| **1** | **Building occupancy** | Activities required to enable the spaces to be occupied. Encompasses **occupancy management** (management of available space to optimise utilisation and maximise economic performance) and **lease management** (the legal and contractual framework, defining the requirements to be met by the **lessor and lessee**) |
| **2** | **Building operation** | Activities necessary to operate the building and its services efficiently: **control and monitoring of building services and plant operations**; facilitation of building operation activities; **coordination and direction of contractors and employees of supply authorities**; effective communication with occupants and users; **identification and management of hazardous substances** |
| **3** | **Building maintenance** | **Reinstating physical condition** to a specified standard; **preventing further deterioration**; **restoring correct operations**; **replacing components at the end of their useful/economic life with modern equivalents**; **temporary repairs for immediate health, safety and security reasons**; **mitigation of the consequences of a natural disaster**; assessing buildings for maintenance requirements |
| **4** | **Building upgrades** | Optimise the **utility, amenity and functionality** of a building and **increase its useful life** to meet future service delivery requirements: **renovations, retrofitting, refurbishments, alterations, extensions, improvements** |

> **Aspect 4 is Topic 9 wearing a different hat.** Renovation and retrofit
> appear here as *building upgrades* — one of the four aspects of building
> management, with the explicit purpose of increasing useful life to meet
> **future** service delivery requirements. If a question links property
> management to retrofitting, this is the hinge.

## Life cycle costing

**Terminology** — the same concept under many names:

```
Life-cost                    (Canada & New Zealand)
Life cycle cost              (Australia, UK & US)
Costs-in-use                 (the original term)
Operational cost, running cost
Occupancy cost, functional-use cost
Terotechnology
Whole-of-life cost (WOL)
```

**Definition** (Langston, 2005):

> The economic assessment of competing design alternatives, considering all
> significant costs of ownership over the economic life expressed in
> equivalent dollars.

And more plainly: *"The total cost of an asset over its operating life,
including initial acquisition costs and subsequent running costs"*:

```
Life cycle cost = Initial design + acquisition costs + running costs
```

**The approach:**

- Measurement of costs is generally undertaken on a **capital cost basis**
- **Capital cost evaluation can lead to society paying more for buildings**
- The whole-of-life-cycle cost approach **also considers operating costs**
- **Not in widespread use in many countries**

**Effective in four main areas** (life cycle costing in decision making):

1. **Identifies the total cost** undertaken in acquisition of the asset
2. **Facilitates an effective choice between alternative methods** by taking
   account of options with differing capital and running costs
3. **Details all capital, running and replacement costs**
4. **Identifies those areas in the operating costs that might be reduced**

**Why it has not caught on:**

- The view taken to date is that **initial costs are clear and visible and
  longer-term benefits are not**
- Decisions in the building process in Australia have predominantly been made
  on the **comparison of initial capital costs**, justified on the grounds
  that:
  - The capital cost is the single most important commitment and all other
    costs are unimportant
  - The capital cost is the single most important cost and there are no real
    benefits to be gained from reduced running costs and increasing capital
    costs
- Decisions **can** be made on the total life cost of an asset rather than
  just the initial cost, **as future costs are as important as those incurred
  in the capital acquisition**

**Importance:**

- The developer should examine the **total cost implications** of any decision
- **Ignorance on the part of the client should not be discounted** — the
  majority of building clients are **not in the business of buildings**
- Some developers may not be aware of the importance of life cost in decision
  making
- **The life cost process should be implemented as early as possible in the
  development to gain the maximum effect**

**Basic elements of implementation:**

1. Identification of an **overall time period** of life cost application to
   all possibilities evaluated (taking account of varying life cycles)
2. **Inclusion of all costs and revenues** attributed to the project by time
   period, including initial investment, recurring costs, and
   revenues/proceeds from sale or other disposal
3. **The effect of time**, including allowance for inflation on costs and
   revenue generated in future years — the **time value of money**

### The seven stages of life cycle costing analysis

```
1. Establish the objective
2. Choice of feasibility method
3. Formulate assumptions
4. Identify costs and the life cycle
5. Compare costs and rank the alternatives
6. Sensitivity analysis
7. Investigate capital cost constraints
```

**Defining the life cycle** is an essential element. It is important to
distinguish between the **building life cycle** and that of the **building
components**. The life of a building is complex, involving many
**obsolescence** issues:

```
Physical  ·  Economic  ·  Functional  ·  Technological  ·  Social  ·  Legal
```

> **Six types of obsolescence, and only one of them is about the building
> falling down.** *Physical* is deterioration. *Economic* is when the site is
> worth more redeveloped. *Functional* is when the floor plate no longer suits
> how people work. *Technological* is when the services cannot support modern
> equipment. *Social* is changing expectations. *Legal* is a code change the
> building cannot meet. A Grade B office is usually **functionally and
> technologically** obsolete long before it is physically worn out — which is
> exactly the retrofit-versus-demolish question from Topic 9.

> **The 5% green premium is a life cycle costing argument.** Topic 9's
> "construction costs about 5% higher, covered within the first 3–4 years"
> only makes sense on a whole-of-life basis. Judged on capital cost alone —
> which the deck says is how Australian decisions are predominantly made — the
> green building loses every time. That is the deck's whole point about
> "capital cost evaluation can lead to society paying more for buildings".

## Value management

- **VM** is the overall name given to a **collection of specific principles,
  techniques and practices** which have been proven effective in **maximising
  value to all stakeholders**
- VM is the **structured analysis of function**, which provides a systematic
  process for **problem-solving and improving value**
- VM seeks to **satisfy client needs by ensuring that all necessary functions
  are provided at the lowest total cost, while maintaining the required
  levels of quality and performance**

> **Value management is not cost cutting.** The definition has three
> constraints, not one: **all necessary functions provided**, at the **lowest
> total cost**, **while maintaining required quality and performance**. Strip
> out a function or drop below the required performance and it is no longer
> value management. The distinctive method is the **structured analysis of
> function** — asking what each element is *for* before asking what it costs.

**Structure the comparison point by point, not building by building.** The

|---|---|---|

## Summary

- **Rawlinsons Cost Guide** = projects up to **$1.5 m**; **Handbook** = every
  stage of a project, large scale.
- Three estimating levels: **$/m² Building Rates → Elemental Building Rates →
  Elemental Comparative Rates**, over detailed trade rates.
- $/m² rates are for **initial feasibility only**, based on **total floor area
  of all levels between the inner faces of external walls**, for a **typical
  building on a flat site**, and they **exclude parking**.
- Brisbane apartment rates: Basic $3,395–3,660/m² (77 m²), Medium
  $3,640–3,920 (110 m² stated but 101 m² implied), High $4,510–4,860 (168 m²),
  Prestige $5,385–5,805 (277 m²). Balconies and parking are separate adds;
  **all parking rates imply 32 m² per car**.
- The **elemental table totals are Sydney means** — verified: the Basic column
  sums to exactly $3,445.00, the Sydney midpoint. **Mechanical jumps from
  $52.75 to $552.00/m² between split-system and ducted air-conditioning.**
- `CV = NI / CR`, `NI = Gross income − Outgoings`, value of $1 pa of net income
  = `1 / CR`. **Lower cap rate = higher value.** Only **non-recoverable**
  savings capitalise to the owner.
- The four value components: **gross rent, outgoings, cap rate, capital
  value.** The cap rate range is market driven but **asset quality is
  manageable** — WALE, tenant quality, vacancy history, arrears, disputes,
  maintenance.
- **Four aspects of building management**: occupancy, operation, maintenance,
  upgrades.
- **Life cycle cost** = initial design + acquisition + running costs; seven
  analysis stages; six kinds of obsolescence; implement it **as early as
  possible**.
- **Value management** = structured analysis of function, all necessary
  functions at lowest total cost without dropping quality or performance.
