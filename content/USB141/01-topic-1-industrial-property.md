# Topic 1 — Industrial Property

Week 1. The sector, how industrial buildings are classified and built, the
Brisbane market, and the first of the three PCA measurement methods.

## The sector in one paragraph

Industrial is the **most diverse** property sector: mines, processing and
manufacturing plants, sugar mills, metalworks, distribution and transport
hubs, down to small local service industries. It is also **geographically
scattered** — some assets sit close to customers, some in remote rural areas,
and high value-add activities like nanotechnology or instrumentation can
locate anywhere, including a university laboratory or technology park.

### Three categories, many types

| Categories | Types |
|---|---|
| Manufacturing | Service industry (transport, entertainment) |
| Warehouse / Transport | Warehouses, cold stores, food processing |
| Hi-Tech (Research and Development) | Waterfront and marine industry |
| | Light and general industry (car sales, Bunnings) |
| | Heavy industry (factories, manufacturing) — capital- and/or labour-intensive: automobile, industrial machinery, steel, rubber, petroleum |
| | Extractive industry (mining) — processes that extract raw materials from the earth |

Memorise the **three categories** as a set. "Name the three categories of
industrial property" is a two-mark gift.

## Industrial classification (CBRE)

The grading system for industrial stock, from the Q4 2025 CBRE definitions
used in the lecture:

| Grade | Age | Height clearance | Other |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Super Prime** | Less than 6 years | 13.7 m – 14.6 m | Design excellence, ESFR sprinklers **and** docks / on-grade doors, strong truck articulation for loading and unloading |
| **Prime** | Generally 6 – 15 years | Over 10 m and up to 13.7 m | — |
| **Secondary** | Over 15 years | 8 – 11 m | Older style but still very functional |

> **The Prime and Secondary height bands overlap and the age bands do not
> meet.** Prime is "over 10 m up to 13.7 m" and Secondary is "8–11 m", so a
> 10.5 m building satisfies both height tests; and a building of exactly 15
> years is "generally 6 to 15" (Prime) while Secondary is "over 15 years".
> The grade is a judgement across age **and** height **and** specification —
> if an exam question gives you a building that straddles two bands, say so
> and justify the grade you pick on the specification (sprinklers, docks,
> articulation). Do not treat the table as three clean boxes.

## Trends

- New forms of manufacturing and processing are emerging across Australia.
- New firms are **smaller and more specialised**, focused on know-how,
  knowledge, human and social capital.
- Australian industry is moving **away from large-scale, heavy
  manufacturing**.
- Growing sectors need **high skill levels and innovation**, and are **not
  restricted to a particular location**.
- **Globalisation and networks**: storage no longer needs to be local because
  transport moves goods quickly around the world.
- Cyber-crime events plus appetite for data storage and cloud computing drive
  **data centre** demand.

The Week 12 revision deck singles out two of these as the answer to "what are
the trends in industrial buildings?": the move away from large-scale heavy
manufacture, and growth in high-skill sectors that are not location-bound.

### Data centres

Data centres have moved from "niche infrastructure play" to a sought-after
real estate asset. Australia combines rising AI-driven demand, resilient
pricing and a globally competitive cost base; Sydney and Melbourne are the
core Asia-Pacific hubs.

**What they are:** essential infrastructure for storage and management of
computing machines and hardware/software equipment supporting information
technology.

**The main challenge:** power. Energy per unit of floor space is around
**10–50 times** that of a typical commercial office building.

**Key site ingredients** — four of them, and they make a clean four-point
answer:

1. High voltage power in proximity
2. Excellent connectivity (fibre providers)
3. Low risk — not near a railway, not in a flood zone
4. Locations likely to receive planning permission or a building permit

## Land use and zoning

What an industrial site needs:

- Large, flat sites; regular shapes
- Easy access to reliable utilities — water, gas, electricity, ICT
- Proximity to raw materials
- Location within a supply chain
- Access to a suitable workforce
- Accessibility to ports, railways and/or highways
- Ability to store materials and final products
- Contamination control, wind and noise levels
- Small-scale industry separated from hazardous industry

## Characteristics of industrial buildings

General: open floor plans, high ceilings, natural light. **Functionality is
prioritised over aesthetics** — structures are built to support heavy
equipment. Strict compliance with guidelines and legal regulations, and with
permitting and occupancy requirements from local, state and federal agencies.
Mostly on the **outskirts** of a city or town. Special conveyance allowing
24/7 shipping and receiving by air or rail, or highway access.

Then four building types with distinguishing numbers — these numbers are the
examinable part:

| | Manufacturing | Storage & distribution | Flex space |
|---|---|---|---|
| Office content | Typically **less than 20%** | About **20%** (80% storage) | **Over 30%** |
| Clear height | At least 3 m, high ceilings for overhead cranes | Up to **20 m** for racking systems | **4–8 m** (slightly lower) |
| Floor | Heavy-duty to support heavy equipment | — | — |
| Loading | Multiple loading docks | Multiple loading docks, some concrete truck courts | Dock-high or ground-level; older buildings may have semi-docks for box trucks and vans |
| Size | Enormous space for production equipment | Single storey, 5,000–10,000 sqm | — |
| Other | — | Climate-controlled to protect sensitive materials | Ample parking, better landscaping, conference and production areas |

**Sub-types.** Storage and distribution: general warehouse, refrigeration /
cold storage, hazardous material, specialised (e.g. wine storage),
distribution warehouse, truck terminals. Flex space: R&D buildings, data
centre buildings, showroom buildings.

> **Office content is the discriminator between manufacturing, warehouse and
> flex.** Under 20% office → manufacturing. About 20% office / 80% storage →
> storage and distribution. Over 30% office → flex space. If an MCQ describes
> a building by its office percentage, it is testing exactly this.

### Dock versus grade loading

- **Dock loading** — the floor sits at truck-tray height so goods roll
  straight off the trailer. Faster, needs a dock and truck apron, favours
  high-throughput distribution.
- **Grade loading** (on-grade) — the door is at ground level and forklifts
  drive in and out. Cheaper and more flexible for smaller vehicles and
  awkward loads.

Super prime specification asks for **both**, plus strong truck articulation.

## Industrial methods and materials

**Footings and foundations.** Reinforced concrete bored piers, pad footings,
strip footings — the choice depends on the foundation material. A **geotech
report is required** to determine the footing type. Two site conditions to
name: **acid sulphate soils** and **contaminated soils**.

**The portal frame.** The defining industrial structure:

- Generally structural steelwork
- Provides **clear open space** inside the building
- Quick and easy construction
- Finish is often galvanised or primed
- Standard span about **7.5 m between frames**
- Flexible building dimensions to suit the client
- **Careful planning and engineering design of temporary bracing is required
  before erection of frames to avoid collapses**

**Wall cladding.** The external shell providing protection. For industrial
buildings usually **steel cladding**, though concrete tilt panels or cold
room panels also count as cladding. The **roofing sub-contractor** generally
supplies and installs the whole cladding system including flashings, gutters,
downpipes and accessories.

Portal frame components are examined in detail in Topic 7 — that is where the
label-the-diagram question lives.

## The PCA method of measurement — introduction

The **Property Council of Australia (PCA)** acts as an advocate for the
property industry, primarily building owners and managers. Its measurement
guidance is the best-practice approach to measuring floor space as part of
lease negotiations, in three steps:

```
1. Agree on the methods to be used.
2. Agree on the measurements obtained.
3. Negotiate the rental rates.
```

The reference is *Method of Measurement: Commercial*. The **2008 version**
provides guidelines for measuring floor space in leased premises, covering
office, industrial, retail and mixed-use premises.

Two rules to hold on to:

- The method varies with the **type of building** and the **purpose of the
  measurement**.
- The **zoning of the land** on which the building sits must **not** be used
  to decide which measurement approach applies.

The three most important methods:

```
1. NLA   Nett Lettable Area — Office Building
2. GLAR  Gross Lettable Area – Retail
3. GLA   Gross Lettable Area
```

### Mixed-use premises

A warehouse complex with an attached office can be measured two ways:

1. **Dominant use**, or
2. **Apportionment**

> **The PCA recommends apportionment, and so does the unit.** The slide is
> explicit: *"It is recommended that apportionment approach be used"* — the
> office area measured on **NLA**, the warehouse area on **GLA**, and the two
> added. Do not measure the whole thing on one method just because the
> warehouse is bigger. Dominant use is the alternative, not the default.

### Dominant portion of a wall

```
Dominant portion = the portion occupying the MOST surface area
                   of a vertical wall.

Area of glass  >  area of solid wall  →  window is dominant
                                      →  measure to the INSIDE FACE OF GLASS

Area of glass  <  area of solid wall  →  wall is dominant
                                      →  measure to the FINISHED SURFACE OF WALL
```

This is a **per-wall test**, not a per-building one. One tenancy can be
measured to the glass on one elevation and to the plaster on another.

## Gross Lettable Area (GLA)

**Applications:** warehouses; industrial buildings; free-standing
supermarkets; showrooms.

**Method:** GLA for each tenancy is measured in square metres —

- **External building walls** — to the **outside faces** of the dominant
  portion of the external walls
- **Inter-tenancy or common walls** — to the **centre line** of the walls
  (multiple-tenancy case)

**Include:** window mullions; window frames; structural columns or piers.

**Exclude (in a multiple tenanted building):**

- Areas set aside for the provision of services (ducts, air-conditioning
  risers)
- Areas dedicated as public spaces not for the exclusive use of occupiers of
  the floor or building (foyers, atria and access ways)
- Lift lobbies, lift shafts, stairs and toilets

**Single-tenancy buildings:** measure to the external face of the dominant
portion of the wall; internal structural columns are included; **protruding
columns on the external face are not included** in the GLA.

**Multiple-tenancy buildings:** measure to the external face of the dominant
portion of the wall *and* the centre line of the inter-tenancy / common wall;
internal structural columns included; protruding external columns still
excluded.

> **The exclusion list is written "in a multiple tenanted building".** That
> qualifier is on the slide and it matters. In a single-tenancy warehouse the
> whole envelope is the one tenancy, so the toilet is not a common area and
> the Assessment 1 answer takes the full outside-to-outside footprint with no
> deduction. Read the tenancy arrangement before you start deducting.
> Worked through in full in the tutorial note.

> **GLA is the only one of the three that measures to the OUTSIDE.** NLA and
> GLAR both measure to the internal surface. For the same building envelope,
> GLA is therefore always the **largest** number. Never compare a $/sqm rate
> struck on GLA with one struck on NLA.

## Checkpoint

<details>
<summary>1. Name the three categories of industrial property.</summary>

Manufacturing; Warehouse/Transport; Hi-Tech (Research and Development).
</details>

<details>
<summary>2. A single-storey building of 7,000 sqm, 18 m clear height, multiple loading docks, climate-controlled, roughly 20% office. Which type is it, and what grade would you expect?</summary>

**Storage and distribution.** The 80/20 storage-to-office split, the multiple
docks, the climate control and the ceiling height up to 20 m for racking all
match. On grade: 18 m clear height is above the super prime band (13.7–14.6 m)
in this classification, so the height alone does not settle it — you would
need the age and the specification (ESFR sprinklers, docks *and* on-grade
doors, truck articulation) to call it Super Prime, and you should say so.
</details>

<details>
<summary>3. Why does the PCA say land zoning must not decide the measurement method?</summary>

Because the method follows the **building type and the purpose of the
measurement**, not the planning scheme. A showroom on industrial-zoned land
is still measured on GLA because of what the building is; an office building
on industrial-zoned land is still measured on NLA. Zoning describes permitted
use, not physical form.
</details>

<details>
<summary>4. A warehouse tenancy has an attached office. How do you measure it, and why?</summary>

**Apportionment** — warehouse on GLA, office on NLA, then add the two. It is
the PCA's own recommendation. The alternative, dominant use, applies one
method to the whole premises and is the less accurate option.
</details>

<details>
<summary>5. A tenancy's north wall is 60% glazing; its west wall is 30% glazing. Where do you measure to on each?</summary>

North wall: glass exceeds solid wall, so the **window is the dominant
portion** — measure to the inside face of the glass. West wall: solid wall
dominates — measure to the finished surface of the wall. The test is applied
wall by wall.
</details>

<details>
<summary>6. Brisbane industrial vacancy — is it 5.1% or 3.1%?</summary>

Both, depending on who you cite. Knight Frank put it at **5.1% at Q2 2025**;
CBRE put it at **3.1% over 2H25** (Q4 2025 report). Different researchers
measure vacancy differently and the market tightened over the year. Always
quote the source and the date.
</details>

## Summary

- Industrial is the most diverse and most geographically scattered sector:
  three categories (manufacturing, warehouse/transport, hi-tech), many types.
- CBRE grades stock Super Prime / Prime / Secondary on **age, clear height
  and specification**; the bands overlap, so justify the grade.
- Office content separates the building types: **<20% manufacturing, ~20%
  storage and distribution, >30% flex space**.
- The trend is away from large-scale heavy manufacturing towards smaller,
  specialised, high-skill, location-flexible firms — and towards data centres,
  which use 10–50 times the energy per square metre of an office.
- The structure is the **steel portal frame** at about 7.5 m spacing, with
  steel cladding or concrete tilt panels, on bored piers or pads determined by
  a geotech report. Temporary bracing before erection is a safety-critical
  step.
- Brisbane industrial at Q4 2025: vacancy 3.1%, super prime yield 5.5%, super
  prime net effective rent AUD 155/sqm, incentives 15.9%, and only 5% of
  zoned industrial land undeveloped and serviced.
- **GLA** applies to warehouses, industrial buildings, free-standing
  supermarkets and showrooms, measures to the **outside** face of the dominant
  portion, centrelines inter-tenancy walls, includes columns and mullions, and
  excludes services, public space, lifts, stairs and toilets in a
  **multiple-tenanted** building.
