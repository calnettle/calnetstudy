## 6.1 The two independent splits

Students collapse these into one axis and lose marks. They are orthogonal.

| | **Primary** | **Secondary** |
|---|---|---|
| **Question it answers** | **WHO** collected it and **WHY** | |
| Definition | Uncollected — you collect it yourself, for this purpose | Already collected by someone else, for some other purpose |
| Methods / examples | Surveys, questionnaires, interviews, observations, focus groups, case studies | ABS Census, RBA, published market reports, the internet; includes **raw data and compiled data** (e.g. published summaries) |

| | **Quantitative** | **Qualitative** |
|---|---|---|
| **Question it answers** | **WHAT FORM** the data takes | |
| Definition | Information about **quantities** — measurable, written down with numbers: how many, how much, how often | Information about **types** — can be represented by a name or symbol: gender, eye colour, the suburb where you live |
| Character | Must have **measurable difference between points**. **Discrete** = only specific values; **continuous** = any value in an interval | Used to **describe a situation**; can capture any data that is not numerical in nature |
| Tools | Mean, median, mode, average, standard deviation | Thematic analysis, coding, content analysis |
| Methods | Surveys, questionnaires, structured observation | In-depth/semi-structured interviews, focus groups, participant observation, case studies, documentary analysis |
| Source | | Can be obtained via **either primary or secondary** sources |

> **The two splits are independent, and the exam tests the cross-cells.** A
> survey you run is **primary AND quantitative**. An ABS published statistic
> is **secondary AND quantitative**. A newspaper interview quote you cite is
> **secondary AND qualitative**. An interview you conduct is **primary AND
> qualitative**. Draw the 2×2 in the margin before you answer.

> **The tutorial asks: "can you think of qualitative examples that use
> numbers?"** Yes — and that is the point. A postcode, a Likert score, a
> survey respondent ID, a bedroom count treated as a category rather than a
> quantity: all numerals, none of them measuring a quantity. **Numbers are not
> the test. Measurable difference between points is the test.** 4169 minus
> 4170 is not −1 of anything.

This unit relies mostly on **secondary** data, because primary collection
takes time and requires **ethics clearance** — which students do not have
time for in a semester.

## 6.2 The collection methods, with advantages and disadvantages

The Week 8 and Week 9 tutorials give this table twice, which is a strong hint
it is examinable.

| Method | Main data type | Advantages | Disadvantages |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Participant observation** | Qualitative | Explains "what is going on" in a social situation · heightens the researcher's awareness of significant social processes · useful for researchers in their own organisation · virtually all data collected is useful | Time consuming · difficult ethical dilemmas · high role conflict ("colleague" vs researcher) · closeness to the situation leads to **observer bias** · demanding, doesn't suit all researchers · access may be difficult · data recording is difficult |
| **Structured observation** | Quantitative | Anyone trained in the instruments can use it · **reliability is high because it can be replicated** · can record relationships between events · data is valuable because collected **at the time it occurs, in its natural setting** | The observer must be present when the phenomenon occurs · results limited to **overt action or surface indicators**, from which the observer must infer · data slow and expensive to collect |
| **Interviews** (semi-structured, in-depth) | Qualitative | Respondent's perspective in **language natural to them** · opportunity to ask **follow-up questions** · researcher can monitor changes in voice and facial expression | Time consuming compared with a questionnaire |
| **Survey / questionnaire** | Quantitative | Responses gathered in a **standardised** way · quicker to collect · information from a **large group** · easily understood and accepted by people | **Low response rates** · if too many questions, respondents answer **superficially** · time-consuming to construct and test |
| **Case study** | Qualitative | Detailed knowledge of a single case or small number of related cases · can **explore and/or challenge existing theory** or generate new hypotheses · various methods available | Not representative; hard to generalise |
| **Content analysis** | Quantifying | Public documents (**no access problems**) · relatively inexpensive · can be revisited and re-examined · no time pressure, can be conducted anytime · **non-obtrusive** — subjects are unaware and unaffected · clear systems and procedures so reliability and validity are not a problem | Does not have a strong theoretical base · conclusions often considered trite · sample may be incomplete or not directly relevant · data reduction discards a large amount of material at an early stage |

**Choosing a method** depends on your research question — what you are trying
to prove — plus **time, money and resources**.

> **The lecturer's own practice question for Section B is:** *"Compare the
> differences between primary data and secondary data collection methods,
> including the advantages and disadvantages of both."* She then said that
> when she marked it, *"a lot of students discussed the advantages, but forgot
> to discuss the disadvantages."* **Both data types AND both advantages AND
> disadvantages.** That is four things and the question says so.

### The model answer skeleton

```
1.  Define each, one line.
       Primary   = you collect it yourself, for this purpose
       Secondary = already collected by others, for another purpose

2.  Primary — advantages:      current; tailored to your question; you control quality
    Primary — disadvantages:   time; cost; ethics clearance; smaller samples

3.  Secondary — advantages:    fast; cheap; large established datasets (ABS Census)
    Secondary — disadvantages: not tailored; may be dated; you did not control
                               how it was collected

4.  One line on choosing:      depends on the research question, plus time,
                               money and resources

UNDERLINE the key terms as you go.
```

### Treat published reports as literature

The Week 8 lecture is explicit: **treat published reports as literature, and
cite them as you would in a literature review.** Its example:

> "Shopping centres suffered as consumers moved online but companies such as
> Waterman Business Centres have jumped on the opportunity by snapping up
> vacant space and turning it into offices for distributed workers who would
> rather stick closer to home than face long commutes to the city
> (Johnston, 2021)."

**Expert opinion quoted inside a secondary report** has its own citation form:

```
In text:        (Mardiasmo 2024, as cited in KPMG 2025)
Reference list: cite the REPORT, with KPMG as the author
```

For unpublished primary data (an interview you conducted):

```
Reference list: J. Smith, personal communication, April 10, 2026
Direct quotation of an unpublished participant: use participant numbers,
                                                not names — confidentiality
```

> **The distinction being tested is who the *author* is, not who *said* the
> words.** Mardiasmo said it; KPMG published it; KPMG is the author you
> reference. Getting this backwards is a referencing error the unit
> specifically calls out.

## 6.4 Sampling

### Probability sampling

> The chance or probability of each case being selected from the population is
> **known and usually equal** for all cases. You will be able to **estimate
> statistically** the characteristics of the population from the sample. Used
> mostly for survey-based research.

Types: **simple random · systematic · stratified · cluster · multi-stage.**

Four rules from the deck:

1. **Sample size influences generalisability**
2. You will need to **compromise between accuracy and effort**
3. Your sample size may be **dictated by your research objectives**
4. Researchers normally work to a **95% level of certainty**

Steps to conduct probability sampling:

```
Define the population        →  who?
Select a sampling framework  →  selection criteria
Choose a sampling method     →  random / systematic / stratified / cluster / multi-stage
Determine sample size        →  number of participants
Implement random selection   →  how do you actually do it
Collect the data             →  a PILOT SURVEY is necessary
                             →  watch the response rate; plan how to lift participation
```

### Non-probability sampling

> The probability of each case being selected is **not known**. It is
> therefore **impossible to make statistical inferences** about the
> characteristics of the population. You may still generalise, **but not on
> statistical grounds**. Most likely used in market sampling or case study
> research, and relies for its success on **the logic behind your sample,
> rather than its size**.

Types: **quota · purposive or judgemental (e.g. interviewing experts or
relevant stakeholders) · snowball · self-selective · convenience.**

| | Probability | Non-probability |
|---|---|---|
| Selection probability | **Known**, usually equal | **Not known** |
| Statistical inference | Possible | **Impossible** |
| Success depends on | **Size** and randomness | **Logic** of the sample |
| Typical use | Survey research | Market sampling, case study research |
| Methods | Simple random, systematic, stratified, cluster, multi-stage | Quota, purposive/judgemental, snowball, self-selective, convenience |

> **The PRD information session in Week 9 is purposive sampling, and naming
> that is a mark.** You interview one expert — the Chief Economist — because
> of *who they are*, not because they were randomly drawn. That is
> **purposive/judgemental, non-probability sampling**, so you cannot generalise
> from it statistically. You use it to *corroborate* a finding you already got
> from secondary data. Which is exactly what the unit tells you to do with it.

### Examples of probability sampling in practice

| Field | Method | Design |
|---|---|---|
| Healthcare | **Simple random** | Randomly select patients from a hospital database and survey satisfaction |
| Education | **Stratified** | Divide by grade level (elementary/middle/high) and randomly select within each — ensures balanced representation |
| Marketing | **Cluster** | Randomly choose certain cities and survey all customers in those cities |
| Environmental science | **Cluster** | Divide a river into sections, randomly select a subset to test |
| Political polling | **Systematic** | From a registered voter list, select **every 50th** voter |

## 6.5 Descriptive statistics

| Statistic | Definition | Excel |
|---|---|---|
| **Mean** | The average value; gives a good idea of where the centre of a dataset is | `=AVERAGE(range)` |
| **Median** | The **middle number** in an ordered set | `=MEDIAN(range)` |
| **Mode** | The **most frequent** number — the value that occurs most often | `=MODE(range)` |
| **Range** | Spread from lowest to highest | `=MIN(range)`, `=MAX(range)` |
| **Standard deviation** | How spread out the values are; how closely observations cluster around the mean | `=STDEV(range)` or `=STDEV.S(range)` for a **sample**; `=STDEV.P(range)` for the **whole population** |

> **STDEV.S versus STDEV.P is a real MCQ.** `.S` divides by *n−1* and is for a
> **sample**; `.P` divides by *n* and is only correct when your dataset **is
> the entire population**. Property data is almost always a sample of a market,
> so `.S` is the default. Plain `=STDEV()` is the legacy alias for `.S`.

> **Property reporting uses the MEDIAN, and you should be able to say why.**
> Property price distributions are right-skewed: the East Brisbane 2025 house
> data has a median of $1,610,000 and a mean of $2,178,858 — the mean is
> **35% higher** because a handful of $12–15 million sales drag it up. The
> median is robust to those outliers; the mean is not. Every median in this
> unit is a median for that reason.

## 6.6 Presenting results — choosing the chart

This is the table to memorise, because it is a near-certain MCQ.

| Chart | Use it to show |
|---|---|
| **Bar / column chart**, multiple bar, **histogram** | **Highest and lowest values** (histogram: **distribution**) |
| **Line graph** | **Trends over time** |
| **Pie chart**, percentage component bar, stacked bar | **Proportions** — share of a whole |
| **Box plot** | **Distributions** |
| **Scatter plot** | **Relationships between variables** |
| **Table** | Continuous data; exact values; anything a chart would obscure |

Information a diagram can convey: **specific values · highest and lowest
values · trends over time · proportions · distributions · relationships.**

Key components of any chart or graph:

- **Chart title**
- y-axis (vertical) and x-axis (horizontal)
- **Axis labels**
- Plotted data
- **Legend**
- **Reference to the source of the data**

> **Six components, and in the exam you will draw them by hand.** The lecturer
> said the marks she loses students most often in Section C are for graphs she
> cannot read: *"they're just free drawing it and I couldn't tell whether the X
> is supposed to be here or whatever."* **Bring a ruler.** Title, both axis
> labels, and the source line take fifteen seconds and are worth marks that the
> plotted data alone will not earn.

> **Match the chart to the question, not to your taste.** "Which suburb is
> most expensive" is a **bar** chart. "Is the market rising" is a **line**
> graph. "What share of the pipeline is residential" is a **pie** chart. "Does
> price fall with distance from the CBD" is a **scatter** plot — that is
> exactly the Everton Park exercise in Note 02. Picking the wrong chart type is
> a "bad figure" in the Week 10 taxonomy: technically correct, but it obscures
> the answer.

### Ugly, bad and wrong figures

Week 10 is not on the examinable list, but its three-way classification is
compact and worth carrying (Claus Wilke, 2019):

| | Problem | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Ugly** | **Aesthetic** — otherwise clear and informative | Garish colours, dominant background grid, three fonts in three sizes |
| **Bad** | **Perception** — unclear, confusing, overly complicated, or deceiving | Each bar on its own y-axis scale, so the bars look closer together than they are |
| **Wrong** | **Mathematics** — objectively incorrect | No y-axis at all, so bars representing 3, 5 and 4 appear as 1, 3 and 2 |

The underlying principle: *"a data visualization first and foremost has to
accurately convey the data. It must not mislead or distort."*

## 6.7 Qualitative research designs

| Design | What it is |
|---|---|
| **Case study** | Detailed knowledge about a single case or a small number of related cases. Various methods — questionnaires, interviews, observation, documentary analysis. Can explore and/or challenge existing theory, or provide new hypotheses. May be descriptive, illustrative, experimental or explanatory. |
| **Experimental** | Identified with the natural sciences: define a theoretical hypothesis · select samples from known populations · allocate samples to experimental conditions · introduce planned change in one or more variables · measure a small number of variables · control other variables. **Before – treatment – after.** |
| **Action research** | A social-science methodology (Lewin, 1958) seeking **transformative change** through a simultaneous process of taking action and doing research. Assumes the social world is constantly changing and that the researcher and the research are part of that change. The aim is to enter a situation, attempt to bring about change, and monitor the results. |
| **Grounded theory** | Sets out to **discover or construct theory from data**. Starts with observations to generate data and develop theory; predictions are made and tested in further observations; continual reference to the data "grounds" the theory. Used to examine people's actions and interactions. |
| **Ethnography** | Gathers observations, interviews and documentary data to produce detailed and comprehensive accounts of social phenomena. Emanates from **anthropology**. Interprets the social world **from the point of view of the research subjects**. Is time-consuming. |

### Cross-sectional versus longitudinal

| | **Cross-sectional** | **Longitudinal** |
|---|---|---|
| Design | Data from many different individuals or properties **at a single point in time** | **Repeated observations of the same variables** over long periods |
| Purpose | Investigate a phenomenon at a particular time; take a **snapshot** of an ongoing situation | Observe **changes over time**; observations at multiple time points |
| When used | When there are **constraints on time or resources** | When change itself is the object of study |
| Types | — | **Trend** studies (changes within a general population over time) · **cohort** studies (specific subpopulations as they change) · **panel** studies (**the same set of people** over time) |

**The unit's cross-sectional example:** Kershaw and McGreal (2012)
investigated the relationships between **time on market (TOM)** and dwelling
type (detached, semi-detached, home units), location (ten regions) and house
size (main rooms), comparing first to last advertised price across locations
and property types over three 3-month periods, using RP Data. The finding:
**location and house size both have a significant impact on time on market.**

> **Trend, cohort and panel are three different things and the MCQ will make
> you pick.** *Trend* = the general population, re-sampled (different people
> each wave). *Cohort* = a defined subpopulation, e.g. everyone born in 1990.
> *Panel* = **the same individuals**, wave after wave. **HILDA is a panel
> study** — it follows the same 17,000 Australians for life. The **Census is
> cross-sectional**, repeated.

## 6.8 Qualitative analysis methods

| | **Quantifying methods** | **Non-quantifying methods** |
|---|---|---|
| | **Informal methods** | General analytical procedure |
| | **Formal methods** — content analysis, repertory grid | Cognitive mapping |
| | | Data displays |
| | | Grounded theory |
| | | Quasi-judicial methods |

**Informal quantifying methods** identify patterns in recorded communication
by quantifying data informally while reducing it — for example, examining
repetitive or patterned behaviours, or using **scaling** as simple as
labelling data "important" or "not important". The problems are **reliability
(repeatability)** and **validity (does it measure what it purports to
measure)**.

**Content analysis** (a formal method) *systematically converts text to
numerical variables which can be quantitatively analysed*. It requires
**pre-constructed "coding units"**, and material — documents, audio, video —
is then classified according to those units.

### Thematic analysis

> Used to find out something about people's views, opinions, knowledge,
> experiences or values from a set of qualitative data — interview
> transcripts, social media profiles, survey responses, field studies.

The moves: identify **key themes or emerging themes** (repetition, patterned
behaviours) · look for themes **emerging from multiple sources** · **code**
the data.

## 6.9 Interview technique

| Question type | Purpose | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Open** | Allow subjects to define or describe a situation or event; encourage them to reply as they wish | *"What factors are driving jobs growth in Brisbane?"* |
| **Probing** | Explore responses of significance to your research; indicate a particular focus or direction | *"Why has there been a decline in property prices?"* · *"How have changes in the Australian economy affected property prices?"* |
| **Specific / closed** | Obtain specific information, or confirm a fact or opinion | *"Do you think the property bubble will burst?"* · *"Is property price growth currently above the long-term average?"* |

Note that *"Is the number of jobs in Brisbane increasing?"* is listed as an
**open** question in the tutorial even though it invites a yes/no — the
lecturer's framing is about what the question *encourages* the respondent to
do, not its grammatical form.

### When to use interviews

- **Exploratory or explanatory** research — semi-structured and in-depth
- To **probe** for further explanation and expand into areas not originally
  covered
- When respondents **prefer** an interview, using language natural to them,
  over a lengthy questionnaire
- When there are a **large number of complex, open-ended questions**
- When the **order and logic of questions needs to be varied**
- When **time constraints** apply — compare designing and administering a
  questionnaire with conducting an interview
- **Focus group** = 10–12 participants; note the risk of "inconsistency"

### When to use questionnaires

- Most suited to **descriptive and explanatory** research
- In **descriptive** research they identify and describe the **variability**
  in phenomena
- In **explanatory** research they examine and explain **relationships between
  variables**, particularly cause-and-effect
- Can be used in a **multi-method** approach — **triangulation**: using
  different data collection methods within one study to enhance the
  **validity and reliability** of data

Questionnaire design rules: all questionnaires should be **pilot tested**;
the **wording, order and flow** of questions should be logical to the
respondent; your choice is influenced by your research questions and available
resources.

**Delivery modes:**

| Self-administered | Interviewer-administered |
|---|---|
| Online | Telephone |
| Postal | Structured interview |

## 6.10 Designing questions — the do's and don'ts

This section is unusually specific because it is drawn from Week 8, Week 9 and
the Week 9 information session where the questions were actually asked. It is
a good source of Section B material because it forces you to say *what data
answers what question*.

**The purpose** of asking an expert: *to check the outcomes of secondary data
or other research outcomes/reports, and confirm them with expert opinion.*

### Do

- Ask questions **backed up by research** — lead with your own finding, then
  ask for the expert's view
- Be **specific** and locally directed — SEQ, Brisbane, East Brisbane, the
  inner ring
- Be prepared with **follow-up questions**

Good shapes:

> *"Based on the secondary data provided on future project developments, [X]
> number of townhouses are due to be constructed in the suburb over the next
> two years. How do you think this will impact the supply cycle in that area?
> Do you think there is demand for that type of development?"*

> *"The majority of the population within this area is …"* (then a
> demand-related question)
> *"Comparing the growth table between unit and house …"*
> *"How will the vacancy rate of offices in the CBD affect commercial spaces
> outside of the CBD?"*
> *"The RBA decided to lower/increase interest rates. What effect do you think
> this will have on …"*

### Don't

| Don't ask | Because |
|---|---|
| *"How many properties were sold in East Brisbane?"* | **You need to calculate it from the secondary data provided** |
| *"What is the density of the area?"* · *"What is the average household income?"* | **You need to find it from the source** |
| *"Do you believe a commercial building is suitable for East Brisbane?"* · *"What would you recommend we put on this block of land?"* | **You need to make the recommendation** — that is the assessment |
| *"How does housing affordability influence market decisions?"* · *"How much is a car space worth?"* · *"What would it take to kill the property boom?"* | **Too broad** |
| Anything about due dates or formatting | Not a research question |

> **The test is: could this be answered from the data you already hold?** If
> yes, it is not an interview question — it is an analysis you have not done.
> The expert exists to **interpret** and **corroborate**, not to supply facts
> you could count or judgements you are being assessed on. That distinction is
> a clean short-answer paragraph.

## 6.11 Research integrity

- **Ethical clearance** — covering the methodology, the data collection
  implementation plan (recruitment), and consent from participants including
  the **right to withdraw**
- **Consent for primary data**:
  - **Survey** — returning the questionnaire is taken to imply consent
  - **Interview / focus group** — **written consent** required

> **Different methods, different consent standards.** Returning a
> questionnaire is implied consent; an interview or focus group needs
> **written** consent. This is a one-mark MCQ and a one-line short-answer
> point, and it is also the reason the unit runs on secondary data — ethics
> clearance takes longer than a semester allows.

**Q1. Classify each on both axes: (a) a survey you run in East Brisbane; (b) the 2021 ABS Census; (c) an interview you conduct with a PRD economist; (d) a direct quote from a Colliers report.**

(a) Primary + quantitative. (b) Secondary + quantitative. (c) Primary +

**Q2. Give one advantage and one disadvantage of interviews versus surveys.**

Interviews: **+** the respondent answers in language natural to them and you
can ask follow-up questions and read tone and expression; **−** time-consuming
compared with a questionnaire. Surveys: **+** standardised responses, quick,
reaches a large group; **−** low response rates, and superficial answers if
there are too many questions.

**Q3. You want a representative sample of East Brisbane residents. Which method, and why not the other three?**

A **survey** (probability sampling). Not the ABS Census — that is
**secondary**, not a primary collection method. Not a case study — a case
study compares across cases rather than sampling within one. Not on-the-ground
observation — you cannot reach every resident, and the observer introduces
**bias**.

**Q4. Distinguish probability from non-probability sampling, and say which the Week 9 PRD session is.**

Probability: selection probability is known and usually equal, so statistical
inference about the population is possible; success rests on size and
randomness. Non-probability: selection probability is unknown, so statistical
inference is impossible; success rests on the **logic** of the sample. The PRD
information session is **purposive / judgemental**, therefore non-probability —
used to corroborate secondary findings, not to generalise.

**Q5. Which chart for each: (a) median price by year; (b) share of the development pipeline by sector; (c) price against distance from the CBD; (d) comparing five suburbs' medians?**

(a) Line graph — trend over time. (b) Pie chart or percentage component bar —
proportions. (c) Scatter plot — relationship between two variables. (d) Bar
chart — highest and lowest values.

**Q6. Name the six components every chart needs.**

Chart title; y-axis and x-axis; axis labels; plotted data; legend; reference
to the source of the data.

**Q7. Trend, cohort and panel — define each and say which one HILDA is.**

Trend: changes within a general population over time, re-sampled each wave.
Cohort: a specific subpopulation followed as it changes. Panel: **the same set
of people** observed over time. HILDA is a **panel** study.

**Q8. When is `=STDEV.P()` the right function?**

Only when your dataset **is the entire population**, not a sample of it. For
a sample — which is almost every property dataset — use `=STDEV.S()`.

## 6.13 Topic 6 Summary

- **Two independent splits.** Primary/secondary = **who collected it and
  why**. Quantitative/qualitative = **what form it takes**. Every combination
  exists.
- Quantitative needs **measurable difference between points** — discrete or
  continuous. Numerals alone do not make data quantitative.
- Know **one advantage and one disadvantage per method**: survey, interview,
  participant observation, structured observation, case study, content
  analysis.
- The lecturer's own Section B practice question is primary versus secondary
  **including the advantages and disadvantages of both** — answer all four
  parts and underline.
- **Probability** sampling: known selection probability, statistical inference
  possible, ~95% certainty convention, pilot survey necessary.
  **Non-probability**: unknown probability, no statistical inference, success
  rests on the **logic** of the sample.
- **Mean · median · mode · range · standard deviation**; `STDEV.S` for a
  sample, `STDEV.P` for a population. Property uses the **median** because
  price distributions are right-skewed.
- **Chart selection: bar/histogram = highest, lowest and distribution · line =
  trend · pie = proportion · box plot = distribution · scatter =
  relationship.** Six components on every figure, drawn with a **ruler**.
- **Cross-sectional = snapshot. Longitudinal = repeated over time**, in three
  flavours: trend, cohort, **panel** (HILDA).
- Qualitative analysis: **thematic analysis** and **coding**; content analysis
  needs **pre-constructed coding units**; grounded theory builds theory *from*
  data; ethnography sees the world from the subject's viewpoint.
- Interview questions: **open · probing · specific/closed**. Ask what the data
  cannot answer; never ask for the recommendation you are being assessed on.
- **Triangulation** = multiple methods in one study to improve validity and
  reliability.
- **Consent**: returning a questionnaire implies it; interviews and focus
  groups need it in **writing**.
