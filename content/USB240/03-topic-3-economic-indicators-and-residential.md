Week 3, delivered by the lecturer in her day job as PRD's Chief Economist.
Two halves: the macro policy machinery the exam tests as definitions, and a

## 3.1 The two policy levers

| | **Monetary policy** | **Fiscal policy** |
|---|---|---|
| Instrument | The **cash rate** | Taxes, grants, schemes, incentives, benefits |
| Who sets it | The **RBA** | **Government** — federal budget, and state/local |
| Purpose | Manage inflation and demand | Manage demand, employment, distribution |
| Property examples | Mortgage rates, borrowing capacity, developer finance cost | First Home Buyer schemes, stamp duty concessions, negative gearing, infrastructure spend |

They work **together**: in a downturn the RBA cuts the cash rate while
government raises spending or cuts tax, both stimulating demand.

### The cash rate

> The interest rate the **RBA targets for overnight lending between banks**.
> It flows through to mortgage and lending rates.

That flow-through is why it is the main lever connecting policy to property.

### Recent history — say it as a sequence

The lecturer's own summary of "the historical rates of the Australian cash
rate", which Lecture 13 lists as examinable:

```
COVID period      historical low — 0.10% (Nov 2020)
From May 2022     13 consecutive increases
~Nov 2023 → end 2024   stable / on hold
2025              three cash rate CUTS  (first Feb 2025)
2026 to date      three cash rate HIKES (one in early February 2026)
Cash rate as at March 2026: 4.10%
```

> **The direction reversed twice inside eighteen months, and answers written
> from muscle memory get it wrong.** The Week 3 charts are annotated "Cash
> Rate Historical Low Nov-20 (0.1%)", "Cash Rate Hike May-22" and "Cash Rate
> Cut Feb-25" — but by February 2026 the RBA is **hiking again**. If a
> question asks what is happening to rates *now*, the answer for Semester 1
> 2026 is **rising**, and rising in contrast to the USA, UK and Canada,
> in line with New Zealand, Japan and the Euro area.

## 3.3 Recession — two definitions

| | Definition | Test |
|---|---|---|
| **Technical recession** | **Two consecutive quarters of negative GDP growth** | Arithmetic — the national accounts |
| **Real recession** | **Widespread inability to afford basic goods and services** | Lived experience |

The lecturer's gloss: *"it doesn't matter if it's −0.8% growth and −1%
growth, it's still two minuses back to back, so it's still technically a
technical recession — but is it really?"* A real recession is when people
cannot afford potatoes and rice.

> **Small negatives still count.** An MCQ that offers "two consecutive
> quarters of *significant* negative growth" or "two consecutive quarters of
> negative growth of at least 1%" is testing exactly this. There is no
> magnitude threshold.

## 3.4 Keynesian economics

**Government action can change the level of unemployment.** The instruments:

- **Fiscal** — deficit spending: increased government expenditure, or tax cuts
- **Monetary** — changes in interest rates and money supply

The "Keynesian Revolution" was a fundamental reworking of economic theory
about the factors determining employment. In Week 2's taxonomy it is a
**growth multiplier theory** (see Note 02, §2.14).

## 3.5 The key economic indicators

| Indicator | What it tells you |
|---|---|
| **GDP and GDP growth** | Overall size and direction of the economy |
| **Inflation (CPI)** | Rising prices; drives RBA policy (target 2–3%) |
| **Unemployment rate** | Labour market health and household income security |
| **Interest rates / lending requirements** | Cost and availability of credit |
| **Consumer confidence** | Willingness to spend and to buy property |
| **Exchange rate / balance of trade** | International competitiveness and capital flows |
| **Population growth** | Underlying demand for housing |
| **Tax and tax policy** | Fiscal drag on, or stimulus to, demand |
| **Government spending / infrastructure** | Direct demand plus long-run accessibility |

### The three levels — this structure is the Section B answer

The Week 7 deck sets out what belongs at each level. Reproduce this shape and
a "what indicators would you use" question answers itself:

| Level | Indicators |
|---|---|
| **National** | GDP, cash rate, inflation, unemployment, population growth, government policy including taxation and infrastructure |
| **State** | State product (SDP/GSP), local unemployment, inflation, **interstate and overseas migration**, population growth and projections, extent of land released for development, state investments and developments, levies and incentives, labour market dynamics |
| **Suburb** | Population, income levels, attributes of the suburb (good schools, health facilities), and others |

Plus the property-specific indicators that sit across all three: **vacancy
rates, days on market, rents, yields, incentives.**

> **The lecturer told the class exactly how the short answer will be
> marked.** *"It's not actually gonna test you and be like, what is the
> Brisbane unemployment rate at the moment. So you'd be looking for Brisbane
> **specific** population growth, Brisbane **specific** unemployment rates."*
> You are marked on **naming the right indicators at the right geographic
> level**, not on reciting current values. Structure beats trivia.

> **The lecturer said GDP growth is "only about 1%" and the Colliers reading
> says 2.6%. Both are right — they are different measures.** Subtract
> population growth from headline GDP growth and you get GDP **per capita**:
>
> ```
> (1 + 0.026) / (1 + 0.016) − 1  =  0.98%  ≈  1%
> ```
>
> Headline GDP is up 2.6%; output per person is up about 1%. If a question
> asks "how is the Australian economy doing", the sophisticated answer says
> *the economy is growing but per-capita growth is close to flat — most of the
> headline growth is population.* Quote 2.6% and name it as headline.

> **Two inflation figures circulate and you should date both.** Colliers
> reports 3.7% YoY as at February 2026; the lecturer said in the Week 13
> lecture that *"inflation is at what, 4.2% as of April"*. Different months, so
> not necessarily a contradiction — the Colliers commentary explicitly warns
> that the February print "now appears dated, as rising global oil and fuel
> prices are expected to push headline inflation higher." Cite a figure **with
> its month**, and say inflation is running **above the 2–3% band**, which is
> the point either way.

## 3.7 Household finances and credit

- **Advanced economies continue to maintain healthy loans.** Australia has a
  very low non-performing loan percentage compared with other countries.
- All borrowers **in arrears by risk factor is below 1%** — extremely low.
- **2.0% of highly leveraged borrowers are at risk.**
- Savings rate recovery is under way after the 2022–23 hikes; household
  savings rose in the Sep-25 quarter and are notably higher than expected.
- Required mortgage payments **declined over 2025** on the back of cash rate
  cuts, with an increase in extra payments and offset balances.

The PRD framing is worth borrowing: *"mortgage payments are met — but at what
cost?"* Low arrears with rising offset balances is a picture of households
absorbing pain rather than defaulting.

### Home loan commitments — Australia, December 2025

```
Total commitments (ex. refinancing)   $108.3 billion   ← first time above $100bn
   Owner-occupiers                          60%
   Investors                                40%
   Of which first home buyers          $19.3 billion
   FHB lending growth, 10 yrs to Dec-25    +169.6%
```

Following the 2022–23 pullback, both segments have rebounded and are
approaching peak levels.

### Property demand — the time-to-buy index

- The **time-to-buy a dwelling index fell 6.3% in February 2026**, a result of
  the early-February cash rate increase
- The **lowest reading since December 2024**
- More than **35 points below the long-run average of 120 points**
- Linked to fading sentiment around First Home Buyer schemes, which in
  previous months had been boosted by 18–34 year olds

> **Rising FHB lending and a collapsing time-to-buy index are not a
> contradiction — they are a sequence.** Lending data is *backward*-looking
> (loans already written, to December); the sentiment index is
> *forward*-looking (intentions, in February, after a rate rise). The story is
> a strong 2025 running into a cooling early 2026. Naming that lag is a
> Section C mark.

## 3.8 Supply — Queensland and Greater Brisbane

**Queensland, Q2 2025:** a 22.5% increase in new residential building work
commenced, against a −23.0% decline in quarterly population change. New
construction is back to about **Q3 2018, pre-COVID levels**. But there is an
inherent backlog since 2021 requiring catching up, and most of the stock
planned is units and townhouses — so **an undersupply of stand-alone houses
remains**.

**Greater Brisbane:** 2021 was the pivotal turning point, when population
growth significantly outpaced supply. New supply peaked in **2024**;
population growth had already accelerated sharply from **2021**. In 2025 new
residential construction fell significantly from its 2024 high while
population growth stayed elevated. Greater Brisbane remains **structurally
undersupplied in the near term**.

Run the ratios and the story sharpens:

```
Grand total of all planned product          = 45,150
Units and apartments                        = 39,334  =  87.1%
Detached dwellings                          =  1,172  =   2.6%
Brisbane City share of units/apartments     = 36,672 / 39,334  =  93.2%
Brisbane City share of everything           =  88.0%
```

> **"Greater Brisbane has 45,150 dwellings in the pipeline" is a true
> sentence that misleads.** 87% of it is units and apartments, and only
> **2.6%** is detached housing — into a market that PRD says is short of
> **stand-alone houses**. The pipeline is not supply *for the shortage*; it is
> supply *for a different product*. Any Section C answer that recites the
> total without splitting by product type has missed the analysis.

### Median unit prices, SEQ 2025

| Market | Median | 1 yr | 5 yr |
|---|---:|---|---|
| Brisbane | **$775,000** | +15.7% (+$105,000) | +55.0% (+$275,000) |
| Gold Coast | $869,000 | −1.8% (−$16,000) | +51.1% (+$294,000) |
| Sunshine Coast | $835,000 | +9.6% (+$73,000) | +54.1% (+$293,000) |

Every one of those six percentage/dollar pairs reconciles when you compute it
against the implied prior-year base — Brisbane's 1-year, for instance:

```
Prior median  = 775,000 − 105,000 = 670,000
Growth        = 105,000 / 670,000 = 15.67%   ✓ (stated 15.7%)
```

> **Always divide the dollar change by the *prior* value, not the current
> one.** 105,000 / 775,000 = 13.5%, which is not the stated 15.7%. Percentage
> change is always measured against the **base you started from**. This is the
> single most common arithmetic slip in property reporting, and it is trivially
> checkable in an exam with a calculator.

SEQ units keep showing higher prices even with higher rates — a **spill-over**
from high demand for houses with little available stock.

## 3.10 The residential market — rents, yields and vacancy

### House rental yields, December 2025

| Market | Gross yield |
|---|---|
| Brisbane Metro | **2.9%** |
| Gold Coast Main | 3.4% |
| Sunshine Coast | 3.3% |

### Unit rental yields, December 2025

| Market | Gross yield |
|---|---|
| Brisbane Metro | **3.8%** |
| Gold Coast Main | 4.1% |
| Sunshine Coast | 4.0% |

Brisbane house yields have been relatively steady over six months with a
slight dip over twelve. **The dip is not because rents fell.** PRD's
explanation:

> The cost of maintaining an investment property has gone up — insurance
> costs climbing and higher council rates after new land valuations in
> Queensland.

Unit yields are declining because of the increasing availability of units for
rent (most new stock coming to market is units), slower population growth
than during COVID, and the same rising holding costs.

> **A falling yield can mean two completely different things and the exam
> will test which.**
>
> ```
> Yield = Net income / Value
>
> Yield ↓ because VALUE rose faster than rent      →  a strong market
> Yield ↓ because NET income fell (costs up)       →  a squeezed investor
> ```
>
> PRD is explicit that Brisbane's case is the second: gross rents held, but
> insurance and council rates ate the net. If you are handed a falling yield
> in Section C, say **which side of the fraction moved** before you interpret
> it.

### Vacancy rates, December 2025

| Market | Vacancy |
|---|---|
| Brisbane Metro | 1.2% |
| Gold Coast Main | 1.3% |
| Sunshine Coast | 1.0% |
| **East Brisbane (4169)** | **1.4%** (down from 1.6% in Dec 2024) |
| Inner Brisbane | 1.4% |
| **REIA "healthy" benchmark** | **3.0%** |

SEQ vacancy has sat between 0.5% and 1.3% and rose slightly over the past
twelve and three months as investors returned to market on the back of 2025's
lower rates.

> **3.0% is the number to memorise.** The REIA benchmark for a *balanced*
> rental market is 3.0%. Below it, tenants compete and rents rise; above it,
> landlords compete and rents fall. Every SEQ market in the table is at less
> than half the benchmark. In Section C, comparing an observed vacancy rate to
> 3.0% is a one-line way to earn a market-condition mark.

## 3.11 East Brisbane — the assessment suburb

### House and unit markets

- East Brisbane is a **blue-chip** suburb, close to the CBD and near
  Southbank, with entertainment, conferences, commercial activity, offices,
  parkland and good schools.
- The **house** market is a premium to both Brisbane LGA and Greater Brisbane,
  commanding well over $1.5M. Fluctuations over ten years are common for a
  blue-chip suburb. A slight decline in 2024, positive signs in 2025, and the
  suburb is now **at a market peak**, with very little stand-alone stock
  available.
- Much of the stock in and around East Brisbane is **units**. Before COVID it
  was a premium unit market for its views and CBD proximity. Unit prices
  suffered through the 2022 hikes and have recovered. From 2023 they became
  **on par with** Brisbane and Greater Brisbane, as unit prices elsewhere rose
  on the back of a lack of houses. East Brisbane units mean **affordability**:
  under $1.0M, almost half the price of houses.

Verified from the raw PRD comparison workbook (see Note 08 for the full
series):

```
East Brisbane median, 2025:   House $1,610,000   Unit $777,500
Unit as % of house            777,500 / 1,610,000  =  48.3%   ← "almost half" ✓
2025 growth                   House +11.71%   Unit +11.07%    ← "at least 11%" ✓
```

### East Brisbane sales and rental market, Q4 2025

| Metric | House | Unit |
|---|---|---|
| Average vendor discount | **+13.3% premium** | **+3.7% premium** |
| Average days on market | 48 days | 33 days |
| Number of rental listings | 29 | 78 |
| Median weekly rent | **$898** | **$650** |
| Average days to let | 25 days | 22 days |

A year earlier, in Q4 2024, the house market was still at a **discount**
(−7.3%) — so the swing to a 13.3% premium in twelve months is the headline
change. Both markets now favour vendors: buyers must offer **above** the
initial listing price.

> **"Vendor discount" that is positive is a premium, and the sign flip is the
> whole story.** A vendor discount measures the change from listing price to
> sale price. Negative = the vendor discounted to sell (a buyer's market).
> Positive = the property sold above listing (a seller's market). East
> Brisbane houses went from −7.3% to +13.3% in four quarters, a **20.6
> percentage point** swing. Reading "+13.3% discount" as a discount inverts the
> conclusion.

Every one of those figures was recomputed from the raw rental spreadsheet.
The median house rent of $898 only reproduces once one clearly erroneous
listing is excluded — see Note 08, §8.5, for the working.

<summary>✅ Checkpoint 1 — policy and indicators</summary>

**Q1. Distinguish monetary from fiscal policy, with two examples of each, and say who sets each.**

Monetary policy is the **cash rate**, set by the **RBA**, used to manage
inflation and demand — examples: raising the cash rate to slow demand, cutting
it to stimulate borrowing. Fiscal policy is taxes, grants, schemes,
incentives and benefits, set by **government** through the budget (federal,
and also state and local) — examples: a First Home Buyer grant, a stamp duty
concession, infrastructure spending, a tax cut. They interact: in a downturn
the RBA cuts while government spends.

**Q2. The economy records GDP growth of −0.3% then −0.1%. Is Australia in recession?**

In a **technical** recession, yes: two consecutive quarters of negative GDP
growth, and there is no magnitude threshold. Whether it is a **real**
recession — widespread inability to afford basic goods and services — is a
separate question the national accounts do not answer.

**Q3. Headline GDP is +2.6% and population growth is +1.6%. What is per-capita growth, and why does it matter for property?**

```
(1.026 / 1.016) − 1 = 0.98%  ≈ 1%
```

Almost all the headline growth is more people, not more output per person.
For property that is a demand signal without an income signal: **more
households needing dwellings, but no real improvement in capacity to pay** —
which pushes toward smaller, cheaper product and toward renting.

**Q4. A market's gross yield falls from 3.4% to 2.9%. Give the two explanations and say how you would tell them apart.**

Either value rose faster than income (a strengthening market), or net income
fell while values held (rising holding costs squeezing the investor). Compare
the movement in median price and median rent directly: if both rose but price
rose faster, it is the first; if rents held and outgoings — insurance, council
rates after new land valuations — rose, it is the second. PRD attributes
Brisbane's fall to the second.

**Q5. State the REIA healthy vacancy benchmark and interpret East Brisbane's 1.4%.**

3.0%. East Brisbane at 1.4% is **less than half** the balanced level: an
extremely tight rental market, quick occupancy, upward pressure on rents, low
letting-up risk for a new development — and a poor outlook for tenants.
