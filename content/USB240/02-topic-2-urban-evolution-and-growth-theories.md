Week 2, plus the Week 3 and Week 4 tutorials. How Australian cities got their

## 2.1 Urban evolution in Australia

Australian urban evolution starts at European settlement, and the dates are
MCQ-friendly:

| Year | Event |
|---|---|
| **1788** | 26 January — arrival of Europeans (First Fleet, Botany Bay) |
| **1799** | Matthew Flinders arrives in Moreton Bay |
| **1824** | First convict settlement at Redcliffe |
| **1825** | Convicts moved to Brisbane |
| **1842** | Free settlement begins |
| **1859** | Queensland separates from NSW as a self-governing colony |

The **Turrbal** people are the original inhabitants of Brisbane (Meeanjin) —
direct descendants of the original owners and custodians. The Week 2 deck
opens on this before it opens on European settlement, and Lecture 13 flags
"Indigenous and non-Indigenous perspectives" as examinable (see Note 07).

**Ports and harbours drove growth.** Cities needed materials brought by ship,
so the port *is* the original economic base. Brisbane's Customs House is the
lecture's example.

## 2.2 What made Australian cities different

Five points, and the last one is the quotable statistic:

- Covered a **much greater area** with a relatively small "walking city"
  heritage
- Most growth occurred during the **public transport era**
- **Land was abundant and cheap**
- Australia had a **commercial rather than industrial** class structure with
  relatively high average incomes
- Home ownership was therefore a real option: at the turn of the 20th century
  **50% of Australians were owner-occupiers, compared with 10% in Britain**

Australian cities were *"characteristically commercial or mercantile, centres
of trade, rather than industrial."*

> **This is the answer to "explain European settlement trends/patterns in
> Australia", which Lecture 13 lists first among the examinable concepts.**
> The chain is: coastal port sites → mercantile rather than industrial
> economies → cheap abundant land + public-transport-era growth → low
> densities and very high owner-occupation. Europe got the opposite of all
> four.

## 2.3 Metropolitan primacy

> **Metropolitan primacy** — the phenomenon of excessive concentration of
> population and development in the main city of a country or region, often
> to the detriment of others.

## 2.4 Cumulative causation

Popularised by the Swedish economist **Gunnar Myrdal** in the 1940s and 1950s.
The Oxford Dictionary of Geography definition used in the deck:

> "Cumulative causation is the unfolding of events connected with a change in
> the economy, as a consequence of the **multiplier effect**. Cumulative
> causation can be set in motion wherever the expansion of a cluster, through
> attraction of firms with complementary competencies, adds to its initial
> attractiveness."

The lecturer's plain version: *"you've got an industry, it creates more jobs,
creates more population, creates more demand, creates investment, etc."* —
**growth stimulates growth**, the snowball effect.

```
Industry → jobs → population → demand for goods & services
        → investment → more industry → (repeat)
```

> **It is not limitless, and this is the half students forget.** The
> definition continues: *"cumulative causation is not limitless and can be
> **reversed** through negative externalities, such as congestion costs."*
> A short answer that only describes the snowball rolling forward is
> incomplete. Congestion, unaffordable housing and infrastructure strain are
> the brakes.

**Urbanisation**, the third of the trio, is migration from rural to urban
areas for **employment**, plus immigration. Population growth stimulates urban
growth through natural increase (post-war), urbanisation and immigration.

## 2.6 The three families of theory

This table is the highest-value thing in Week 2, because the Canvas quiz
tested the *distinction between families*, not the theory names.

| Family | Theories | Answers the question |
|---|---|---|
| **Location theory** | von Thünen (1826) | **WHERE** different land uses sit, and what rent they pay |
| **Patterns of growth** | Concentric zone (Burgess 1925) · Axial/radial development · Sector/wedge (Hoyt 1939) · Multiple nuclei (Harris & Ullman 1945) | The **FORM** growth takes across the city |
| **Growth multiplier** | Economic base · Keynesian model · Urban hierarchy / central place (Christaller 1933) | The **REASONS / WHY** a city grows |

> **The Keynesian model is a GROWTH MULTIPLIER theory, not a pattern
> theory.** Cal missed exactly this in the Canvas urban-growth quiz. Pattern
> theories draw maps; multiplier theories explain mechanisms. Keynes explains
> a mechanism (government spending changes employment), so he sits with
> economic base and central place.

## 2.7 Location theory — von Thünen (1826)

**Johann Heinrich von Thünen**, German economist. The **oldest land-use
model**, connecting spatial economics with the theory of rent.

The model is built on **urban transport costs for agricultural produce from a
central market**, substituting *general accessibility* for transport costs.
It produces a pattern of **concentric zones**, each specialising in a
particular agricultural product.

> "Humans will use land in relation to the **cost of land** and the **cost of
> transporting products to the market**."

### The seven assumptions

1. A city is centrally located in an "isolated state" — **one city = one
   market**
2. **Maximum profit** — decisions are made to maximise economic benefit
3. The surrounding area is **wilderness**
4. Land is generally **flat**
5. **Soil quality and climate are consistent** — equally farmable, equally
   productive
6. Farmers transport goods to market mainly by cart — **transport costs are
   fixed**
7. Farmers **behave rationally** in choosing where to conduct their activities

The Canvas quiz phrased the assumptions more generally as: markets are
**perfectly competitive**; decisions **optimise utility or economic benefit**;
and **constraining influences** (land-use controls, government intervention)
are **ignored**.

### The four rings

From the town outwards:

```
1.  Intense farming        ← closest, highest rent, most intensive
2.  Forest lands
3.  Extensive farming
4.  Grazing                ← furthest, lowest rent, least intensive
```

Rent for land is **high near the city** and falls with distance.

### Von Thünen revisited for the modern city

Collapse the model to two urban users:

- Just two land users — **residential and commercial**
- The optimal location is **the CBD**
- **Commercial users can outbid residential users**
- Homogeneous topography and transport routes throughout

```
Price of land
    ▲  ╲
    │   ╲  CBD  — commercial wins the bid
    │    ╲
    │     ╲   Transition
    │      ╲___
    │           ╲______  Value & density diminish
    └──────────────────────► Distance from CBD
```

This is the **forerunner of the land-use / density / value concept** and the
foundation of **bid-rent theory** — why land near the CBD is more valuable.

> **Say the words "bid rent" and "ability to pay" in any von Thünen
> answer.** The examinable content is not the four agricultural rings — it is
> that *demand for land in a free market creates a hierarchy of uses based on
> ability to pay*, and that the hierarchy is set by **transport cost and
> distance to market**. The rings are the illustration; the bid-rent gradient
> is the theory.

### Worked example — Everton Park against von Thünen

The Week 4 tutorial plots median house price against distance from the
Brisbane CBD for the suburbs 3–10 km north:

| Suburb | ~km from CBD | Median house price |
|---|---:|---:|
| Newmarket | ~4 | ~$1,600,000 |
| Grange | ~5 | ~$1,600,000 |
| Wilston | ~4.5 | ~$1,450,000 |
| Wooloowin | ~6 | ~$1,420,000 |
| Alderley | ~6.5 | ~$1,270,000 |
| Enoggera | ~7 | ~$1,270,000 |
| Everton Park | **~8** | **~$1,000,000** |
| Chermside | ~9 | ~$920,000 |
| Keperra | ~9.5 | ~$880,000 |

*(Source: RP Data 2024, read off the tutorial's scatter plot — treat the
figures as approximate.)*

The gradient is real: price falls as distance rises, exactly as von Thünen
predicts. But Everton Park sits **below** the trend for its distance, and von
Thünen cannot explain why, because assumption 4 (flat land) and assumption 6
(fixed transport costs) have quietly done the work of assuming the problem
away.

## 2.8 Axial / radial development theory

Developed in the **1930s** as a variation on the concentric model; the deck
attributes it to **Sir Peter Hall**.

> Land use, land values and real estate development **radiate outward along
> axes driven by transportation routes**, changing from the city's centre.

Roads, railways, even rivers emanating from the CBD. Commercial and
residential development begins next to those transport lines as it moves out,
forming vibrant commercial, residential and industrial hubs along the axes.

The Brisbane version the deck offers is **knowledge corridors** (City of
Brisbane / The Demographics Group).

### Worked example — Everton Park against the axial model

Re-plot the same suburbs against **minutes** from the CBD instead of
kilometres, and Everton Park stops being an anomaly: it is ~8 km out but at
the far right of the time axis (~28 minutes), because it has **poor transport
infrastructure**. The tutorial's own caption: *"About 8 km from the Brisbane
CBD but poor transport infrastructure."*

Brisbane City Council has identified the gap and is investigating an
underground rail station at Everton Park.

> **This is a genuinely good exam answer, so learn the shape of it.** Under
> the **axial** model, adding a station raises accessibility, pulls the suburb
> *up* the price-vs-time curve, and should lift values toward its
> distance-based peers. Under **von Thünen**, nothing changes at all —
> transport cost is an *assumption* of the model, not a variable in it. Two
> theories, one intervention, opposite predictions. If you are asked to
> compare theories, compare what each one lets you *change*.

## 2.9 Sector / wedge theory — Hoyt (1939)

Proposed by land economist **Homer Hoyt**, 1939. *A city develops in
**sectors**, not rings.*

- Describes the growth of US cities based on economic and physical geography
- Certain areas are more attractive for different activities because of an
  environmental factor or **mere chance**
- Most cities evolved around the **nexus of several important transport
  facilities** — roads, railroads, sea ports, trolley lines — emanating from
  the centre
- The model connects a **CBD** to a **factories/industrial** sector, a
  **low-class (working class) residential** sector and a **middle-class
  residential** sector

Each sector expands **outwards** with population growth, and high-quality
housing extends along the fastest travel routes while low-income groups
locate on the opposite side of the CBD, often near industrial land.

> **Its strength and its weakness are the same thing: it concentrates on
> housing.** That is the phrase the tutorial matrix uses, and it is the mark
> in a "critically evaluate the sector model" question.

## 2.10 Multiple nuclei theory — Harris & Ullman (1945)

Proposed in **1945** by **Chauncy Harris and Edward Ullman**, based on
**Chicago**.

- A city begins with a CBD, then **other smaller CBDs develop on the
  outskirts** near more valuable residential areas, allowing shorter travel
- This creates **several nuclei** — hence the name
- Large cities have a structure that is essentially **cellular**
- Put forward as an **alternative to the concentric model**, to capture the
  complex nature of urban space and its activities

### Worked example — Everton Park as a nucleus forming in real time

The Week 4 tutorial's before-and-after is the cleanest illustration in the
unit:

| Everton Park 2017 | Everton Park 2024+ |
|---|---|
| Old stores, high vacancy | New restaurant precinct (delivered) |
| Empty "Masters" store | Bulky goods precinct renovated — Rebel, BCF, other tenants |
| Recently demolished industrial buildings | Medical, childcare and new townhouse development |
| Traffic choke point | New road completed late 2021 |
| Old farm land, flood prone | Mirvac "Ashford Residences" |

A suburb 8 km out acquires retail, food, medical, childcare and higher-density
housing — it stops being a dormitory and becomes a **secondary nucleus**.

## 2.11 Concentric zone theory — Burgess (1925)

**Ernest Burgess**, 1925. One of the earliest theoretical models of urban
social structure, explaining the distribution of social groups within urban
areas. Explicitly *"an urban version of von Thünen's regional land use model
developed a century earlier."*

The circles are based on **the amount people will pay for the land**, which
is based on the **profits obtainable from a business on that land**. The
centre has the most customers, so it is profitable for retail; manufacturing
pays slightly less because it only needs accessibility for workers, goods in
and goods out; residential takes the surrounding land.

The rings, from the centre:

| Ring | Content |
|---|---|
| 1. **CBD** | City functions — administration, retail, commercial, social |
| 2. **Transition zone** | Original rural fringe; mixed use; waiting for or in partial redevelopment / re-use |
| 3. **Low income housing** | Fair but crowded conditions; **good access to CBD employment** |
| 4. **Better housing** | Newer and larger homes |
| 5. **Commuter zone** | Rural/urban fringe; high quality housing; high incomes |

> **In Burgess, LOW income lives closest to the CBD — and it is measured in
> DISTANCE, not time.** Cal missed this in the Canvas quiz. The model was
> built on 1920s Chicago, where the poor lived near the factories and the rich
> commuted out. Australian cities largely inverted that, which is the model's
> first criticism.

### Criticisms

- Describes the **peculiar American geography**, where the inner city is poor
  and the suburbs wealthy — **the inverse is the norm elsewhere**
- Assumes an **isotropic plane** — an even, unchanging landscape. Physical
  features restrict growth; hills and water make some locations unusually
  desirable
- **Decentralisation** of shops, manufacturing and entertainment
- **Urban regeneration** — more expensive property is now found in formerly
  "low class" areas
- Many new housing estates were built on the **edges** of cities in Australia
  and Britain

## 2.13 Growth multiplier theory 1 — economic base

The size of the urban area depends on the amount of goods and services
supplied to **outsiders ("exports")**.

```
Basic activities      = industries producing goods and services for OUTSIDE demand
Non-basic activities  = goods and services produced for the area's own inhabitants
```

The mechanism:

```
Demand for basic activities ↑
  → demand for labour ↑
  → new labour attracted to the urban area
  → income level rises
  → growth of NON-BASIC activities + higher import expenditure
  → total employment ↑
```

> **The ratio of total employment to total population gives the size of the
> urban area.** The Canvas quiz asked which theory uses the
> employment-to-population ratio, and the named answer is **economic base
> theory** — not "the multiplier theory". Be precise with the name.

### Worked example — sizing a town from its base

From the quiz-derived material:

```
Population                             = 30,000
Total employment : population  = 1 : 3 → employed        = 30,000 / 3 = 10,000
Basic : non-basic              = 1 : 1 → basic employed  = 10,000 / 2 =  5,000
                                       → non-basic       =              5,000

Basic multiplier = total employment / basic employment = 10,000 / 5,000 = 2.0
```

So every job in the export base supports **one further local job**. Add 500
basic jobs and the model predicts 1,000 total jobs and, at the 1:3 ratio,
**3,000 more people**.

```
+500 basic  ×  multiplier 2.0  =  +1,000 total jobs
+1,000 jobs ×  3 (pop:emp)     =  +3,000 population
```

### Issues with the model

- Does not explain the **existence or expansion** of basic industries
- Largely ignores the **level of income** — assumed to depend solely on
  demand for exports, treating domestic trade as only a "service", not a
  "nation-building" industry
- It is **impossible to divide industries into black-and-white categories** —
  the real world is grey; some service industries export part of their output
- The **relationship between income and import spending** is not taken into
  account: if all export earnings were spent on imports there would be no
  extra demand for non-basic goods
- Rests on assumptions about the basic : non-basic relationship

## 2.14 Growth multiplier theory 2 — the Keynesian model

The "Keynesian Revolution" was a fundamental reworking of economic theory
concerning the factors determining **employment**.

The central policy change: **government action can change the level of
unemployment**, using

- **Fiscal policy** — deficit spending, increased government expenditure, tax
  cuts
- **Monetary policy** — changes in interest rates and money supply

That is the whole content of the theory as far as this unit tests it, and it
links straight into Note 03.

## 2.15 Growth multiplier theory 3 — central place theory

**Walter Christaller, 1933**, German geographer.

The theory explains the **number, size and range of market services** in
commercial or human settlements, and the **spatial distribution of cities
across the landscape**. Settlements function as **"central places"** providing
economic services to surrounding areas.

The mechanism is **threshold** and **range**:

```
Everyday, low-value needs (bread from a bakery)
    → people will not travel far
    → many small settlements, located close together, for efficiency

Expensive, infrequent, specialised wants
    → people will travel
    → fewer, larger settlements, located further apart
```

Different thresholds produce a **hierarchy of centres, including a shopping
hierarchy**.

> **Central place theory is the intellectual ancestor of the PCA shopping
> centre classification in Note 05.** Neighbourhood centre (one supermarket,
> ~10–35 specialty stores, small catchment) through to super regional
> (two department stores, ~250 specialty stores, regional catchment) is
> Christaller's threshold-and-range hierarchy with square-metre thresholds
> bolted on. Saying so in a retail question earns a mark for linking theory to
> practice.

## 2.16 The four forms of urban growth

1. **Urban renewal** — higher buildings
2. **Inter-urban competition** — movement between cities as some grow (e.g.
   greater Brisbane) and some decline
3. **Sharp distinction between town and country**
4. **Evolution of a hierarchy of urban centres** — regional, national,
   international

## 2.17 The comment that earns critical-evaluation marks

The deck's own summary of location and pattern-of-growth theories:

- They should be viewed as **mutually INCLUSIVE, not exclusive**
- Most are **descriptive rather than analytical** — they are therefore **not
  predictive models**
- They **use different theories to describe the same development**
- Most are **overly simplistic**
- They **ignore the public sector** and other non-market land-use decisions

> **"Mutually inclusive" is the marks-winning phrase, and Everton Park proves
> it.** The same suburb is simultaneously a point on a von Thünen bid-rent
> gradient, a laggard on an axial corridor awaiting a station, and an emerging
> multiple-nuclei centre. The lecturer was visibly pleased when students
> applied theories to East Brisbane in Assessment 2 — *"is Brisbane like a
> nuclei, or like a centric map"* — so a Section C answer that names two or
> three theories and says what each explains and what each misses will
> outscore one that picks a single winner.

## 2.18 Disasters, pandemics and the urban footprint

Climate change (drought, bushfires, floods) and COVID-19 both reshape cities:
working from home, and online shopping.

The deck's flood evidence covers 22 Brisbane suburbs — **including East
Brisbane** — comparing mean and average prices for flood-affected and
non-affected properties across 2008–2010, 2011–2013, 2014–2016 and 2017–2019.
Prices in *affected* areas kept rising.

Potential reasons offered:

- Buyers **discount the possibility of another flood** — 2011 was called a
  "once in a lifetime flood"
- **Non-disclosure of damage** by sellers
- Houses have **adapted** to future floods
- Buyers are **aware of the issues but the utility is greater than the
  immediate risk**

> **This directly contradicts the intuitive answer, so it is a good MCQ
> stem.** "Flood-affected suburbs saw prices fall relative to non-affected
> suburbs" is *false* in the unit's own data. If East Brisbane's flood overlay
> comes up in Section C, the defensible line is: flood risk affects insurance
> cost, finance and development feasibility more reliably than it affects
> observed sale prices — and buyers systematically under-price low-frequency,
> high-severity risk.

**Q1. State von Thünen in three sentences, and say what sets land use and rent.**

Von Thünen (1826) models concentric rings of land use around a single central
market in an isolated state. Land use, intensity and rent are determined by
**transport cost and distance to the market**, so land closer in pays higher
rent and is used more intensively. It is the foundation of bid-rent theory,
which explains why land near the CBD is more valuable — a hierarchy of uses
based on ability to pay.

**Q2. Match each theory to author and year.**

Concentric zone — Burgess, 1925. Sector/wedge — Hoyt, 1939. Multiple nuclei —
Harris & Ullman, 1945 (Chicago). Central place — Christaller, 1933. Location
theory — von Thünen, 1826. Axial/radial — 1930s, attributed in the deck to
Sir Peter Hall.

**Q3. Which family does the Keynesian model belong to, and how would you tell?**

Growth multiplier. Pattern theories describe the *form* growth takes across
the city (a map); multiplier theories explain *why* growth happens (a
mechanism). Keynes describes a mechanism — government action changing
employment — so he is a multiplier theory.

**Q4. In the Burgess model, who lives closest to the CBD, and is the model built on distance or time?**

Low-income housing sits closest (after the transition zone), in "fair but
crowded conditions" with good access to CBD employment. The model uses
**distance**, not journey time.

**Q5. Which pattern theory accommodates topography and transport infrastructure?**

Axial/radial development — it accommodates topography, transport
infrastructure and special accessibility, and uses journey time, distance and
cost. Multiple nuclei *can* accommodate topography and transport. Concentric
and sector ignore topography.

**Q6. A town of 45,000 has a total-employment-to-population ratio of 1:3 and a basic-to-non-basic split of 2:3. How many basic jobs are there, and what is the basic multiplier?**

```
Employment      = 45,000 / 3          = 15,000
Basic share     = 2 / (2+3) = 0.4     → basic = 15,000 × 0.4 = 6,000
Non-basic                              = 9,000
Basic multiplier = 15,000 / 6,000     = 2.5
```

Every export-base job supports 1.5 further local jobs.

**Q7. Give the two reversals that most often appear as MCQ distractors.**

(1) Burgess places *low* income near the CBD, not high, and uses distance not
time. (2) Axial/radial *accommodates* topography and infrastructure rather
than ignoring them.

## 2.19 Topic 2 Summary

- Australian urban evolution begins with European settlement (1788 Sydney;
  Brisbane 1799 → 1824 Redcliffe → 1825 Brisbane → 1842 free → 1859 Qld
  separates), on **port and harbour** sites.
- Australian cities were **commercial/mercantile, not industrial**, with cheap
  abundant land and public-transport-era growth, producing **50%
  owner-occupation by 1900** against Britain's 10%.
- **Metropolitan primacy** — capitals dominate to the detriment of other
  centres. **Cumulative causation** — growth stimulates growth, but is
  reversible through congestion and other negative externalities.
  **Urbanisation** — rural-to-urban migration for employment.
- **Three families**: location (von Thünen — *where*), patterns of growth
  (concentric, axial, sector, multiple nuclei — *the form*), growth multiplier
  (economic base, Keynesian, central place — *the why*).
- **von Thünen 1826** is the flagged must-know: concentric rings around one
  central market, rent set by transport cost and distance, the origin of
  **bid rent** and of the hierarchy of uses by **ability to pay**.
- **Burgess 1925** — rings, distance not time, low income near the CBD.
  **Axial** — along transport routes, accommodates topography and
  infrastructure, uses time as well as distance. **Hoyt 1939** — sectors, and
  its housing focus is its weakness. **Harris & Ullman 1945** — cellular,
  multiple nuclei beyond the CBD.
- **Economic base** uses the **employment-to-population ratio** and splits
  employment into **basic (export)** and **non-basic (local)**.
- All these models are **descriptive, not predictive**, **overly simplistic**,
  ignore the **public sector**, and are **mutually inclusive** — use two or
  three together.
