## 1.2 The law of demand

```
Ceteris paribus (all else being equal):

  P ↓  →  Qd ↑
  P ↑  →  Qd ↓
```

The ceteris paribus condition is not decoration. It is the reason the demand
curve can be drawn in two dimensions at all: you are holding every other
variable constant so you can look at **one relationship at a time**.

The lecture's demand schedule (tablets, Hubbard et al.):

| Price ($) | Quantity demanded (m/month) |
|---|---|
| 700 | 3 |
| 600 | 4 |
| 500 | 5 |
| 400 | 6 |
| 300 | 7 |

### The distinction that gets tested

| | What happens | What it is called |
|---|---|---|
| **Price** changes | Movement **along** the curve | A change in **quantity demanded** |
| Any **non-price** variable changes | The whole curve **shifts** | A change in **demand** |

> **"Demand rose because prices fell" is wrong, and it is the single most
> common first-year error.** If price fell, *quantity demanded* rose — you
> moved along the curve. Demand only "rises" when there is **more Qd at every
> price**, which requires a non-price driver: income, migration, credit,
> expectations. Get this backwards in an MCQ and you lose the mark; get it
> backwards in Section C and you have mis-diagnosed the market.

### What shifts demand

The general list (Hubbard et al.): **income · prices of related goods ·
tastes · population and demographics · expected future prices.**

The real-estate specific list (Pirounakis 2013; Mourouzi-Sivitanidou 2021),
which is the one to quote in a property exam:

- **Market size**
- **Income / wealth**
- **Price of substitutes**
- **Expectations**
- **Interest rates and availability of credit**

Increase in demand → curve shifts **right**. Decrease → **left**.

## 1.3 The law of supply

```
Ceteris paribus:

  P ↑  →  Qs ↑
  P ↓  →  Qs ↓
```

A firm deciding how much to supply thinks about: the price of the good, what
it costs to produce, and what else it could be producing instead.

### What shifts supply

**Prices of inputs · technological change · prices of substitutes in
production · number of firms in the market · expected future prices.**

The lecture's worked illustration:

```
Input cost ↑ → cost of production ↑ → firm supplies LESS at every price
                                    → supply curve shifts LEFT / UP

Input cost ↓ → cost of production ↓ → firm supplies MORE at every price
                                    → supply curve shifts RIGHT / DOWN
```

In 2026 Brisbane terms the input-cost shifter is doing all the work:
construction cost inflation and labour shortages (amplified by the Olympic
build) have pushed the construction schedule left, which is why the Colliers
Q1 2026 report says the Brisbane Metro office development pipeline has
*"effectively come to a halt"* and describes *"a gap in supply not seen in
at least the past 20 years."*

## 1.4 What determines new construction

Straight from the Week 1 deck, quoting Mourouzi-Sivitanidou (2021, pp. 31–32):

> "The major motivation for private real estate development is to make money
> or profit. Thus the major determinants of new construction in a market are
> factors that determine a project's profitability and the uncertainty
> associated with it. Therefore, more profit with little uncertainty, the
> more new construction."

Three factors:

1. **Availability and factors of production** — land, labour, materials,
   capital
2. **Expectations** regarding future real estate demand and prices
3. **Perceived market risk**

> **Profit alone does not trigger construction — profit *and low
> uncertainty* does.** That is why a market can show rising rents, falling
> vacancy and no new building at the same time, which is precisely the
> Brisbane fringe office story in Note 05. If an exam question asks why
> supply is not responding to obvious demand, "perceived market risk" and
> "expectations" are the two marks most students miss.

## 1.5 Long-run versus short-run supply

The lecture contrasts two supply curves:

| | Shape | Why |
|---|---|---|
| **(a) Long run** | Flat / elastic | Supply is **not restricted** by time, financial or topographical constraints |
| **(b) Short run** | Steep / inelastic | Time to build, finance limits and physical constraints all bind |

Now shift demand right against each:

```
Against the FLAT (long-run) curve:
    price rises a little,  quantity rises a lot

Against the STEEP (short-run) curve:
    price rises A LOT,     quantity rises a little
```

> **This one diagram explains most of Australian housing.** Population growth
> (a demand shifter) hits a short-run supply curve that cannot move, so the
> entire adjustment comes out in price. It is also why "just build more
> houses" is a *long-run* answer to a *short-run* problem — and why the Week 3
> data shows Greater Brisbane structurally undersupplied since 2021 with
> prices still climbing.

## 1.6 Price determination

```
Market price P* is set where the demand and supply curves intersect.

If P1 < P*   →   Qd > Qs   →   EXCESS DEMAND   →   price is bid UP toward P*
If P2 > P*   →   Qs > Qd   →   EXCESS SUPPLY   →   price falls back toward P*
```

At P* nobody is motivated to move: Qd = Qs.

### Worked example — the Brisbane light-industrial land release

The Week 1 in-class activity: *Brisbane City Council has unlocked more than
**70 ha** of "tired old (light industrial and) commercial areas" and will
rezone them to allow mixed-use development (converted to residential).*

Work it in two steps, because the answer is not "supply".

**Step 1 — the direct effect (supply side).**

```
Rezoning adds developable residential land
  → residential supply curve shifts RIGHT  (S1 → S2)
  → at the old demand curve D1, price falls  P1 → P2, quantity rises Q1 → Q2
```

**Step 2 — the indirect effect (demand side).** The lecture's second diagram
adds a demand shift:

```
New residential amenity + inner-city location attracts buyers
  → demand shifts RIGHT  (D1 → D2)
  → price recovers part-way,  P2 → P3, quantity rises further
```

**And the other market.** Removing 70 ha of light-industrial land *reduces*
industrial supply. Same council decision, opposite direction, different
market.

> **The trap in this question is the word "only".** The tutorial asks: *"Will
> it impact industrial/commercial or only residential property or both?"* The
> answer is **both, in opposite directions** — residential supply up,
> industrial supply down — and it is a **supply-side intervention that
> triggers a demand-side response**. Answering "it's a supply issue" alone
> gets half the marks.

## 1.7 Property cycles

The Week 1 deck's cycle diagram runs A → B → C → D → E with two axes, price
and time, and three bullets:

- Property is a **long-term investment**
- The best time to buy is … the best time to sell is …
- **DO NOT BE A FOLLOWER**

The lecturer expanded on this in the lecture itself. Her position, in
substance: knowing where you sit in the cycle is necessary but not
sufficient — *"the best time to buy real estate always depends on your own
financial ability… if you push yourself too far and you overcapitalise and
you are in so much debt that you can't service your mortgage, then you're
going to be in trouble."* Property is a 5–7, sometimes 10–15 year hold, and
you will ride the cycle up and down inside that hold.

### Do the sector cycles align?

Lecture 13's revision checklist asks *"Do cycles for different sectors and
investment types align?"* The lecturer answered it out loud: **yes** —
broadly they align with the wider economic cycle (expansion → peak →
contraction → trough).

> **"Broadly aligned" is not "identical", and Section C rewards the
> difference.** In 2026 Brisbane, residential is running hot (prices +11–12%)
> while metro office has just posted its **first negative net absorption in
> nearly five years**. Same economy, same city, opposite sector cycles. Say
> "the cycles broadly align with the economic cycle but lead and lag each
> other by sector" and you have both marks.

## 1.8 Why the property market is less efficient than other markets

Flagged in Lecture 13's checklist as an examinable question. Four reasons:

| Reason | Consequence |
|---|---|
| **Illiquid** | You cannot sell a building this afternoon; exit takes months |
| **Slow to transact** | Contract, finance, settlement — weeks to months per trade |
| **Imperfect information** | No central price screen; every asset is unique; sale prices lag |
| **Planning constraints** | Zoning, overlays and approvals decide what can exist at all |

Add the **supply lag**: an approval granted at the top of a cycle completes
into a different market.

## 1.9 Economic, physical and legal characteristics of property

Three separate lists that MCQ writers love to swap. This distinction came up
in the Canvas economics quiz.

| Characteristic set | Members |
|---|---|
| **Economic** (Whipple 2006) | Immobility · large economic units · durability · scarcity · land is unproductive by itself |
| **Physical** | Shape · size · topography · views · improvements |
| **Legal** | Tenure · restrictions imposed at law |

> **"Immobility" is economic, not physical — and that is the trap.** It reads
> like a physical fact, but the reason it is classed economic is that it is
> what makes property markets *local*: you cannot arbitrage a Brisbane
> shortage with Perth stock. Likewise "durability" and "scarcity" sound
> physical and are economic. If an MCQ offers you "topography" among a list of
> *economic* characteristics, that is the distractor.

### Redevelopment

```
Redevelop when:   value of the CLEARED SITE  >  value of the property in its EXISTING USE
```

That is the definition, and the Canvas quiz distractor was "value uplift".
Value uplift is a consequence, not the test. The test is a comparison of two
values.

Applied to the assessment site: 822 m² of office/warehouse on 1,395 m² in
inner-city East Brisbane. If a high-density residential site value exceeds
the value of the shed as a shed, you redevelop — and the whole Assessment 2
report is an argument about which use maximises the cleared-site value.

## 1.10 Intensity of land use and the hierarchy of uses

The Week 1 and Week 2 decks share this diagram, attributed back to von
Thünen (see Note 02):

```
Price per m²
    ▲
    │        Retail / commercial          ← highest bid, most intensive use
    │
    │   Industrial / residential
    │
    │  Agricultural                        ← lowest bid, least intensive use
    └────────────────────────────► Intensity of use
```

> "Demand for land in a free market economy creates a **hierarchy of uses
> based on ability to pay**."

Two urban users, one optimal location (the CBD): **commercial users can
outbid residential users**, so commercial takes the centre and residential is
pushed out. Value and density both diminish with distance.

## 1.11 The indicator list

The Week 1 deck's full list of economic, demographic and other indicators
for market analysis — worth being able to reproduce roughly, because a
Section B question that asks "what indicators would you use" is answered
from here:

- GDP and GDP growth
- Inflation rates
- Employment figures; employment **types**
- Income / wealth
- Interest rates and lending requirements
- Consumer confidence
- International factors — exchange rate, balance of trade
- Tax and tax policy
- Population growth and other demographic trends
- Urbanisation, land use patterns (externalities) and accessibility
- Building codes and regulation
- Future predictions for the housing market
- Perceived market risk
- Other events, such as incidents of natural hazards

> **"Employment types" is a separate line from "employment figures", and it
> is the property-specific one.** White-collar employment drives *office*
> demand; transport, postal and warehousing employment drives *industrial*
> demand. A falling unemployment rate tells you nothing about which sector
> benefits — the composition does. Note 05 uses exactly this to explain the
> Brisbane office market.

<summary>✅ Checkpoint 1 — demand, supply and construction</summary>

**Q1. Interest rates fall. Draw the effect on the residential market and name the mechanism.**

Falling rates increase borrowing capacity and credit availability — a
**non-price demand shifter**, so the demand curve shifts **right** (D1 → D2).
Against a short-run inelastic supply curve, most of the adjustment comes out
in **price** rather than quantity. This is the Week 3 story: 2025 rate cuts,
sharp house price growth, dwelling investment slow to respond.

**Q2. Construction costs rise 15%. Which curve moves, which way, and what happens to P and Q?**

An input-price shifter, so **supply** shifts **left/up** (S → S2). Less real
estate is supplied at the same price level. Equilibrium price rises,
equilibrium quantity falls. The Colliers Q1 2026 language for this is "the
continued widening gap between economic and market rents… continues to deter
new office development activity."

**Q3. A market has rising rents, falling vacancy and no new construction. Reconcile that with the theory.**

Profitability alone does not trigger construction — the determinants are
availability of factors of production, **expectations** about future demand
and prices, and **perceived market risk**. High construction and labour
costs plus an uncertain outlook mean expected profit does not clear the risk
hurdle, so the supply response waits. Colliers expects speculative Brisbane
metro projects only "from late 2027".

**Q4. Why does redevelopment happen, in one line?**

When the value of the cleared site exceeds the value of the property in its
existing use.

**Q5. Name three reasons the property market is less efficient than the share market.**

Any three of: illiquidity; slow transaction process; imperfect information
(no central price screen, unique assets, lagged sale evidence); planning
constraints; supply lag.

## 1.12 Topic 1 Summary

- **Price change → movement along the curve → change in *quantity*
  demanded. Non-price change → shift → change in *demand*.** Never confuse
  them.
- Demand shifters for property: **market size, income/wealth, price of
  substitutes, expectations, interest rates and credit availability.**
  Supply shifters: **input prices, technology, substitutes in production,
  number of firms, expected future prices.**
- New construction needs **profit *and* low uncertainty**: factors of
  production, expectations, perceived risk.
- **Short-run supply is inelastic**, so a demand shock comes out mostly in
  price. Long-run supply is elastic, so it comes out mostly in quantity.
- Equilibrium is where Qd = Qs; below P\* excess demand bids price up, above
  P\* excess supply pushes it down.
- A **rezoning is a supply intervention that provokes a demand response**,
  and it moves two markets in opposite directions.
- Property is **illiquid, slow, informationally imperfect and planning
  constrained** — hence inefficient.
- **Economic** characteristics: immobility, large economic units,
  durability, scarcity, land unproductive by itself. Not physical. Not legal.
- **Redevelop when cleared-site value > existing-use value.**
- Land use forms a **hierarchy based on ability to pay**: retail/commercial
  outbids industrial/residential, which outbids agricultural.
