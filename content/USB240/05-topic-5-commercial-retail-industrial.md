## 5.3 Commercial office grades

| Grade | Description |
|---|---|
| **Premium** | The highest-quality office space in the market. Generally newly constructed, fitted with top-of-the-line fixtures, amenities, HVAC and technological systems. |
| **A Grade** | High quality; shares several similarities with Premium. Typically newly constructed, in a highly sought-after location, but may differ on size, floorplates, Green Star or NABERS ratings, or quality of finishes. |
| **B Grade** | Good office space. Usually lacks the high-quality fixtures, locational appeal, architectural details and impressive lobbies of Premium or A. Generally nice structures with fully functional facilities. **Some buildings start as A Grade and are downgraded after 10 years, or once wear and tear becomes apparent. Capital investment and refurbishment can reverse this.** |
| **C Grade** | The poorest-quality structures on the market. Tend to be in less desirable areas and usually need major repairs or complete renovation, typically because of age — C Grade properties are generally **more than 20 years old**. |

The reference document is the Property Council of Australia's *A Guide to
Office Building Quality*, third edition.

> **Grading is self-regulated and relative to the sub-market — say both.** The
> deck's own words: *"a formal standard exists for classifying a building but
> it is self-regulated. Buildings must be viewed in the context of their
> sub-market. A Premium / Class A building in one city (or suburb) may not be
> a Premium / Class A building in another."* So "A Grade" in Toowoomba is not
> "A Grade" in the Brisbane CBD, and an owner has an incentive to grade
> generously. Two marks in a short-answer question: **self-regulated**, and
> **sub-market relative**.

> **Grade is not permanent, and this is the examinable consequence.** An A
> Grade asset can be downgraded to B after about ten years or once wear shows
> — and **capital investment and refurbishment can reverse it.** That makes
> grade a *managed* attribute, which is why the Week 7 tutorial lists
> "conversion of B and C grade product to apartments" as an office market
> challenge: if you cannot refurbish your way back up, you change the use.

### The two Brisbane examples

| | **320 Adelaide Street** | **1 Eagle Street** |
|---|---|---|
| Grade | **B Grade** | **Premium** |
| Occupancy | circa 95% | circa 95% |
| Condition | Refurbishment proposed | Extensively refurbished |
| Major tenants | Christie Spaces | Minter Ellison, CBRE, Dexus |
| **NLA** | **18,732 m²** | **59,558 m²** |

> **Both buildings are ~95% occupied, and only one is Premium.** Occupancy is
> not a grade indicator — it is a leasing outcome. What separates them is
> **NLA (3.2× larger), floor plate, location, finish and tenant covenant**.
> If an MCQ offers "high occupancy" as a defining characteristic of Premium
> grade, that is the distractor.

| **Population growth** | Queensland recorded the highest interstate migration in Australia between the 2016 and 2021 Censuses, a net increase of **107,549** new residents from other states. > that earns the mark.** A headline unemployment rate tells you nothing about
> office demand. **Professional, scientific and technical services** employment
> does — that is the cohort that occupies desks. The industrial equivalent is
> transport, postal and warehousing. Match the employment series to the
> property sector and you are analysing rather than reciting.

## 5.5 The office market indicators

From the Week 7 tutorial, the key market indicators for **commercial**:

- Leasing — **take-up by tenant type**
- **Net absorption** (sqm)
- **Net additions** (sqm)
- **Vacancy rate**
- **Face rents ($/sqm)** and rental growth
- **Incentive (%)**
- **Yield (%)**

### Market versus sub-market

- Grade of asset
- Substitute to higher grade and CBD location
- Location: **CBD, Fringe, Metro (hubs)**
- Impact on the office environment and working arrangements following COVID-19
- Co-working trends
- **The reduction of workspace per worker** — the PCA standard is only
  **10 m² per person** in the Office Building Quality Guidelines
- Government investment in infrastructure

### Office market challenges

- **Conversion of B and C grade product to apartments**
- **Location, location, location** — near mixed use and amenity (Toowong,
  Newstead)
- Energy costs
- Sustainability / Green Star / NABERS
- Post-COVID workplace environments and flexible working

> **10 m² per person turns a headcount into a leasing requirement, and that
> is a calculation Section C can ask for.**
>
> ```
> A tenant with 240 staff needs  240 × 10 m²  =  2,400 m² of NLA
> If hybrid working cuts attendance to 60% of desks:
>                                 144 × 10 m²  =  1,440 m²   →  40% less space
> ```
>
> This is why the unit says office demand is falling **not because vacancy is
> rising from tenants leaving, but because tenants take less space at
> renewal**. The tenant is still there; the requirement shrank.

> **The deck's own footnote says this table "reflects Q4 2022 data".** It is
> more than three years old in a Semester 1 2026 unit, and the slide says
> "see Colliers 2024 for updates". Do not quote these as current market
> conditions — quote them as an **exercise in reading a market table**, and
> use the Colliers Q1 2026 figures below for anything about today.

Even as a 2022 snapshot, the table teaches the right reading:

```
Brisbane      vacancy DOWN, absorption strongly POSITIVE and accelerating,
              rents UP 5.7%, but incentives HIGHEST at 42.9%, yields HIGHEST
              (5.25–6.50%)  →  strong occupier demand, weakest pricing

Melbourne     vacancy UP, absorption NEGATIVE and reversed from positive,
              rents flat, incentives 39.2%   →  weak occupier demand

Sydney        vacancy UP but absorption positive, highest rents, lowest
              incentives and lowest yields  →  most expensive, best priced
```

> **Highest incentive and highest yield in the same column is the market
> telling you it is the cheapest, not the strongest.** A 42.9% incentive means
> nearly half the headline rent is given back; a 6.5% yield means investors
> demand more return per dollar of income than in Sydney (4.38%). Brisbane had
> the best *demand* momentum and the weakest *pricing* — which is exactly the
> arbitrage that drew capital north.

The narrative behind those numbers:

- **The market has turned.** Brisbane Metro has ended a growth streak
  consistent since mid-2021. Even the previously resilient A grade sector,
  which had benefited from flight to quality, has slowed. Overall market net
  absorption fell to **−15,824 m²** and the all-grade vacancy rate rose from
  **10.5% to 11.4%**. This is the **first negative net absorption in Brisbane
  Metropolitan in nearly five years.**
- **Supply has stopped.** High construction and labour costs have created a
  supply gap "not seen in at least the past 20 years". Speculative projects
  may emerge only from **late 2027**.
- **Rents.** Over the past year prime rents rose **5% to an average
  $727/m²**, B grade rose **2.7% to $625/m²** — a slowing trend not seen since
  2023.
- **Investment.** Approximately **$896 million** of sales in 2025, a **208%
  increase** on 2024 (implying roughly $291m in 2024). Cross River Rail is
  reshaping fringe precincts and drawing investors near transport hubs.

**Brisbane Urban Renewal** (Fortitude Valley / Newstead — "widely regarded as
an extension of the CBD"):

- First negative net absorption since 2021: **−10,586 m²** in the six months
  to January 2026, the sharpest decline in the Brisbane metropolitan market,
  from macroeconomic pressure and **fringe tenants relocating to the CBD**
- Vacancy jumped from **8.6% (July 2025) to 10.7% (January 2026)**
- Last major completion was 895 Ann Street, Fortitude Valley, in 2023, with
  only **1,788 m²** added since
- A grade rents **+10%** over the year, B grade **+5%**, against CBD A grade
  **+11%** and B grade **+7%**
- Investment volume rose to **$317 million** after **no sales in 2024**

> **The Brisbane fringe office story is the single most useful case study in
> this unit for Section C, because it is counter-intuitive in three ways at
> once.** Rents are *rising* while absorption is *negative*; vacancy is
> *rising* while supply is *zero*; and investors are *buying more* while
> occupiers are *leaving*. The reconciliation: falling demand meets a frozen
> pipeline, so the surviving quality stock still prices well, and investors are
> buying the future scarcity rather than the current occupancy. If you can
> explain that, you can explain any market table.

> **East Brisbane is fringe, not CBD, and the fringe is losing tenants to the
> CBD.** Urban Renewal's −10,586 m² is explicitly attributed to *"fringe
> tenants relocating to the CBD"*. Any Assessment-2-style argument for an
> office use at 26 Hampton Street has to answer that. Two student groups asked
> the lecturer this exact question in the Week 9 information session.

### The rents that do not reconcile — and why

Try to derive net effective rent from net face rent and the quoted incentive:

```
Net effective  =  Net face × (1 − incentive)

Melbourne Metro   $503 × (1 − 0.40) = $301.8   reported $301   ✓
West Perth        $423 × (1 − 0.31) = $291.9   reported $291   ✓

Brisbane Metro    $601 × (1 − 0.36) = $384.6   reported $337   ✗  ($48 out)
Sydney Metro      $662 × (1 − 0.41) = $390.6   reported $341   ✗  ($50 out)
Adelaide Fringe   $355 × (1 − 0.21) = $280.4   reported $253   ✗  ($27 out)
```

The pattern is not random. Colliers' own footnote explains it:

> *"Incentives — Sydney, Brisbane and Adelaide are **gross** incentives and
> Melbourne and Perth are **net** incentives."*

> **You cannot mix a gross incentive with a net rent, and this is a genuine
> exam-grade trap.** The two markets quoted on **net** incentives reconcile to
> the dollar. The three quoted on **gross** incentives do not, because a gross
> incentive is measured against gross rent (rent *plus* outgoings), so applying
> it to a net face rent understates the discount. Brisbane's true implied
> discount is:
>
> ```
> 1 − (337 / 601)  =  43.9%      not the quoted 36%
> ```
>
> **Read the footnotes before you do the arithmetic**, and if you must compute
> an effective rent, say which basis you used.

## 5.7 Retail — the PCA classification hierarchy

This table is the most memorisable thing in Week 5 and it is prime MCQ
material. The source is the Property Council of Australia's Shopping Centre
Classifications.

| Class | GLA | Anchors | Specialty stores |
|---|---|---|---|
| **City Centre** | **> 100,000 m²** | Dominated by specialty shops; **typically do NOT include supermarkets**; typically coexist with department stores | — |
| **Super Regional** | **> 85,000 m²** | **Two** full-line department stores; **two or more** supermarkets; one or more discount department stores | circa **> 250** |
| **Major Regional** | **50,000 – 85,000 m²** | One or more full-line department stores; one or more discount department stores; one or more supermarkets | circa **150** |
| **Regional** | **30,000 – 50,000 m²** | **One** full-line department store; one full-line discount department store; one or more supermarkets | circa **100** |
| **Sub Regional** | **10,000 – 30,000 m²** | One discount department store; one full-line supermarket | circa **40** |
| **Neighbourhood** | **< 10,000 m²** | **One** full-line supermarket | circa **10–35** |
| **Bulky Goods** | **> 5,000 m²** | Dominated by bulky goods retailers (furniture, white goods) | Often a small number |

> **GLA alone does not classify a centre — the anchor mix does.** A 110,000 m²
> centre could be City Centre *or* Super Regional; what separates them is that
> **City Centre typically has no supermarket** while Super Regional has **two
> or more**. Likewise Bulky Goods (>5,000 m²) overlaps every band above it on
> size and is distinguished purely by **tenant type**. Read GLA first, then
> test against the anchors.

### Worked example — classify Westfield Chermside

| Feature | Chermside |
|---|---|
| Department stores | David Jones, Myer |
| Discount department stores | Big W, Target, Kmart, Harris Scarfe |
| Supermarkets | Coles **and** Woolworths |
| Other key tenants | Event Cinemas, Uniqlo, H&M, Zara, Sephora |
| Specialty stores | circa **480** |
| **GLA** | circa **175,000 m²** |
| **MAT** | **> $1,200,000,000 per annum** |

```
Step 1 — GLA test:      175,000 m²  →  above the >100,000 (City Centre) and
                        >85,000 (Super Regional) thresholds; both remain open

Step 2 — anchor test:   TWO full-line department stores (DJ, Myer)   → Super Regional ✓
                        TWO supermarkets (Coles, Woolworths)          → Super Regional ✓
                        City Centre "typically does not include supermarkets" → ruled out

Step 3 — specialty:     ~480 stores against "circa >250"              → Super Regional ✓

Classification: SUPER REGIONAL
```

Then run the productivity metric:

```
Productivity ($/m²)  =  MAT / GLA
                     =  1,200,000,000 / 175,000
                     =  $6,857 per m² per annum
```

> **MAT is stated as "greater than $1.2 billion", so $6,857/m² is a floor, not
> a point estimate.** Write it as "at least $6,857/m²". And note the GLA is
> **2.06×** the Super Regional threshold — Chermside is at the top of its class,
> not the bottom, which is why it carries 480 specialty stores against a "circa
> 250" guideline.

### Worked example — the bulky goods centre

The deck's bulky goods case (HomeCo Midland, Perth):

| Feature | Value |
|---|---|
| Key tenants | Winning Appliances, Spotlight, Rugs a Million, National Storage, Chemist Warehouse, Audi |
| Tenancies | circa **25 large format** |
| **GLA** | **19,958 m²** |
| MAT | Typically do not report sales |
| Last sale price | **$85 million (2016)** |
| **Initial yield** | **5.9%** |

Back out the income the buyer paid for:

```
Net income  =  Price × Initial yield
            =  85,000,000 × 0.059
            =  $5,015,000 per annum

Income per m²  =  5,015,000 / 19,958  =  $251.28 / m² pa
```

> **"Typically do not report sales" is itself the analytical point.** Without
> MAT you cannot compute occupancy cost or productivity, so you cannot judge
> whether the rent is sustainable. That is a structural information problem in
> bulky goods retail, and it is why the yield sits *above* a comparable
> supermarket-anchored centre: less information means more perceived risk.

- Incentive (%)
- Yield (%)

### Emerging trends

| Trend | What it is | How it impacts |
|---|---|---|
| **Localism** | Consumers becoming increasingly attached to local ecosystems and their communities | Retailers must become entrenched in the catchments they serve; **landlords must increase the supply of public and community spaces** to secure centres as community hubs |

### Challenges

Trade · annual turnover · energy costs · experience-based retail · online
shopping (eBay, Amazon) · buy-now-pay-later (Afterpay, ZipPay, Klarna).

> **"90% of sales still in store" is the statistic that reframes the
> e-commerce question.** Online is the *growth* story, not the *volume* story.
> The strategic response in the deck is not to fight online but to sell what
> online cannot: **dwell time and experience**. That is why gyms, medical and
> childcare appear in a *retail* tenancy mix — they are footfall generators
> that cannot be delivered by parcel.

### Trends and drivers

| Trend / factor | Content |
|---|---|
| **Critical to economy** | An essential platform for the movement of goods, manufacturing and economic development |
| **Supported by infrastructure investment** | Improved road networks both facilitate and drive demand. Government expenditure on infrastructure increased 4.8% in the year to June 2018 |
| **Technology and innovation** | Automation, robotics, autonomous vehicles, big data and AI are shaping the sector: **last mile facilities · increasing warehouse densities · increased JIT distribution · higher ceilings (up to 13 metres)** |
| **E-commerce** | Approximately **14% penetration**, up from **7.4%** the year prior |
| **Drivers** | Population growth · unemployment · GDP/GSP · infrastructure spend · future events |

The deck asks: **"Technology and automation is increasing WALE? Why?"**

> **Because automation makes the fit-out specific to the tenant, and
> specificity buys term.** A tenant who installs automated racking, robotics
> and a 13-metre-clear specification has sunk capital into *that* building.
> Relocating means writing it off, so the tenant signs a longer lease — and a
> longer weighted average lease expiry is exactly what an investor pays a
> tighter yield for. Capital intensity converts into lease term.

- Net absorption (m²), net additions (m²), future supply (m²)
- Vacancy rate
- Face rents ($/m²) and rental growth
- Incentive (%)
- Yield (%)

### Challenges

Planning constraints · infrastructure charges · energy costs ·
suburbanisation of manufacturing · availability of land in the south and
south-west · the freight task · e-commerce and same-day delivery.

**Q1. Name the four traditional sectors and two classifications of each.**

Commercial office — Premium, A, B, C, Regional. Retail — CBD/City Centre,
Super Regional, Major Regional, Regional, Sub-Regional, Neighbourhood, Large

multiple dwelling units (apartments), house and land.

**Q2. Define vacancy rate, net absorption, face rent, effective rent, incentive and yield.**

Vacancy rate — the percentage of lettable space currently unoccupied. Net
absorption — the change in occupied space over a period; a demand signal.
Face rent — the advertised or headline rent, before incentives. Effective
rent — the rent after incentives are deducted. Incentive — an inducement to a
tenant (rent-free period, fit-out contribution), expressed as a percentage.
Yield — annual income divided by property value.

**Q3. A centre has 62,000 m² GLA, one full-line department store, two discount department stores, one supermarket and about 150 specialty stores. Classify it.**

```
GLA 62,000 m² → falls in 50,000–85,000  → Major Regional
One or more full-line DS ✓ ; one or more DDS ✓ ; one or more supermarket ✓
~150 specialty ✓

**Q4. A bulky goods centre of 12,400 m² sold for $46 million on a 6.4% initial yield. What income did the buyer capitalise, and what is that per square metre?**

**Q5. Prime net face rent is $601/m² and the quoted incentive is 36%. Why can you not conclude the effective rent is $385/m²?**

Because Brisbane incentives in that table are quoted **gross**, not net. A
gross incentive is measured against gross rent (including outgoings), so it
cannot be applied directly to a net face rent. The reported net effective is
$337, implying a **43.9%** discount off net face, not 36%. Melbourne and
Perth, quoted on net incentives, do reconcile exactly.

**Q6. Vacancy is rising, net supply is zero and rents are still growing. Explain.**

Vacancy is rising through **negative net absorption** — tenants giving space
back — not through new supply. Because the construction pipeline has stalled
on high costs, the surviving quality stock still commands rental growth, and
investors buy anticipated future scarcity even while current occupancy falls.
That is the Brisbane Metro Q1 2026 position exactly.

**Q7. Why does automation increase WALE?**

Automated racking, robotics and high-clear specifications are capital sunk
into a specific building. Relocation means writing that investment off, so
tenants sign longer leases — lengthening the weighted average lease expiry and
supporting a tighter investment yield.
