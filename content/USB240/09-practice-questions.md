### Question 41

Compare **primary** and **secondary** data collection methods, including the
**advantages and disadvantages of both**. Give **two examples** of each type.
*(10 marks)*

<details>
<summary>✅ Model answer — Q41</summary>

**Definitions.** **Primary data** is data you collect yourself, for this
specific purpose. **Secondary data** is data already collected by someone
else, for some other purpose, and includes both raw data and compiled or
published summaries.

**Examples.** Primary: a survey of suburb residents; a semi-structured
interview with a market economist. *(Also: observation, focus groups, case
studies.)* Secondary: the **ABS Census**; a published **Colliers** market
report. *(Also: RBA statistics, Price Finder transaction data, Cordell
development data.)*

**Primary — advantages.** It is **current**; it is **tailored** to your exact
research question; you **control the quality** of collection; and you can ask
follow-up questions where the method allows.

**Primary — disadvantages.** It is **time consuming** and **costly**; it
requires **ethics clearance** and participant consent (written consent for
interviews and focus groups); and sample sizes are usually **smaller**, which
limits generalisability.

**Secondary — advantages.** It is **fast and cheap**; the datasets are
**large and established** (the Census counts the whole population); it allows
**longitudinal** comparison because historic series already exist; and it is
**non-obtrusive**.

**Secondary — disadvantages.** It is **not tailored** to your question; it may
be **dated** — the last Census was August 2021; you did **not control how it
was collected**, so you inherit its definitions and errors; and the
geographic boundaries may not match the area you are analysing.

**Choosing between them** depends on the **research question** — what you are
trying to prove — plus available **time, money and resources**. This unit
relies mainly on secondary data because primary collection requires time and
ethics clearance that a semester does not allow. A **multi-method** approach
using both is **triangulation**, which improves the validity and reliability
of the findings.

</details>

### Question 42

Identify the **key economic indicators** you would use to assess the strength
of the residential property market in a Brisbane suburb. Explain **what each
indicator tells you** and at which **geographic level** you would source it.
*(10 marks)*

<details>
<summary>✅ Model answer — Q42</summary>

Structure the answer by level; the lecturer said the marks are for naming the
right indicators at the right level, not for reciting current values.

**National level.** **GDP and GDP growth** — the size and direction of the
economy, and the underlying capacity to pay; note that headline GDP growth
should be read against population growth to get **per-capita** growth.
**Inflation (CPI)** — drives RBA policy; the target band is **2–3%**.
**The cash rate** — set by the RBA; it flows into mortgage rates and so into

**State level.** **Gross State Product**; **Queensland unemployment**;

**Suburb level.** **Population and age profile**; **household income**;
**household and family structure**; amenity attributes such as schools,
health facilities and transport.

**median rent** and rental growth; **gross rental yield**; **days on market**;

### Question 43

Explain **von Thünen's model** and its relevance to modern urban land use.
Identify **three** assumptions of the model and **two** limitations. *(10 marks)*

<details>
<summary>✅ Model answer — Q43</summary>

**The model.** Von Thünen (1826) is the oldest land-use model, connecting
spatial economics with the theory of rent. It places a single city in an
isolated state and asks how surrounding land will be used. **Land use,
intensity of use and rent are determined by transport cost and distance to the
market** — land closer to the market bears lower transport cost, so it can
pay higher rent and is used more intensively. The result is a pattern of
**concentric rings**, each specialising in a use: intense farming, forest,
extensive farming, then grazing.

**Relevance to modern urban land use.** Von Thünen is the forerunner of the
land-use / density / value concept and the foundation of **bid-rent theory**.
Replace agricultural products with urban uses and the same logic produces a
**hierarchy of uses based on ability to pay**: retail and commercial outbid
industrial and residential, which outbid agricultural. Applied to a city with
two users and an optimal CBD location, **commercial outbids residential**,
pushing residential outward, with value and density both diminishing with
distance. Burgess's 1925 concentric model is explicitly described as an urban
version of von Thünen.

**Three assumptions** (any three): one centrally located city in an isolated
state — **one city, one market**; decisions are made to **maximise economic
benefit**; land is **flat**; **soil quality and climate are consistent**;
**transport costs are fixed**; farmers **behave rationally**; the surrounding
area is wilderness. Framed generally: markets are **perfectly competitive**
and **constraining influences such as land-use controls and government
intervention are ignored**.

**Two limitations.** (1) It **assumes away topography and variable transport
costs**, so it cannot explain why a suburb 8 km from the CBD is cheaper than
its distance implies — the axial model, which accommodates topography and
infrastructure, explains that. (2) It **ignores the public sector** and
non-market land-use decisions such as zoning, heritage overlays and
government land release. More broadly, it is **descriptive rather than
analytical**, so it is not a predictive model.

*(These theories should be treated as mutually inclusive, not exclusive.)*

</details>

### Question 44

A client is comparing an office investment with an industrial investment.
Identify the **key market indicators** you would analyse for each sector,
define **four** of them, and explain what a **rising** value of each would
signal. *(10 marks)*

<details>
<summary>✅ Model answer — Q44</summary>

**Common to both sectors.** Vacancy rate · net absorption · net additions and
future supply · face rents and rental growth · incentive (%) · yield (%).

**Office-specific.** Leasing **take-up by tenant type** — because office
demand tracks **white-collar employment** (professional, scientific and
technical services), not headline employment. Also relevant: grade of asset,
sub-market (CBD / fringe / metro), and workspace ratios — the PCA standard is
**10 m² per person**, and hybrid working reduces requirements at renewal.

**Industrial-specific.** **Take-up (m²)**. Demand tracks e-commerce
penetration, the freight task and transport/postal/warehousing employment,
plus infrastructure investment and land availability.

**Four definitions and what a rise signals:**

**Vacancy rate** — the percentage of lettable space currently unoccupied. A
**rise** signals supply exceeding demand, which weakens the landlord's
bargaining position and puts downward pressure on effective rents. It can be
caused by new supply *or* by tenants giving space back.

**Net absorption** — the change in occupied space over a period; a demand
signal. A **rise** signals tenants taking more space overall, that is,
genuine expansion of demand. Negative absorption means the market is
contracting.

**Incentive (%)** — an inducement to the tenant, such as a rent-free period
or a fit-out contribution, expressed as a percentage of the lease. A **rise**
signals a weakening leasing market: the landlord is protecting the headline
face rent (and therefore capitalised value) by giving away cash instead.

**Yield (%)** — annual income divided by property value. A **rise** signals
that investors require more return per dollar of income, that is, higher
perceived risk or a softer market; values are falling relative to income.
Compressing yields signal the opposite.

**A caution on rents.** Face rent is the headline before incentives;
effective rent is after. A market can show face rental growth while effective
rents are flat because incentives rose. Incentives may be quoted **gross** or
**net**, and you cannot apply a gross incentive percentage to a net face rent.

</details>

### Question 45

Explain **cultural heritage** and its relevance to property market analysis.
Identify the **four levels** at which Australian heritage is managed and name
the principal instrument at each. Give **two** arguments for and **two**
against heritage protection. *(10 marks)*

<details>
<summary>✅ Model answer — Q45</summary>

**Definition.** Cultural heritage is the **tangible and intangible** heritage
of a group or society, inherited from past generations — and importantly,
**heritage is a product of selection by society**: not everything from the past
is heritage. It comprises **tangible** culture (buildings, monuments,
landscapes, objects), **intangible** culture (language, folklore, tradition,
knowledge) and **natural** heritage (culturally significant landscapes,
biodiversity).

**Relevance to property analysis.** Heritage listing and overlays are
**planning controls**: they determine what can be built, altered or
demolished, and therefore constrain the development envelope and feasibility.
Destroying or altering heritage without permission carries **heavy
penalties**. Heritage also affects value in both directions, so a site
analysis must check heritage before any highest-and-best-use conclusion.

**The four levels:**

| Level | Instrument |
|---|---|
| **World** | UNESCO World Heritage List — Australia has 20 sites, 5 in Queensland |
| **National** | Australia's National Heritage List — 120 places, 13 in Queensland |
| **State** | **Queensland Heritage Act 1992**, register administered by the Department of Environment and Heritage Protection (1,992+ places). Indigenous heritage is separate, under the **Aboriginal Cultural Heritage Act 2003** and **Torres Strait Islander Cultural Heritage Act 2003** |
| **Local** | Local government heritage register or the local planning scheme, e.g. Brisbane City Council |

**Two arguments for.** (1) It protects cultural value and generates
**economic** benefits — tourism stimulus (Tisdell and Wilson 2002) and
measurable price premiums; the Auckland study found a **+4.3%** premium in
Special Character Areas and **+1.7%** for a heritage feature within 50 m.
(2) Demolition is **irreversible**: recovering what is lost is enormously more
expensive than preserving it, and a displaced community cannot be restored at
all.

**Two arguments against.** (1) It **restricts housing supply** at a time of
undersupply — a supply-side planning constraint that shifts the supply curve
left. (2) Strong regulation imposes a real cost on owners: houses located
**exclusively** within an Auckland Heritage Designated Area, where building
regulations are strong, recorded an average price **reduction of 9.6%**.

</details>

### Question 46

Distinguish between **quantitative** and **qualitative** data, and between
**primary** and **secondary** data. Using **five examples**, show that these
are two independent classifications. *(10 marks)*

<details>
<summary>✅ Model answer — Q46</summary>

**The two classifications answer different questions.**
Primary/secondary answers **who collected the data and why**.
Quantitative/qualitative answers **what form the data takes**.

**Quantitative** data is information about **quantities** — measurable and
written down with numbers: how many, how much, how often. It requires a
**measurable difference between points**, and may be **discrete** (only
specific values) or **continuous** (any value in an interval). Tools: mean,
median, mode, range, standard deviation. Methods: surveys, questionnaires,
structured observation.

**Qualitative** data is information about **types**, and can be represented
by a name or symbol — gender, eye colour, the suburb where you live. It is
used to **describe a situation** and can capture anything not numerical in
nature. Methods: in-depth and semi-structured interviews, focus groups,
participant observation, case studies, documentary analysis. It can be
obtained from **either** primary or secondary sources.

**Five examples showing independence:**

1. A **survey you administer** to East Brisbane residents — **primary and
   quantitative**
2. An **in-depth interview** you conduct with a market economist — **primary
   and qualitative**
3. **ABS Census median income** for the suburb — **secondary and
   quantitative**
4. A **direct quotation from a published Colliers report** on market
   sentiment — **secondary and qualitative**
5. **Structured observation** of pedestrian counts you carry out on site —
   **primary and quantitative**

**A caution.** Numbers alone do not make data quantitative. A **postcode**
(4169) is a numeral used as a **label** — there is no measurable difference
between 4169 and 4170 — so it is qualitative data expressed numerically.
The test is measurable difference between points, not the presence of digits.

</details>

### Question 47

A developer is assessing a 1,400 m² inner-city site currently improved with an
800 m² office/warehouse. Explain **five factors** you would investigate in a
**site analysis**, and explain how **urban growth theory** can be used to
justify your reading of the suburb. *(10 marks)*

<details>
<summary>✅ Model answer — Q47</summary>

**Five factors** (exactly five, as asked):

1. **Physical characteristics of the site** — area, shape, frontage, access
   and existing improvements. Here, 800 m² on 1,400 m² is roughly **57% site
   cover**, so the existing improvement is low-rise. That frames the question
   as one of **redevelopment**: does the cleared-site value exceed the value
   of the property in its existing use?
2. **Town planning** — zoning, permitted uses, density and height controls,
   and the assessment pathway. Planning controls restrict what can be built and
   therefore determine supply and value.
3. **Heritage** — check all four levels, and in particular the **local**
   heritage overlay in the planning scheme and the **Aboriginal cultural
   heritage** database, since the ACH Act 2003 protects a significant area, a
   significant object, **or evidence** of archaeological or historic
   significance of occupation.
4. **Natural hazard exposure** — flood overlay, storm and, in relevant
   locations, bushfire. This affects insurance cost, finance and resale, and
   is often material for inner-Brisbane riverside suburbs.
5. **Surrounding amenity and accessibility** — distance to the CBD, public
   transport, schools, hospitals, parks and retail; plus existing and future
   projects nearby, both **competing** and **complementary**, mapped out.

*(Also available: a site visit as primary data collection — photos, walking
and driving the site and surrounds; and the suburb's demography.)*

**Using urban growth theory.** The suburb should be explained **historically,
currently, and in terms of what it is transforming into**, and urban growth
theory supplies that narrative. **Von Thünen and bid-rent** explain why an
inner-city site carries a land value that an industrial use can no longer
justify — the hierarchy of uses by ability to pay has moved past
warehousing. **Axial development** explains the effect of transport
infrastructure such as Cross River Rail on accessibility and therefore value
along the corridor. **Multiple nuclei** explains the emergence of secondary
centres and mixed-use precincts outside the CBD. Because these theories are
**mutually inclusive rather than exclusive**, naming two or three and saying
what each explains — and what each misses, since they are **descriptive rather
than predictive** — is stronger than choosing one.

</details>

| Residential — rental vacancy rate | 1.4% (REIA healthy benchmark 3.0%) |

| Office (metro/fringe) — vacancy | 10.7%, up from 8.6% six months earlier |
| Office (metro/fringe) — prime net face rent | $601/m², +5% over the year |

| Retail — trend | ~90% of sales still transacted in store; tenancy mixes diversifying toward gyms, medical, childcare |
| Development pipeline, postcode, 2026 | 13 projects, $306.5m, **all at "Possible" status**; 528 units; **no detached dwellings, no lots** |

**(a)** Assess each of the four sectors against the data. *(12 marks)*
**(b)** Identify the site-specific investigations you would complete before

**(c)** Present the data in a form that supports your argument. *(6 marks)*
**(d)** Recommend a use and justify it. *(6 marks)*

<details>
<summary>✅ Section C Q49 — outline of a strong answer</summary>

**(a) Sector by sector.**

**Residential — strongest case.** Double-digit price growth, vacancy at 1.4%
against a 3.0% healthy benchmark, and a pipeline that delivers **no detached
dwellings at all**. Demand indicators all point one way. The caveat is that
the pipeline delivers **528 units**, so the competing supply is in exactly the
product a residential development here would produce — and 100% of that
pipeline is at "**Possible**" status, so it is neither committed nor safely
discounted.

**Office — weakest case.** Negative net absorption of **−10,586 m²** in six
months, vacancy rising from 8.6% to 10.7%, and the underlying cause is
**fringe tenants relocating to the CBD** — which is precisely what this site
would be competing against. Note the apparent contradiction: rents are still
**rising 5%** on a **zero** supply pipeline. That reflects scarcity of quality
stock rather than occupier strength, and a new fringe office building would be
entering a market that is losing tenants. Reject, but explain the rent figure
rather than ignoring it.

**Industrial — weak on this site.** Only $1.6m of industrial pipeline in the
whole postcode, in a suburb transitioning away from industrial use as
bid-rent moves past warehousing (von Thünen: the hierarchy of uses by ability
to pay). Inner-city land values cannot be supported by industrial rents.
Last-mile logistics is the one arguable exception and would need a specific
demand case.

**Retail — supporting, not leading.** 90% of sales are still in store and
tenancy mixes are diversifying toward experience and services, but a 1,395 m²
site cannot support a standalone centre at any PCA classification —
Neighbourhood requires a full-line supermarket. Retail belongs here as a small
ground-floor component of a mixed-use scheme, generating dwell time and
activating the street.

**(b) Site-specific investigations.** Zoning, permitted uses, density and
height under the planning scheme, and the assessment pathway · **heritage** at
all four levels, especially the **local overlay** and the **Aboriginal
cultural heritage database** · **flood** overlay and other hazard exposure ·
soil, contamination and remediation cost, given the existing industrial use ·
services capacity · a **site visit** as primary data, with photographs of the
site and its surrounds · and a feasibility test at current construction costs
and the current cash rate.

**(c) Presentation.** A **bar chart** comparing a normalised demand indicator
across the four sectors, or a **table** with a consistent column set (demand
signal, supply signal, risk) so the sectors are genuinely comparable. If you
use the pipeline data, present it **twice** — by value and by count — and say
which base you are using, because the two tell different stories. Title, axis
labels, source line, ruler.

**(d) Recommendation.**

> **Recommendation: medium-density residential with a small ground-floor
> retail component (mixed use).** Residential is the only sector whose demand
> indicators all point the same way — double-digit price growth, vacancy at
> less than half the healthy benchmark, and no detached or land supply in the
> pipeline. Office is rejected because the fringe market is losing tenants to
> the CBD; industrial is rejected because inner-city land value has moved past
> what industrial rents can support; retail is included only as an activating
> ground-floor component, since the site is too small for any standalone PCA
> classification. The recommendation is conditional on heritage and flood
> clearance, on remediation cost given the existing industrial improvement,
> and on feasibility at current construction costs — noting that 100% of the
> competing 2026 pipeline is at "Possible" status and may not proceed, which
> is an opportunity as much as a risk.
