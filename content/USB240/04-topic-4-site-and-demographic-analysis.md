## 4.1 Why demographics belongs in a property unit

The tutorial puts it as a syllogism worth memorising:

> Economics is about the allocation of scarce resources — **supply and
> demand**. Demographics gives us information about a population's **current
> and future demand for property**. We study demographics to ensure the
> product we **supply** matches the **demand**.

Definition, from the Week 5 tutorial:

> **Demographics** — studies of a population based on factors such as age,
> race, sex, economic status, level of education, income level and employment,
> among others. Used by governments, corporations and non-government
> organisations to learn about a population's characteristics for many
> purposes, including policy development and economic market research.
>
> **Demographic trends are also important**, as the size of different
> demographic groups will change over time as a result of economic, cultural
> and political circumstances.

**Once you know *who*, you can determine *where* and *what* to build:** high
or low amenity, whether it needs to be near other things, price points, what
your return could be, and what supporting infrastructure it needs — office
and commercial space, roads, transport, entertainment.

### The product logic

```
Developing and selling a product = identifying a market and satisfying their needs
Market identification            = segmenting the population
Population segmentation          = demographics
```

Buyer segments in the Australian residential market: **renters · first home
buyers · upgraders · downsizers · retirees · the aged care market.**

Then three questions the tutorial insists you ask:

1. Is this the **right demographic**?
2. Does this demographic have the **ability to buy**?
3. What are you **competing against** — and are you "pioneering", and is that
   good or bad?

> **"Right demographic" has two halves and students answer only one.**
> Identifying that East Brisbane skews young and professional is the easy
> half. The second half is **ability to pay** — a young renter cohort creates
> demand for *rental* product at a price point they can service, not for
> $1.6M houses. Demand without capacity is not demand. Say both.

## 4.2 Suburb and neighbourhood characteristics

The checklist appears in both the Week 4 lecture and the Week 5 tutorial, so
it is likely examinable as "what would you assess about a suburb":

- **The demography** — types of families, education levels, incomes of
  existing residents
- Are residents **owner-occupiers or renters**? How has the property market
  fared in recent years?
- **Distance to public amenities** — schools, hospitals, parks, transport
  options, availability of restaurants
- **Distance to CBD**, tourist destinations, airports, sporting events, other
  infrastructure
- Existence of **green space, walkways**
- **Variety and quality of housing types** and the architecture
- The **safety and friendliness** of the area
- Existence of **noise** and **threats from natural disasters** — e.g. floods

## 4.4 Population and its components

```
Population growth = births − deaths + arrivals − departures
                  = natural increase + net migration
```

For Queensland, add a third component: **net interstate migration**. The
Week 5 tutorial's activity is to build the Queensland chart with all three
series — natural increase, net overseas migration, net interstate migration —
where the national chart has only the first two.

**Australia's population as at 19 March 2026: 27+ million.**

### The ABS population clock

| Event | Interval |
|---|---|
| One birth | every 2 minutes 16 seconds |
| One death | every 3 minutes 33 seconds |
| One person arriving to live in Australia | every 0 minutes 59 seconds |
| One Australian resident leaving to live overseas | every 2 minutes 35 seconds |
| **Overall total population increase** | **one person every 1 minute 15 seconds** |

Check it — the clock is internally consistent:

```
Births per day      86,400 / 136 s  =   635.3
Deaths per day      86,400 / 213 s  =   405.6
Arrivals per day    86,400 /  59 s  = 1,464.4
Departures per day  86,400 / 155 s  =   557.4
                                     ─────────
Net per day  635.3 − 405.6 + 1,464.4 − 557.4 = 1,136.7
Implied interval  86,400 / 1,136.7 = 76.0 s  =  1 min 16 s   (clock says 1 min 15 s)

Annualised at the stated 1m15s:  86,400 / 75 × 365.25  ≈  420,800 per year
```

The one-second gap is rounding in the component intervals. And the annualised
figure lands within 1% of the Colliers Q1 2026 statement that the population
rose by **approximately 424,000 to 27.7 million** over the year — two
independent sources agreeing.

> **Net migration is doing about three-quarters of the work.** Natural
> increase contributes 635 − 406 = **229 people a day**; net migration
> contributes 1,464 − 557 = **907 a day**. That is why the Week 3 note tracks
> net overseas migration (~311,000 of the ~424,000 annual increase) as a
> *property* indicator: it is the dominant driver of household formation, and
> it is the component most exposed to policy change.

## 4.5 The Census

**The ABS** is Australia's national statistical agency, providing key
statistics on economic, environmental and social issues.

> **The Census of Population and Housing** — a descriptive count of everyone
> who is in Australia on one night, and of their dwellings. The largest
> statistical collection undertaken by the ABS.

- Last collected **10 August 2021**
- Counted close to **10 million dwellings** and approximately **24.7 million
  people**

### What is collected

Age · gender · marital status · family structure · married more than once ·
number of children · birthplace of yourself and your parents · number of
residents in the house · impairments and disability · year arrived in
Australia · language use · citizenship · religion · Aboriginal and Torres
Strait Islander status · child care · hours worked · paid and unpaid work ·
industry you work in · education (level and qualifications) · type of
benefits received · number of bedrooms · how you get to work · number of
vehicles · rent or mortgage.

Up to the late 1970s the Census also collected building materials used and
facilities (gas, electricity, toilet, TV).

### Why do a census

- To **estimate the population** of each state, territory and local
  government area
- To determine **electoral boundaries**
- To show **characteristics of Australia's people and their housing** within
  small geographic areas and for small population groups
- To give governments and other users information for **planning,
  administration, policy development and evaluation**
- To help plan **basic services** — housing, social security, transport,
  education, industry, shops and hospitals

> **The Census "count" and the population "estimate" are different numbers,
> and both are correct.** The 2021 Census counted ~24.7 million people. The
> ABS estimated resident population at 30 June 2021 was ~25.9 million — you
> can back it out of the Indigenous statistics below (983,700 ÷ 3.8% =
> 25.89m). The ~1.2 million gap (about 4.8%) is people missed on Census night,
> temporarily overseas, or otherwise adjusted for. **Cite the Census for
> characteristics; cite ERP for population totals.**

## 4.6 Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander population

At **30 June 2021** there were **983,700** Aboriginal and Torres Strait
Islander people, representing **3.8%** of the total Australian population:

- **One-third (33.1%) were under 15 years of age**
- **Median age 24.0 years**
- **Three-quarters (74.5%) lived in NSW, Queensland and Western Australia
  combined**

Of the 983,700:

| Identification | Share |
|---|---|
| Aboriginal | 91.7% |
| Torres Strait Islander | 4.0% |
| Both Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander | 4.3% |
| **Total** | **100.0%** |

> **A median age of 24.0 against a national median in the mid-30s is a
> property statement, not just a social one.** A population one-third of whom
> are under 15 is a population forming households over the next fifteen years.
> Where that population is concentrated, the demand pipeline is for family
> housing and services, not downsizer product. The Week 5 tutorial has a
> hands-on exercise on this — pull the Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander
> Peoples Profile from ABS QuickStats for a suburb and read its age, income and
> education distribution.

## 4.7 The ageing population

- Over the twenty years between **2000 and 2020**, the proportion of the
  population aged **65 and over rose from 12.4% to 16.3%**
- This group is projected to increase **more rapidly over the next decade** as
  further cohorts of baby boomers (**born 1946–1964**) turn 65

### Intergenerational Report 2023

Projects the economy and the Commonwealth budget over the **next 40 years**.
It contains analysis of the **key drivers of economic growth — population,
participation and productivity** — and examines what projected changes mean
for living standards and public policy.

Its findings:

- Australians are living longer, with more years in full health and more time
  using government-funded services
- **Increased longevity alongside low fertility** means the population will
  continue to age over the next 40 years
- The number aged **65 and over will more than double**; the number aged
  **85 and over will more than triple**
- Population ageing will be an ongoing economic and fiscal challenge

The Treasurer's framing over the past 40 years: Australia became more open to
global trade and international investment, and the economy and workforce were
transformed by rising women's participation and the shift away from primary
production and manufacturing **towards services**.

> **"Population, participation and productivity" is the phrase to reproduce.**
> The three Ps are the IGR's own framework and they map cleanly onto property:
> *population* drives dwelling demand, *participation* drives office demand,
> *productivity* drives incomes and therefore capacity to pay. And from Cal's
> Canvas residential quiz: the **fastest-growing residential demographic group
> is the ageing population** — retirees and aged care. That shapes demand for
> downsizer dwellings, single-level low-maintenance housing and aged-care
> facilities.

## 4.8 Data sources

| Source | What it gives you |
|---|---|
| **ABS** | Census, regional population, economic series |
| **Intergenerational Report** | 40-year projections; the three Ps |
| **Queensland Government Statistician's Office** | State and regional demographic detail |
| **HILDA** | Household, Income and Labour Dynamics in Australia |
| **Australian Institute of Health and Welfare** | Health and welfare series |
| **National Housing Supply Council** | Housing supply analysis |
| **United Nations Population Fund** | International comparison |
| **Brisbane Community Profiles** (BCC) | Suburb-level profiles for Brisbane |
| **AIATSIS** | Map of Indigenous Australia |

### HILDA in detail

- Follows more than **17,000 Australians each year**
- Collects information on household and family relationships, income and
  employment, and health and education
- Participants are **followed over the course of their lifetime**
- **The only study of its kind in Australia**
- Researchers in Australia and overseas may apply to access the data

> **HILDA versus the Census is a longitudinal-versus-cross-sectional
> question in disguise.** The Census is a **cross-sectional** snapshot of
> everyone on one night, every five years. HILDA is a **longitudinal panel
> study** — the same people, followed for life. The Census tells you what the
> population looks like; HILDA tells you what happens to people over time.
> Note 06 formalises the distinction, and it is examinable there too.

## 4.9 The geography trap

The Week 5 tutorial devotes a slide headed **"Be Careful with Group
Assignments!!"** to this, and it is the best-designed trap in the unit.

The slide first states, as a single flowing sentence:

> *"In the 2021 Census, it was found that Brisbane has a population of
> 1,242,825 people across 674,188 households. The median age is 34, and the
> median weekly household income is $2,155."*

Then it shows where each number actually came from:

| Geography | Median weekly household income | Population | Median age | No. households |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| **Brisbane Inner City (SA4)** | **$2,155** | 292,245 | **34** | 70,141 |
| **Brisbane City (LGA)** | $2,068 | **1,242,825** | 36 | 324,833 |
| **Brisbane (GCCSA)** | $1,849 | 2,526,238 | 36 | **674,188** |

> **That one sentence mixes three different geographies.** The population is
> the **LGA**. The household count is the **GCCSA**. The median age and the
> median income are the **SA4**. Every individual number is real; the sentence
> is fiction. It describes a place that does not exist.
>
> The consequences are not cosmetic: income is **$2,155 or $1,849** depending
> on the boundary — a **16.5% difference** on the same "Brisbane". Choose the
> flattering one and your Section C recommendation is built on sand.

> **The household column does not survive a sanity check either, so do not
> quote it.** Divide population by households at each level:
>
> ```
> Brisbane Inner City (SA4)   292,245 /  70,141  =  4.17 persons per household
> Brisbane City (LGA)       1,242,825 / 324,833  =  3.83 persons per household
> Brisbane (GCCSA)          2,526,238 / 674,188  =  3.75 persons per household
> ```
>
> Australian average household size is about **2.5**. Values of 3.75–4.17 are
> implausible for inner Brisbane — an inner-city SA4 full of one- and
> two-person households should be *below* the national average, not 67% above
> it. So at least one of those two columns is not measuring what its label
> says, or the two columns come from different vintages. **Pull household
> counts from ABS QuickStats directly and state your source.**

### The rule to write down

```
Always name the geographic level beside the number:
    suburb / SA2  <  SA3  <  SA4  <  LGA  <  GCCSA  <  state  <  national
Compare like with like — a suburb against the state, not a suburb against a
capital-city region.
```

This is the same failure mode as Note 03's "Brisbane 8.0% vs Greater
Brisbane 12.5%" and Note 02's two different Brisbane populations. It is the
unit's signature trap and it will show up in Section C.

## 4.10 Demographics and urban change

Do broader demographic analysis **only when it is relevant** — national,
state, local. Cover **migration, overseas and interstate**.

**Demand determinants**, applied across the four sectors:

- Demographic trends
- Nearby areas
- Household income
- Household / family structures

### Greenfield and brownfield

| | What it is | Property implication |
|---|---|---|
| **Greenfield** | New cities, master-planned communities | Full infrastructure build; long lead times; house and land product |
| **Brownfield** | Redevelopment opportunity — urban renewal, higher density, "old houses / heritage"; or refurbishment of existing property to add value | Existing services; planning and heritage constraints; the East Brisbane case |

### The Pallara warning

The tutorial shows NearMap imagery of Pallara in **2000** and **2020** — open
paddocks, then a suburb.

> **"Be careful! The Census was taken in 2021. Look at where places are going;
> don't always assume status quo."** A five-yearly Census is a *lagging*
> indicator on a greenfield frontier. The forward-looking counterpart is the
> development pipeline data — which is exactly what PRD supplied for postcode
> 4169, and exactly what Note 08 takes apart.

## 4.11 East Brisbane — applying it

The Week 4 lecture points at the ABS QuickStats page for East Brisbane
(SA2 305021115) and then asks you to link demographics to the four sectors.

From the material available across the unit, the East Brisbane profile is:
a younger, professional, smaller-household population; a high unit share of
stock (apartments **50.2%** of dwelling structure against Greater Brisbane's
**14.7%**, with single dwellings **44%** against **73.4%** — figures quoted by
a student group in the Week 9 information session); close to the CBD,
Southbank and the Gabba; served by good schools; and carrying a **flood
overlay** on parts of the suburb.

> **Those dwelling-structure percentages come from a student's question in the
> Week 9 information session, not from a lecture slide.** They are consistent
> with everything else the unit says about East Brisbane being a unit market,
> but they have not been verified against ABS. Use them as an illustration of
> the *shape* of the suburb, and pull your own figures from QuickStats before
> putting a number in an assessment.

The chain a Section C answer should make explicit:

```
Young, professional, small households + high unit share + CBD proximity
   →  demand for one- and two-bedroom rental product near transport
   →  medium-density or mixed-use is the demographically supported use
   →  BUT test capacity to pay, competing supply, and the flood overlay
```

<summary>✅ Checkpoint 1 — site and demographics</summary>

**Q1. Define demographics in one sentence and list eight indicators.**

The study of a population by its characteristics, used by governments,
corporations and NGOs for policy development and market research. Eight
indicators: population and population growth; age (median age); gender; income
(median household income); employment/unemployment and industry; education
level and qualifications; country of origin, migration and language; family
and household structure. (Also: tenure — rent or mortgage — number of
bedrooms, how you travel to work, number of vehicles, Aboriginal and Torres
Strait Islander status.)

**Q2. Name two demographic data sources and say what distinguishes them.**

The **ABS Census** — a cross-sectional count of everyone in Australia on one
night plus their dwellings, taken every five years, last on 10 August 2021.
**HILDA** — a longitudinal panel following 17,000+ Australians over their
lifetime, annually. The Census describes the population at a point in time;
HILDA tracks the same individuals through time.

**Q3. A report quotes a suburb's median income as $2,155 and compares it to the state median. What should you check first?**

The **geographic level**. $2,155 is Brisbane Inner City at SA4; the same
"Brisbane" is $2,068 at LGA and $1,849 at GCCSA — a 16.5% spread. Confirm
which boundary the figure is on, and compare like with like.

**Q4. What is the difference between greenfield and brownfield, and which is East Brisbane?**

Greenfield is new development on undeveloped land — master-planned
communities, house and land. Brownfield is redevelopment or refurbishment of
already-developed land — urban renewal, higher density, old or heritage
housing stock. 26 Hampton Street is **brownfield**: an existing 822 m²
office/warehouse on 1,395 m² of inner-city land.

**Q5. Why does the ABS population clock matter more than the Census for a 2026 market analysis?**

Because the Census is five years stale (August 2021) and lags fast-changing
areas, as the Pallara imagery shows. The clock and the ERP series are current;
they also decompose growth into natural increase and net migration, and
migration is contributing roughly 907 of the ~1,137 people added each day —
the component that actually drives household formation.

**Q6. State the two halves of "is this the right demographic?"**

Whether the segment exists in sufficient numbers (identification), and
whether it has the **ability to buy or rent** at the price point you are
proposing (capacity). Also: what you are competing against, and whether you
are pioneering.

## 4.12 Topic 4 Summary

- Demographics tells you **current and future demand**; you study it so your
  **supply matches the demand**. Once you know *who*, you know *where* and
  *what* to build.
- Segment the population, then test the segment for **ability to pay** and for
  **competing supply** — identification alone is half an answer.
- **Site analysis** = site visit, physical characteristics, town planning,
  heritage, suitability tested against all four sectors. **Suburb
  description** = historical, current, transforming — and **use urban growth
  theories to justify the history**.
- **Population growth = natural increase + net migration**; Queensland adds
  **net interstate**. Net migration supplies roughly three-quarters of
  Australia's daily increase.
- **Census**: everyone in Australia on one night plus their dwellings; last
  10 August 2021; ~10 million dwellings, ~24.7 million people. Census *count*
  ≠ estimated resident *population*.
- **Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander population 30 June 2021: 983,700 =
  3.8%**; 33.1% under 15; median age 24.0; 74.5% in NSW/Qld/WA.
- **65+ rose from 12.4% (2000) to 16.3% (2020)**; the IGR 2023 projects 65+ to
  more than double and 85+ to more than triple over 40 years, on the three
  drivers **population, participation, productivity**. The ageing population
  is the **fastest-growing residential demographic**.
- **HILDA** = longitudinal, 17,000+, lifetime, unique in Australia.
- **The geography trap is the unit's signature error.** SA4 ≠ LGA ≠ GCCSA.
  Name the level beside every number and compare like with like.
