# TOPIC 7 — Cultural Heritage

Week 12, plus the Week 12 tutorial. The lecturer flagged this section
repeatedly for **Section A** and possibly Section B: what cultural heritage
is, the Indigenous and non-Indigenous perspectives, how Australia's heritage
is managed, the **Aboriginal Cultural Heritage Act 2003**, and the **Burra

## 7.1 What cultural heritage is

Two definitions, and the exam can quote either.

**UNESCO** (2009 *Framework for Cultural Statistics*):

> Cultural heritage is the heritage of **tangible and intangible heritage
> assets** of a group or society that is **inherited from past generations**.
> **Not all heritages of past generations are "heritage"; rather, heritage is
> a product of selection by society.**
>
> It includes artefacts, monuments, a group of buildings and sites, and
> museums that have a diversity of values including symbolic, historic,
> artistic, aesthetic, ethnological or anthropological, scientific and social
> significance. It includes tangible heritage (movable, immobile and
> underwater), intangible cultural heritage (ICH) embedded into cultural, and
> natural heritage artefacts, sites or monuments. It covers industrial
> heritage and cave paintings.

**ICOMOS** (International Council on Monuments and Sites, 2002):

> Cultural Heritage is an expression of the **ways of living developed by a
> community and passed on from generation to generation**, including customs,
> practices, places, objects, artistic expressions and values.

### The three types

| Type | Contents |
|---|---|
| **Tangible** | Buildings, monuments, landscapes, archive materials, books, works of art, artefacts. *(UNESCO further splits movable, immobile and underwater.)* |
| **Intangible** | Folklore, traditions, language, knowledge |
| **Natural** | Culturally significant landscapes, biodiversity |

ICOMOS's alternative cut, by environment:

| | Contents |
|---|---|
| **Built environment** | Buildings, townscapes, archaeological remains |
| **Natural environment** | Rural landscapes, coasts and shorelines, agricultural heritage |
| **Artefacts** | Books and documents, objects, pictures |

> **"Heritage is a product of selection by society" is the sentence that
> unlocks the whole topic.** Not everything old is heritage. Something becomes
> heritage because a society *decides* it has value — which is why the
> registers exist, why they are political, and why the pros-and-cons section
> below is a genuine argument rather than a formality.

## 7.2 The two perspectives

Lecture 13 lists *"cultural heritage from both Indigenous and non-Indigenous
perspectives"* as examinable for Section A and possibly B.

| | Content |
|---|---|
| **Indigenous / First Nations** | Significance arising from Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander tradition, history and occupation. Protected under **separate legislation** from the general heritage regime. |
| **Non-Indigenous** | For example Crown land (British Crown), and state- or council-level historical significance not related to First Nations people. |

> **The two streams are legally separate in Queensland, and that separation is
> the examinable fact.** The Queensland Heritage Register **does not include**
> places of Aboriginal or Torres Strait Islander cultural heritage unless the
> place has *shared* heritage values (the Glass House Mountains being the
> example given). Indigenous heritage is managed and protected under its own
> Acts, by a different department. Two regimes, not one.

## 7.3 Acknowledgement of Country versus Welcome to Country

| | **Acknowledgement of Country** | **Welcome to Country** |
|---|---|---|
| Who delivers it | **Can be by a non-Indigenous person** — anyone | **Only Traditional Owners/Custodians of the land** on which the event takes place |
| What it is | A way of showing awareness and respect for the Traditional Custodians of the land on which we gather | **A ceremony performed by an Aboriginal elder local to the land**, to acknowledge and consent to the gathering taking place |

*(Source: Reconciliation Australia, via the Week 12 tutorial.)*

> **The distinction is who has the standing to give it, and it is the first
> item on the Lecture 13 cultural heritage checklist.** Anyone may
> *acknowledge*; only a Traditional Owner of *that specific country* may
> *welcome*. The Welcome also carries something the Acknowledgement does not —
> **consent** to the gathering. A one-line MCQ, and free marks.

## 7.4 How Australia's heritage is managed — four levels

Lecture 13 lists this explicitly: *"how is Australia's heritage managed — make
sure you know that for your Section A."*

```
WORLD      →  UNESCO World Heritage List
NATIONAL   →  Australia's National Heritage List
STATE      →  Queensland Heritage Register (Queensland Heritage Act 1992)
LOCAL      →  Local government heritage register / local planning scheme
```

### World Heritage

- Australia has **20 World Heritage Sites**
- **Queensland has 5** of them
- The most recently recognised are the **Australian Convict Sites (2010)**,
  **Ningaloo Coast (2011)** and **Budj Bim Cultural Landscape (2019)**
- World Heritage sites are classified as **Natural and/or Cultural**

#### Budj Bim — why it is special

- Declared a World Heritage Site in **July 2019**, located in **Victoria**
- **The first place in Australia to receive international recognition solely
  for its Aboriginal cultural values**
- Contains one of the world's most extensive and **oldest aquaculture
  systems** — the Gunditjmara first constructed these systems **at least
  6,600 years ago** along the Budj Bim lava flow, and many are **still in use
  today**
- Engineered to **trap, store and harvest kooyang — short-finned eel**
- Gunditjmara knowledge and practices have endured and continue to be passed
  down through their Elders

> **"Solely for its Aboriginal cultural values" is the examinable phrase.**
> Most Australian World Heritage listings rely heavily on *natural* criteria,
> with Aboriginal heritage significant for several of them but not the basis
> of the listing. Budj Bim is the first that stands on cultural values alone —
> and at 6,600 years it predates the Egyptian pyramids.

### National Heritage

- **120 places** on the National Heritage List, of which **13 are in
  Queensland**

Queensland examples given:

| Place | Location | Type |
|---|---|---|
| Quinkan Country | Laura, north Qld | Indigenous |
| Australian Fossil Mammal Sites, Riversleigh | western Qld | Natural |
| Dinosaur Stampede National Monument | Winton–Jundah Road | Natural |
| Fraser Island | southern Qld coast | Natural |
| Glass House Mountains National Landscape | south east Qld | **Natural, Indigenous and historic** |
| Great Artesian Basin Springs | western Qld | Natural |
| Great Barrier Reef | — | Natural |
| Ngarrabullgan | north Qld | Indigenous |
| QANTAS Hangar, Longreach | western Qld | Historic |
| Tree of Knowledge, Barcaldine | western Qld | Historic |
| Wet Tropics of Queensland | north Qld | Natural |
| Gondwana Rainforests of Australia | south east Qld | Natural |
| The Dig Tree and Fort Wills Site | south west Qld | Historic |

The deck's own caveat: *the places currently listed do not necessarily
represent the full extent of Queensland's natural, historic and Indigenous
places that may meet the criteria.*

### State Heritage — Queensland

- Queensland's heritage is protected by the **Queensland Heritage Act 1992**
- Administered by the **Queensland Government**
- The register is maintained and managed by the **Department of Environment
  and Heritage Protection**
- There are **1,992+ places** entered in the Queensland Heritage Register,
  including **48 places added in 2018–2020**
- **Queensland's Indigenous cultural heritage is protected under specific,
  separate legislation** — the Aboriginal Cultural Heritage Act 2003

Types of place on the register: sites of public recreation, parks and gardens
· rural homesteads, suburban houses, flats, caravan parks · community halls,
theatres, picture theatres, showgrounds · churches, places of worship, sites
of public commemoration, burial places · commercial buildings · government
buildings including schools, police stations, courthouses, post offices ·
factories, industrial sites, mining sites · roads, bridges, railways, railway
infrastructure.

> **Memory hook: the Act is 1992 and the register holds 1,992+ places.**
> Coincidence, but it makes the year stick — and the year is the examinable
> part. Do not confuse **Queensland Heritage Act 1992** (non-Indigenous) with
> **Aboriginal Cultural Heritage Act 2003** (Indigenous). Different years,
> different Acts, different departments.

### Local and regional heritage

Places that are important **at a local level** but which do not necessarily
meet the State Heritage criteria. Such properties are required to be
recognised on a **local government's heritage register** or identified in the
**local planning scheme** — Brisbane City Council's heritage register is the
example.

> **Local heritage is the level that will actually constrain a development,
> and it is the one students forget.** A site does not need to be on the
> Queensland or National register to carry a heritage overlay in the
> Brisbane City Plan. When Section C hands you a site, the background section
> should check **all four levels**, and the two that most often bite are
> **local overlay** and **Aboriginal cultural heritage**.

## 7.5 Indigenous cultural heritage legislation

Two Acts, both 2003:

- **Aboriginal Cultural Heritage Act 2003** (Queensland)
- **Torres Strait Islander Cultural Heritage Act 2003**

They provide effective **recognition, protection and conservation** of
Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander cultural heritage, defined as anything
that is:

1. **A significant Aboriginal or Torres Strait Islander area** in Queensland
   (tradition, history); **or**
2. **A significant Aboriginal or Torres Strait Islander object**; **or**
3. **Evidence, of archaeological or historic significance, of Aboriginal or
   Torres Strait Islander occupation** of an area of Queensland

**Sources of significance:** anthropological, biogeographical, historical and
archaeological information.

### The Cultural Heritage Database and Register

Its stated purposes:

- **Assemble information** about Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander
  cultural heritage in a **central and accessible location**
- Provide a **research and planning tool** to help Aboriginal and Torres
  Strait Islander parties, researchers and other persons **assess the cultural
  heritage values of particular areas**

> **Three limbs to the ACH Act definition — area, object, evidence — and
> "evidence" is the one that catches developers.** You do not need a
> recognised site or a visible object; **archaeological or historic evidence of
> occupation** is enough to engage the Act. That is why cultural heritage due
> diligence happens *before* you commit to a development envelope, not after.

## 7.6 The Burra Charter

> **The Burra Charter defines a place as significant if it possesses:**
>
> - **Aesthetic value**
> - **Historic value**
> - **Scientific value**
> - **Social value**

It is a set of principles adopted to create a **nationally accepted standard
for heritage conservation practice in Australia**. It was **last updated in
2013**.

> **Four values, and the test is disjunctive — any ONE is enough.** A place
> does not need all four. "Aesthetic, historic, scientific, social" is the
> highest-probability short-answer list in this topic; make it a four-word
> mnemonic and lock it in. Also lock in **2013** as the last update and
> **nationally accepted standard** as its status.

## 7.7 Cultural heritage mapping and language

Documentation happens on **local, State and Federal** levels — Brisbane City
Council City Plan 2014, the Cultural Heritage Database and Register Map, and
the Commonwealth department's map.

Other resources the tutorial covers:

- **AIATSIS Map of Indigenous Australia** — Aboriginal and Torres Strait
  Islander peoples have been living on the continent for millennia; they are
  **the world's oldest surviving culture**; numerous groups and languages
- **The 50 Words Project** (University of Melbourne) — an online resource that
  lets you hear 50 words in a range of First Nations languages; currently
  **66 languages**
- **Australia Post** campaign to include **Traditional Place names** when
  sending mail — using the AIATSIS map you can identify the traditional
  Indigenous place name for anywhere in Australia
- **NAIDOC Week 2020** theme: *"Always Was, Always Will Be"*

> **Language is cultural heritage, and the tutorial makes the point
> deliberately.** The three types are tangible, intangible and natural —
> language sits squarely in **intangible**. If an MCQ asks for an example of
> intangible cultural heritage, "language" is the answer the unit has in mind.

## 7.8 The pros and cons of heritage protection

This is the balanced-argument section, and the tutorial's learning outcome is
literally *"heritage protection has both pros and cons"*.

**For:**

- **Protects cultural heritage and could provide positive values**
- Benefits arising from **tourism**, and its positive (in some cases negative)
  impact on nearby property values
- **Heritage-listed properties show an increase in prices**
- **Penalties apply** when cultural heritage is destroyed or altered without
  permission
- There is **private interest and investment** in preserving cultural heritage
  — Lamb House in Kangaroo Point is the Brisbane example

**Against:**

- **Restricts housing supply**
- Despite their importance, and despite legislation, some cultural heritage
  **continues to be destroyed or heavily altered** — the deck's example is a
  French-built factory in Hanoi, built early last century, demolished for an
  11-storey hotel-office building in a contemporary international style

> **"Restricts housing supply" is the property-economics half of the argument,
> and it connects straight back to Note 01.** A heritage overlay is a
> **planning control** — it shifts the supply curve left by removing
> developable capacity, at the same time as it may shift demand right through
> amenity and tourism. Net effect on price is ambiguous and empirical, which
> is exactly what the Auckland study below measures.

## 7.9 The economics — the World Bank framing

Martin Rama, Director of World Development Report 2012, in *"Investing in the
Sense of Place"*:

> In a context of rapid urbanization, interventions to develop old cities
> emphasize infrastructure but often pay scant attention to the architecture
> of the buildings, or the social fabric associated with them.

His argument in sequence:

- Without strategic public intervention, old cities **drift into a haphazard
  mix of demolition, new construction and building upgrading**
- **Densification is welcome economically** but is often accompanied by the
  **displacement of the original population**, which is questionable socially
- Economic activity typically increases, sometimes substantially, but in the
  process these places **lose their distinctive traits and become less
  liveable**
- The inhabitants themselves **regret the loss of a sense of place** and the
  disappearance of the physical markers of their identity
- Development interventions emphasise water, sanitation and commuting — all
  commendable — but pay scant attention to architecture or social fabric, and
  their "livelihoods" components focus on **economic activity, not aesthetics
  or culture**
- They compensate original inhabitants for property taken over; **less
  frequently do they consider how to keep those inhabitants in place**
- There is an element of **irreversibility**: recovering what was lost is
  **enormously more expensive than preserving it**, and bringing back the
  original population is **simply not possible**

He also concedes the limit of the heritage approach: financing individual
landmark projects — the Lijiang, Luang Prabang, Hoi An type — *"can only be
marginally relevant when upgrading major cities and dealing with the
challenges of large-scale urbanization."*

> **Irreversibility is the economic argument for heritage protection, and it
> is the one to use in an essay.** It is not sentiment. It is that the option
> to preserve is destroyed the moment you demolish, and the replacement cost
> of the lost asset is asymmetric — enormously higher than preservation would
> have been, and in the case of a displaced community, infinite. Framing it as
> an **irreversible decision under uncertainty** is a genuinely economic
> argument, and it is a better answer than "old buildings are nice".

## 7.10 Does heritage listing pay? Two studies

### Tisdell and Wilson (2002) — World Heritage listing and tourism

*World Heritage Listing of Australian Natural Sites: Tourism Stimulus and Its
Economic Value*, published in **Economic Analysis and Policy**.

- There are **economic benefits arising from tourism**
- Most Australian properties rely heavily on **natural criteria** for their
  listings, although **Aboriginal heritage is significant for several**
- The model: World Heritage listing occurs at time `tr`. Visitor
  numbers follow path **ABC** in the absence of listing, but diverge along
  **BD** if listing occurs. **The gap between BD and BC is the increase in
  tourism demand attributable to listing.**
- As time passes, with sustained and increased marketing of World Heritage
  properties, visitor numbers can be expected to increase
- An **instantaneous jump** in visitors is also possible (part b of the
  figure) but is **less likely**, because it takes time for visitors to
  acquire information, plan visits and **save** for them — these visits are
  not inexpensive
- If an instantaneous increase were recorded, it is **more likely to come from
  domestic rather than foreign visitors**

International visitor data for World Heritage and non-World Heritage
properties between **1991 and 1999** were compiled using BTR data. The deck is
candid that *"although the data presented are incomplete, they are the only
data available"*, and that domestic visitor data were **not available as a
time series**.

> **The gradual path is the theoretically expected one, and the reasoning is
> examinable.** Demand responds slowly because visitors must **learn** about
> the listing, **plan** a trip and **save** for it. That lag is why a
> listing's economic benefit shows up over years, not months — and why any
> instantaneous jump is more plausibly domestic (shorter planning horizon,
> lower cost).

### Bade et al. (2020) — the Auckland price premium

Published in **Land Use Policy**. A study of the price premium of heritage
listing in the Auckland housing market.

**Method:**

- **226,286 sales transactions, 2006–2016**, extracted from the Valuation
  Rates Dataset compiled by Auckland Council
- Two heritage preservation policies with **different levels of restriction**:
  **Heritage Designated Areas (HA)** — building regulations are **strong** —
  and **Special Character Areas (SCA)** — **less restrictive**
- Controls for whether a house is in an HA, an SCA, or **both**; calculates
  distances from each house to the nearest HA or SCA; accounts for **heritage
  density** — the number of heritage places within 50, 100 or 200 m
- A **hedonic price model** is used

**Results:**

| Location | Effect on price |
|---|---|
| Within an **SCA** only (less restrictive) | **+4.3%** |
| Within **both** an HA and an SCA | **+7.91%** |
| Within an **HA** only (strong regulation) | **−9.6%** |

| Heritage feature or landmark within… | Premium |
|---|---|
| 50 m radius | **+1.7%** |
| 100 m radius | **+1.4%** |
| 200 m radius | **+0.5%** |

The conclusion: *"the associated externalities of heritage have a positive but
**localized** effect on the prices of properties."* Houses **exclusively**
within an HA, where building regulations are strong, show a negative average
effect of −9.6%.

The authors' own caveat: estimating the value of heritage is challenging —
there are **potential confounding effects** because other environmental and
urban amenities in Auckland also make areas appealing, attract higher-income
households and drive up prices.

> **The three effects do not add up, and that is the finding — not an error.**
>
> ```
> SCA only          +4.30%
> HA only           −9.60%
> naive sum         −5.30%      ←  what you would predict if effects were additive
> both, observed    +7.91%      ←  what actually happens
> ```
>
> Being in both is **better than either alone**, by a wide margin. The reading
> is that HA and SCA overlays mark *different things*: HA marks the regulatory
> burden, SCA marks the desirable streetscape. Where both apply, the amenity
> dominates the restriction. Where only the strong regulation applies, the
> restriction dominates. **Regulation is priced as a cost; character is priced
> as an amenity.**

> **The distance decay is the cleanest result in the study, and the easiest to
> reproduce.** +1.7% at 50 m, +1.4% at 100 m, +0.5% at 200 m — the benefit of
> being *near* heritage falls to almost nothing by 200 metres. Heritage is a
> **local** amenity. If an exam asks how far a heritage externality reaches,
> the answer is "roughly 200 metres, decaying steeply".

### Enforcement

Penalties are real. The deck cites a **massive fine for illegal demolition of
a historic home** by a Brisbane builder (news.com.au), and the Week 12
tutorial adds **Lamb House, Kangaroo Point** as the positive counterpart — an
iconic Brisbane home restored to its previous glory through private
investment.

## 7.11 Natural disasters and property values

**This half of Lecture 12 is *not* highlighted as examinable** on the
Lecture 13 slide — only "Cultural heritage" is. It is summarised briefly here
because flood and disaster risk recur in the site-analysis material and in the
Section C background section, and because the empirical results are
counter-intuitive.

### The framing question

> When do property **amenities** become **dis**amenities?

- Many people have a **strong preference for living close to green space**,
  most of which has considerable amenity value — Tyrväinen and Miettinen
  (2000) found **−5.9% for every 1 km increase in distance** from urban forest
- But there is the risk of **highly unpredictable bushfires**, which can turn
  green space into a disamenity
- **The majority of buildings affected by major bushfires in Australia have
  been located less than 100 metres from green space** (Chen and McAneney
  2010; Hughes and Steffen 2013)
- Residents **discount the possibility of bushfire risk while obtaining
  utility from green space**

> **That is the same 100-metre band that carries the heritage premium.** In
> Auckland, proximity within 50–100 m is worth +1.4 to +1.7%. In the bushfire
> literature, proximity within 100 m is where the buildings burn. **The same
> distance can be an amenity and a hazard**, and the market prices the amenity
> far more reliably than it prices the hazard.

### Bushfire in Australia

- Australia is **one of the top three bushfire-prone areas in the world**
  (BOM); many native plants are fire prone and very combustible
- Risk periods: **summer and autumn** for southern Australia; **spring and
  early summer** for NSW and southern Queensland; **winter and spring** for
  the Northern Territory (Geoscience Australia 2017)
- Between **1967 and 2013**, major Australian bushfires caused over **8,000
  injuries and 433 fatalities** — close to **50% of all deaths from major
  Australian natural disasters** in the period, excluding heatwaves (Ladds et
  al. 2017)
- The **2019/2020** bushfires burnt **12.6 million hectares** across three
  states and the ACT, with **33 fatalities**
- With rising temperatures and strong winds, frequency and intensity are
  expected to increase substantially; and with populations growing at the
  **rural–urban interface**, risk rises further

### Flood — and the counter-intuitive result

The Week 2 and Week 12 decks share the same dataset: average property price
changes across four periods (2008–2010, 2011–2013, 2014–2016, 2017–2019) for
flood-**affected** and **non-affected** properties across 22 Brisbane suburbs
— Anstead, Auchenflower, Coorparoo, Corinda, **East Brisbane**, Fortitude
Valley, Greenslopes, Hemmant, Indooroopilly, Kangaroo Point, Kenmore, Middle
Park, Moggill, Moorooka, Oxley, Riverhills, Taringa, Tingalpa, Toowong,
Westlake, Windsor, Yeronga.

**Prices rose in the affected areas.** The deck's potential explanations:

- Buyers **discount the possibility of another flood** — 2011 was called a
  "once in a lifetime flood"
- **Non-disclosure of damage by sellers**
- Houses have **adapted** to future floods
- Buyers are **aware of the issues but the utility is greater than the
  immediate risks**

### Insurance and policy

- Although **no region in Australia is uninsurable**, increasing costs are
  making a growing number of owners unwilling or unable to purchase flood or
  storm damage insurance
- The Insurance Council of Australia: *"there are around **230,000 properties**
  in Australia that face a **one-in-20, or 5 per cent, risk of a catastrophic
  flood each year**"*
- In **2022** alone, more than **300,000 disaster-related insurance claims**
  were lodged, costing **$7.28 billion** — about **$6 billion** of it for the
  major floods in south-east Queensland and northern NSW

```
$6bn of $7.28bn  =  82.4% of the 2022 national claim cost came from ONE flood event
```

**Policy taxonomy:**

| **Private** | **Public** |
|---|---|
| Insurance | Limited mitigation activities |
| **Disclosure laws** | |
| Building code | |

**Pope (2008)**, *Journal of Urban Economics*: a seller **disclosure**
requirement (for airport noise) **reduced property prices by 2.9%**, and his
conclusion is that **publicly available information may not be adequately
considered by all buyers**.

**The call to action** — what is being proposed: stop building new communities
in high-hazard risk zones · relocate those already living in hazard zones
(**buy-back schemes**) · build protective levees and dams · reduce fuel load
and implement fire management schemes.

CoreLogic's Eliza Owen, on differential impact:

> "When we looked at the housing market in the week of the Brisbane floods,
> we found that markets where you have high-end apartment buildings on the
> Brisbane River might have their prices far less impacted than low-lying
> detached housing in low socio-economic areas… with a tall, high-rise
> apartment building, only the car park or the basement is affected."

> **Pope (2008) explains why the flood-price puzzle exists.** If mandatory
> **disclosure** cuts prices by 2.9%, then the *absence* of disclosure leaves
> risk unpriced — and "non-disclosure of damage by sellers" is one of the
> Brisbane deck's own explanations. The market is not concluding that flood
> risk is costless; it is failing to observe it. **The efficient-markets
> assumption fails on information, exactly as Note 01 said property markets
> do.**

**Q1. Define cultural heritage and give an example of each of the three types.**

The tangible and intangible heritage of a group or society, inherited from
past generations — and heritage is a **product of selection by society**, not
simply anything old. **Tangible**: a building, monument or artefact.
**Intangible**: language, folklore, tradition, knowledge. **Natural**: a
culturally significant landscape, or biodiversity.

**Q2. Who may deliver an Acknowledgement of Country, and who may deliver a Welcome to Country?**

An Acknowledgement may be given by **anyone**, including a non-Indigenous
person, to show respect for the Traditional Custodians. A Welcome may be
delivered **only by a Traditional Owner/Custodian — an Aboriginal elder local
to that land** — and it also conveys consent to the gathering.

**Q3. Name the four levels at which Australian heritage is managed, with the instrument at each.**

World — UNESCO World Heritage List. National — Australia's National Heritage
List. State — the Queensland Heritage Register under the **Queensland
Heritage Act 1992**, administered by the Department of Environment and
Heritage Protection. Local — a local government heritage register or the local
planning scheme (e.g. Brisbane City Council).

**Q4. What does the Aboriginal Cultural Heritage Act 2003 protect?**

Anything that is (1) a significant Aboriginal area in Queensland by tradition
or history, (2) a significant Aboriginal object, or (3) **evidence of
archaeological or historic significance** of Aboriginal occupation of an area
of Queensland. Sources of significance: anthropological, biogeographical,
historical and archaeological.

**Q5. State the Burra Charter test and its status.**

A place is significant if it possesses **aesthetic, historic, scientific OR
social** value — any one suffices. The Burra Charter is the **nationally
accepted standard for heritage conservation practice in Australia**, last
updated **2013**.

**Q6. Which Act covers non-Indigenous Queensland heritage, and does the register include Aboriginal heritage?**

The **Queensland Heritage Act 1992**. The Queensland Heritage Register does
**not** include places of Aboriginal or Torres Strait Islander cultural
heritage unless the place has **shared** heritage values (e.g. the Glass House
Mountains); Indigenous heritage is managed separately under the ACH Act 2003.

**Q7. Auckland: a house sits inside a Heritage Designated Area only. What happens to its price, and how does that compare with a house in both an HA and an SCA?**

A house exclusively within an HA — where building regulations are strong —
shows an average price **reduction of 9.6%**. A house within **both** an HA
and an SCA shows a **premium of 7.91%**. The effects are not additive: the
character amenity outweighs the regulatory burden when both apply.

**Q8. Give the economic argument for heritage protection in one sentence.**

Demolition is **irreversible** and the cost of recovering what was lost is
enormously higher than the cost of preserving it — and a displaced community
cannot be brought back at all — so the option value of preservation exceeds
its holding cost.

## 7.12 Topic 7 Summary

- Cultural heritage is **tangible, intangible and natural**, inherited from
  past generations — and **heritage is a product of selection by society**.
  Definitions from **UNESCO (2009)** and **ICOMOS (2002)**.
- **Two perspectives — Indigenous and non-Indigenous — and in Queensland they

- **Acknowledgement of Country: anyone. Welcome to Country: only a Traditional

- **Four levels of management: World · National · State · Local.** Australia
  has **20 World Heritage sites** (Qld has 5) and **120 National Heritage

  **solely for Aboriginal cultural values**; aquaculture at least **6,600

- **Queensland Heritage Act 1992** — non-Indigenous, Department of Environment
  and Heritage Protection, **1,992+** places. **Aboriginal Cultural Heritage
  Act 2003** and **Torres Strait Islander Cultural Heritage Act 2003** —
  Indigenous, covering a significant **area**, a significant **object**, or
  **evidence** of occupation.
- **Burra Charter: aesthetic, historic, scientific, social.** Any one is
  enough. Nationally accepted standard, last updated **2013**.
- Heritage protection **protects value and attracts tourism** but **restricts

- **Bade et al. (2020), Auckland**: SCA only **+4.3%**; both HA and SCA
