# TOPIC 4 — Negligence, Misrepresentation and the ACL

Week 4 lecture, plus the pre-contractual statement material at the front of
the Week 5 lecture.

Topics 1 to 3 were about the deal: how a contract is formed, who the agent
acts for, what the seller must put in writing. This topic is about what
happens when someone **says something that is not true**, or **fails to take
care**, and a buyer relies on it. It is the law that fills the gap left by
*caveat emptor* — and the gap is smaller than it used to be, because the
buyer no longer has to prove a contract to sue.

## 4.1 What is a tort?

> A tort is a **civil wrong** — a breach of a legal duty owed to another
> person, where that person has suffered a loss.

- The person who commits the wrong is the **tortfeasor**. The person harmed
  is the **claimant** or **plaintiff**.
- The legal system **does not penalise all conduct that causes a loss**.
  Losses caused by lawful conduct that is not malicious are **not
  compensable**. Undercutting a competitor's rent is a loss; it is not a
  tort.
- Some conduct is **actionable *per se*** — the law regards it as so bad
  that a person whose rights have been breached can sue **even though there
  has been no loss or damage**, and even though the tortfeasor was **not
  aware** of the infringement. Trespass to land is the property example.

Torts are grouped by the interest they protect:

| Interest protected | Torts |
|---|---|
| Physical security and health | Battery, assault, **negligence** |
| Avoiding imprisonment | False imprisonment |
| Security and enjoyment of property (land, chattels, intangibles) | Defamation, conversion, **trespass** |
| Avoiding legal prosecution | Malicious prosecution |

The area moves as society's attitudes move — the lecture's example is the
David Jones sexual harassment claim of 2010, which was framed not as a
harassment case but as **misleading and deceptive conduct** under the then
*Trade Practices Act 1974* (Cth). The claim was that statements made at and
after the job interview — that the board was committed to "providing a safe
and healthy workplace", that the employer "did not tolerate harassment,
discrimination or bullying", that it was committed to its Code of Ethics and
Conduct — were representations the employee relied on in accepting the job.

> **The David Jones claim settled; it decided nothing.** It is on the slide
> as an illustration of how far the misleading-conduct provisions can be
> stretched, not as authority. Cite it as an *example of the reach of the
> statute*, never as a case that establishes a rule. The rule it illustrates
> is the one that matters for property: **a corporate statement made to
> induce someone into a transaction is conduct in trade or commerce**, and a
> glossy IM or a "quality assured" brochure is exactly that.

### Tort versus crime

| | Tort | Crime |
|---|---|---|
| Victim | An **individual** | The **whole community** |
| Aim | **Compensate** | **Punish** |
| Perpetrator called | **Tortfeasor** | **Offender** |
| Court | Civil | Criminal |
| Standard of proof | **Balance of probabilities** | **Beyond reasonable doubt** |
| Outcome | Compensation to the plaintiff | Conviction or acquittal |

The same act can be **both** — an assault is a tort and a crime, prosecuted
in one court and sued on in another.

### Tort versus contract

This comparison is worth more marks than it looks, because it decides
**who can sue** and **when the clock starts**.

| | Contract | Tort |
|---|---|---|
| Who can sue | **Only the parties** to the contract (privity) | A duty of care is owed to **the world at large** |
| Measure of damages | Put the party where they would have been **had the breach not occurred** | Put the party where they would have been **had the tort not occurred** |
| Right of action starts | From the **breach of contract** | From the **loss** |

> **Privity is why tort exists in property transactions at all.** The buyer
> has a contract with the seller — not with the seller's agent, not with the
> valuer, not with the building inspector the seller engaged. When the loss
> is caused by one of those people, contract gives the buyer nothing and
> **negligence is the only route**. Whenever a problem question puts a false
> statement in the mouth of somebody who is not a party to the contract,
> that is the signal to run the negligence elements, not the contract ones.

## 4.2 Donoghue v Stevenson

A friend bought a bottle of ginger beer for a woman. She drank some before
tipping the rest into a dish, only to find the decomposing remains of a
snail. She suffered severe nervous shock and later gastroenteritis.

The lecture asks two questions: **does she have a right to recover?** and
**from whom** — the regulator, the manufacturer, or the seller?

She had no contract with anyone. Her friend bought the drink, so privity
barred a contract claim against the café; she had never dealt with the
manufacturer at all. *Donoghue v Stevenson* [1932] AC 562 answered the
question by inventing the modern **duty of care**, owed by the manufacturer
to the ultimate consumer, and with it the **neighbour principle**: you owe a
duty to persons so closely and directly affected by your act that you ought
reasonably to have them in contemplation.

> **The answer to "who does she sue" is the manufacturer, and the reason is
> the reason.** Not the regulator — administering food standards is not
> proximate to this drinker. Not the seller — no contract with her, and the
> defect was not one a seller could detect in an opaque bottle. The
> manufacturer, because it alone had control of the product and knew it
> would reach a consumer with no opportunity of intermediate examination.
> Reproduce that chain of reasoning and you have already written the duty
> element of any negligence answer.

## 4.3 Negligence

> **Negligence: the absence of care where the law imposes a duty to take
> reasonable care of others.**

Three elements, all of which the plaintiff must prove. Miss one and the
action fails.

```
1. A duty of care was owed to the plaintiff
2. The defendant breached that duty
3. The plaintiff suffered damage as a result of the breach
```

### Element 1 — duty of care

The plaintiff must show:

1. the harm or injury was **foreseeable** — a **real possibility**, not
   far-fetched or fanciful; and
2. in the circumstances it was **not unreasonable to impose a duty** on the
   defendant to avoid causing that harm.

On foreseeability, the defendant **need not foresee the precise loss to a
specific person**. It is enough that **some loss** could have been suffered
by a **class of persons of whom the plaintiff was one**.

The duty is limited to those in a relationship of **proximity**, of which
there are three kinds:

| Proximity | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Physical** | Closeness in space between the persons and the property of plaintiff and defendant |
| **Circumstantial** | Arising out of the particular circumstances — an existing relationship such as adviser and advisee |
| **Causal** | The **closeness and directness** of the relationship between the defendant's conduct and the injury sustained |

Proximity is only one consideration. The court also asks:

- was there a **reasonably foreseeable risk of injury**?
- is the case **closely analogous** to cases where a duty is already
  established?
- if not, is the relationship of **neighbour** close enough to warrant
  finding a duty?
- are there **policy or other considerations** against imposing a duty?

> **"Reasonably foreseeable" in the duty element is a low bar, and students
> keep arguing it as if it were a high one.** The test is a **real
> possibility, not far-fetched or fanciful** — it is not "likely", and not
> "probable". Probability belongs to element 2, where the magnitude and
> likelihood of the danger set the **standard of care**. Argue likelihood
> under duty and you have spent your best material in the wrong paragraph.

### Element 2 — breach of the duty

The plaintiff must prove two distinct things:

| What must be proved | Character of the question |
|---|---|
| They were **owed a standard of care** | A **question of law** |
| The defendant **failed to meet that standard** | A **question of fact** |

**The standard of care** is how a **reasonable person** would respond to the
risk in these circumstances. Consider:

- the **probability and magnitude** of the danger;
- whether the risk of the event was **remote**;
- whether the activity was **dangerous**;
- a **heightened standard where children are involved**;
- the **likelihood** of the danger resulting;
- risk to persons with **known disabilities** must be taken into account;
- the **options open to the defendant**;
- having taken **all reasonable precautions**, the defendant is **not
  liable** if a person is nonetheless injured.

**Failure to meet the standard** is failure to do what a reasonable person
would have done in the circumstances. What is reasonable turns on:

- the risk associated with the defendant's conduct;
- whether the situation could be described as an **emergency**;
- the **practicality** of adopting preventative measures;
- whether the defendant **observed community standards**.

> **"Took all reasonable precautions" is a complete answer to element 2, and
> it is the defendant's best line in a property problem.** A managing agent
> who inspected quarterly, logged the defect, engaged a licensed trade and
> barricaded the area has met the standard even if a visitor is still hurt.
> Negligence is not strict liability — the question is never "was someone
> injured", it is "what would a reasonable agent have done, and did they do
> it".

### Element 3 — damage suffered as a result of the breach

There must be a **causal link** between breach and damage. The plaintiff
must prove three things:

1. the loss, damage or injury is **of a type the law is prepared to
   compensate**;
2. it was **caused by the defendant's negligence** —
   - the **'but for' test**: but for the defendant's conduct, would the loss
     have occurred?
   - the better test is whether the defendant's conduct **caused or
     materially contributed to** the loss;
3. it was **not too remote in law** to be recovered — the defendant is
   liable only for **reasonably foreseeable consequential damage**.

Two rules sit on top of remoteness:

- **Egg shell skull rule** — if the *injury* is reasonably foreseeable, the
  defendant is liable for the *consequences*, even where the plaintiff's
  unusual susceptibility makes them far worse than expected. You take your
  victim as you find them.
- ***Novus actus interveniens*** — a defence based on a **new intervening
  act**. The link between the defendant's conduct and the loss is broken;
  the original event is no longer the proximate cause.

> **The egg shell skull rule and *novus actus* pull in opposite directions
> and are tested together.** Both are about what happens *after* the breach.
> The egg shell skull rule says an unexpected **magnitude** of harm does not
> break the chain. *Novus actus* says an unexpected **intervening act** can.
> A weak building slab that collapses more expensively than anyone predicted
> is egg shell skull — the defendant pays. A third party who cuts through
> the slab after the inspection is *novus actus* — they may not.

### Defences to negligence

| Defence | Effect |
|---|---|
| The plaintiff has **not made out the elements** | Complete answer — the action fails |
| **Contributory negligence** — the plaintiff is partly responsible for their own loss, having failed to exercise care for themselves | Damages **reduced proportionally** |
| ***Volenti non fit injuria*** — "the willing cannot be injured"; the plaintiff **assumed the risk** | A **complete** answer to a negligence action |

> **Contributory negligence reduces; *volenti* extinguishes.** The examinable
> difference is the remedy, not the description. If the facts let you argue
> the plaintiff knew of and accepted the specific risk, argue *volenti*
> first — it wins the whole case — and plead contributory negligence in the
> alternative.

## 4.4 Negligent misstatement

The Week 4 lecture treats this as the property-specific application of
negligence, because it is the one real estate agents, valuers and building
consultants get sued under.

**Same elements as a general action in negligence.** The live issue is
almost always **whether a duty of care was owed** in respect of *words*.
Ask:

1. Was the advice given in respect of a **serious or business matter**?
2. Were the circumstances such that the adviser **should have realised the
   person being advised was trusting the opinion and intending to act on
   it**?
3. In the circumstances, was it **reasonable to have relied** on the advice
   — or would a solicitor normally have been engaged to advise properly?

> In summary: was there an **assumption of responsibility** by the defendant
> and **reliance** by the plaintiff?

> **Limb 3 is the agent's escape hatch and the buyer's trap.** If the matter
> is one on which a buyer would ordinarily engage a solicitor or a valuer,
> reliance on the agent's off-the-cuff remark is **not reasonable**, and no
> duty arises. That is why "you should get your own legal advice" appears at
> the bottom of every brochure — it is aimed squarely at limb 3.

### Butcher v Lachlan Elder Realty

The lecture poses two questions:

1. Did the agents engage in misleading and deceptive conduct when they
   distributed sales brochures containing untrue or misleading statements
   **provided by a third party**?
2. **Can real estate agents rely on disclaimer statements to escape
   liability?**

The facts: an agent's brochure for a waterfront property reproduced a survey
diagram prepared by a surveyor, which wrongly showed the position of the
swimming pool relative to the boundary. The brochure carried a disclaimer
that the agent had not checked the accuracy of the information and passed no
warranty on it.

The High Court held, by a narrow majority (*Butcher v Lachlan Elder Realty
Pty Ltd* (2004) 218 CLR 592), that the **agent was not liable**. To the
reasonable purchaser in that position — buying an expensive property with
their own solicitor engaged — the agent was **merely passing on information
from a third party** and was disclaiming any belief in its truth. The
conduct of the agent, read as a whole, was not misleading.

> **Butcher is not authority that a disclaimer always works, and writing
> that will cost you the question.** The majority looked at the **whole
> conduct in context**: sophisticated buyers, a professional adviser
> engaged, a clearly identified third-party source, and a disclaimer they
> could read. Change those facts — an unsophisticated buyer, no solicitor, a
> statement the agent presents as their own — and the same disclaimer will
> not save the agent. Compare *Demagogue v Ramensky* in 4.6: where there is
> a **reasonable expectation of disclosure**, even **silence** is conduct.

## 4.5 Pre-contractual statements and advertising

The Week 5 lecture puts three bodies of law over the same facts:

```
Australian Consumer Law (statute)                 → ACL s 18, ss 20–22, ss 254–258
Innocent, negligent and fraudulent
  misrepresentation (common law / equity)         → rescission, damages, tort
Contract terms (express and implied)              → breach of contract
```

**Advertising** is generally **mere puff** or a statement of opinion — "the
best position on the river" commits no one. The problem arises when
advertising includes a **statement of fact**. That can be:

- a **misrepresentation** at common law (fraudulent, negligent or innocent —
  see Topic 1, §1.4); **or**
- a breach of the **Australian Consumer Law**, ss 18 and 19.

> **Section 19 is not a second prohibition — it is a carve-out, and the
> slide's "sections 18 and 19" hides that.** Section 18 is the operative
> prohibition. **Section 19 restricts the application of s 18 to
> information providers** — broadcasters and publishers who carry someone
> else's material. Cite **s 18** for the conduct; mention s 19 only if the
> defendant is a media outlet republishing an advertisement. Citing "ss 18
> and 19" as the source of the prohibition reads as if you have not opened
> the Act.

| Type of misrepresentation | Test | Primary remedy |
|---|---|---|
| **Fraudulent** | Made knowingly, without belief in its truth, or recklessly | Avoid the contract; damages in deceit |
| **Negligent** | Duty of care breached — the 4.4 questions | Damages in tort (negligent misstatement) |
| **Innocent** | Untrue but honestly believed | Rescission |

All three can also be **misleading or deceptive conduct** under s 18, which
requires **no fault at all**. That is why s 18 has swallowed so much of this
area.

## 4.6 The Australian Consumer Law

**What it is.** A **single, national consumer law**, replacing provisions in
**20 national, State and Territory consumer laws**. It is **Schedule 2 to
the *Competition and Consumer Act 2010* (Cth)**, which is the renamed
*Trade Practices Act 1974* (Cth).

> **Two citation errors from the slides will cost you marks in a quiz.**
> First, the Week 4 framework slide calls it the "Competition and Consumer
> **Law** 2010" — the Act's short title is the **Competition and Consumer
> *Act* 2010 (Cth)**. Second, several slides and the Week 1 deck still refer
> to **s 52 TPA**; the successor provision is **ACL s 18**. Write:
> *Competition and Consumer Act 2010* (Cth) Sch 2 (the Australian Consumer
> Law), s 18.

**Who enforces it.**

| Regulator | Scope |
|---|---|
| **ACCC** | National enforcement and administration |
| Each **State and Territory consumer agency** | State enforcement — in Queensland, the **Office of Fair Trading** |
| **ASIC** | **Financial services** |

**What it covers.**

- a national **unfair contract terms** law covering **standard form
  contracts**;
- a national law **guaranteeing consumer rights** when buying goods and
  services, replacing the old conditions-and-warranties laws;
- a national **product safety** law and enforcement system;
- a national law for **unsolicited consumer agreements**, replacing State
  and Territory door-to-door and direct marketing laws;
- simple national rules for **lay-by agreements**;
- new **penalties, enforcement powers and consumer redress**.

**The two elements of consumer protection legislation:**

1. **Conduct during the consumer transaction** — advertising,
   pre-contractual negotiations, and the contract itself; and
2. **Anti-competitive behaviour**.

### Section 18 — misleading or deceptive conduct

> **18 Misleading or deceptive conduct**
> (1) A person must not, in trade or commerce, engage in conduct that is
> misleading or deceptive or is likely to mislead or deceive.
> (2) Nothing in Part 3-1 (which is about unfair practices) limits by
> implication subsection (1).
> *Note: For rules relating to representations as to the country of origin
> of goods, see Part 5-3.*

Four things to notice in that text:

```
"A person"                    → not just corporations. Agents personally.
"in trade or commerce"        → excludes purely private, one-off dealings
"conduct"                     → includes silence; includes passing on
                                 someone else's statement
"or is likely to mislead"     → no one need actually have been misled
```

**Silence can be conduct.** Where there is an obligation to correct an
untruth and the other party is relying on the information, silence can
breach s 18 — ***Demagogue Pty Ltd v Ramensky***: failure to disclose
something that ought to be disclosed can constitute misleading and deceptive
conduct. The test is whether, in the circumstances, there was a **reasonable
expectation of disclosure**.

> **Section 18 carries no criminal liability and no pecuniary penalty — the
> remedies are private.** The lecturer's own note on the tort slide says so:
> "no criminal liability for a breach of s 52". A plaintiff sues for
> **damages, injunctions and compensatory orders**. Contrast unconscionable
> conduct (civil penalties, no criminal action) and country-of-origin claims
> (criminal **and** civil). A quiz option offering "criminal penalties for
> misleading conduct" is wrong.

### Unconscionable conduct — ACL Part 2-2, ss 20–22

> Behaviour that **takes advantage of someone's particular disadvantage**,
> generally in a contractual setting, and goes against good conscience or
> society's norms. An action **in equity**.

- It must be **beyond stiff commercial negotiation** — something society
  would consider **genuinely unreasonable or oppressive**.
- Examples: **not explaining the terms of a contract to someone who does not
  speak English**; **pressure tactics against the elderly or infirm**.
- A business must not act unconscionably when **selling or supplying goods
  and services to a consumer**, or **supplying or acquiring goods and
  services to or from a business**.
- **Civil penalties apply — no criminal action.**

The equitable doctrine is in Topic 1, §1.4; the statutory version in Part 2-2
covers the same ground with a wider reach (it protects businesses too, and
does not require proof of a "special disadvantage" in the strict equitable
sense under s 21).

### Country of origin — ACL ss 254–258

Businesses must not make **false or misleading representations about country
of origin**: "made in", "product of", "produce of", "produced in", use of a
specified logo, or claims that goods were **grown in** a country. The ACL
sets criteria businesses must follow. **Penalties are criminal and civil.**

### Restrictive trade practices

Contracts, arrangements or understandings that **restrict dealings or affect
competition**:

- arrangements in relation to **prices** (price fixing);
- **misuse of market power**;
- **exclusive dealing**;
- **resale price maintenance**;
- **acquisitions that would lessen competition**.

> **These live in Part IV of the *Competition and Consumer Act*, not in the
> ACL.** The slide says "contained in Part IV of the Act" and it means the
> Act itself, not Schedule 2. The ACL (Sch 2) is consumer protection; Part
> IV is competition. Putting price fixing "in the ACL" mixes up the two
> halves of the statute.

**Consumer law as a corporate weapon.** Businesses sue each other under
these provisions more often than consumers do — the lecture lists *Colgate
Palmolive Pty Ltd v Rexona Pty Ltd*, *Wills Pty Ltd v Philip Morris*, and
*CA Henschke v Rosemount Estates Pty Ltd* (a wine-labelling dispute). The
statute is steadily **replacing common law actions** such as
misrepresentation and passing off, because it needs no contract, no fault
and no proof that anyone was actually deceived.

## 4.7 Stigmatised property and caveat emptor

**In Australia there is no definition of a stigmatised property.** The
lecture's categories:

| Category | What it is |
|---|---|
| **Public / history stigma** | Notoriety attached to the property's past |
| **Criminal stigma** | The site of criminal activity |
| **Murder / suicide stigma** | A death on the premises |
| **Phenomena stigma** | Claimed hauntings and paranormal activity |
| **Health stigma** | Features that create perceived health risks |
| **Off-site stigma** | Something nearby — a waste dump, a tower |
| **Minimal stigma** | Slight, transient effect on value |

### The cases

| Case | Stigma | Held |
|---|---|---|
| *Reed v King* 145 Cal App 3d 261 | History — a **murder** | Failure to disclose was **material to the contract** |
| *Stambovsky v Ackley* 169 AD 2d 254 | Phenomena — the house was reputed **haunted** | Plaintiff **successfully rescinded** the contract |
| *Strawn v Canuso* 657 A 2d 420 | **Off-site** — new houses built near a **hazardous waste dump** | Disclosure required |
| *Hinton & Ors v Commissioner for Fair Trading* [2006] NSWADT 257 | Murder — 6 Collins Street, North Ryde, where Sef Gonzales killed his sister and parents | The leading **Australian** stigma matter |

The first three are **American** — Californian, New York and New Jersey
decisions. They are **persuasive at best** in a Queensland court (Topic 1,
§1.1). *Hinton* is the Australian one, and it is a **New South Wales
administrative tribunal** decision, so it does not bind Queensland either.

In *Hinton*, Mr Kwok and Ms Lin contracted to buy the North Ryde house.
Soon after paying the deposit they discovered the truth about the property.

**Amityville** is the lecture's illustration of how strange the economics
are: 112 Ocean Avenue, the site of a mass murder, with a claim the house was
haunted and responsible for it. The house **traded one year after the
murders**; the new owners reported paranormal activity, which produced the
book and the film. The address was later **changed to 108 Ocean Avenue** to
deter tourists, and one family then occupied it for a long time with no
issues.

### The Queensland position

- ***Caveat emptor*** — Latin for **buyer beware**. First applied to real
  estate transactions in England in the **16th century**.
- The **onus is on the purchaser** to do all the necessary searches to
  ensure the property is as they believe it is.
- The problem is **latent defects** — those the buyer could not appreciate
  through a physical inspection.
- *Caveat emptor* must now be **balanced against the disclosure requirements
  of the *Property Law Act 2023*** (Topic 3, §3.1).
- **There is no specific requirement in the POA to disclose the history of a
  property.** Instead, **consumer protection legislation is used**, and it
  is a **question of fact** whether silence constitutes a breach.

> **The two-step answer for any stigma question in Queensland.**
> **Step 1 — disclosure statute:** PLA 2023 s 99 lists what must be
> disclosed, and a murder, a suicide or a haunting is **not on the list**.
> The POA imposes nothing either. So there is **no statutory duty to
> disclose the history**, and the buyer cannot terminate under s 104 for
> non-disclosure of it. **Step 2 — consumer protection:** the buyer's real
> argument is **ACL s 18**, and it only gets up if silence in the
> circumstances created a **reasonable expectation of disclosure**
> (*Demagogue*) — usually because a direct question was asked, or a half
> truth was told. Answer step 1 only and you have written half the answer.

## Summary

- A **tort** is a civil wrong; the **tortfeasor** compensates the
  **plaintiff** on the **balance of probabilities**. Some torts are
  **actionable *per se***.
- Contract binds **only the parties** and runs from the **breach**; tort is
  owed to the **world at large** and runs from the **loss**. Privity is why
  a buyer sues an agent in **tort**, not contract.
- *Donoghue v Stevenson* [1932] AC 562 created the **duty of care**.
- Negligence has **three elements**: duty, breach, damage. Duty needs
  **foreseeability** (a real possibility, not far-fetched) plus
  **proximity** — physical, circumstantial or causal. Breach is measured
  against the **reasonable person**. Damage needs **causation** ('but for',
  or material contribution) and must **not be too remote**.
- **Egg shell skull**: unexpected magnitude does not break the chain.
  ***Novus actus interveniens***: an intervening act can.
- Defences: elements not made out; **contributory negligence** (reduces
  damages); ***volenti*** (defeats the claim).
- **Negligent misstatement** turns on **assumption of responsibility** plus
  **reasonable reliance**. *Butcher v Lachlan Elder Realty* shows a
  disclaimer can work — **in context**, for a sophisticated buyer with their
  own adviser.
- **ACL s 18**: a *person*, in *trade or commerce*, must not engage in
  conduct that is misleading, deceptive, **or likely** to mislead. **Silence
  counts** where there is a reasonable expectation of disclosure —
  *Demagogue v Ramensky*. **No criminal penalty**.
- **Unconscionable conduct**: Part 2-2, ss 20–22, **civil penalties only**.
  **Country of origin**: ss 254–258, **criminal and civil**. **Restrictive
  trade practices**: **Part IV of the CCA**, not the ACL.
- **Stigmatised property**: no Australian definition, **no PLA or POA duty
