# Practice Questions

Exam-style questions across Topics 1 to 4 and the Weeks 2–6 tutorials —
the scope of the Week 7 quiz. Answers are hidden: work the question first,
then tap.

These are **not** past papers. They are written to the same shapes the unit
uses: true/false and multiple choice for the rules, short answer for the
distinctions people blur, and two full **ISAAC** problems.

## Part A — Multiple choice and true/false

**A1.** Under a **sole** agency appointment, the seller finds the buyer
herself through an old work colleague and sells without the agent's
involvement. Is the agent entitled to commission?

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**No.** Section 23(3) POA: under a sole agency the agent is **not** entitled
where the **seller was the effective cause of the sale**. This is **case 4**
in the statutory example. Under an **exclusive** agency on identical facts
the agent **would** be paid — s 23(2).

</details>

**A2.** A property is knocked down at auction $35,000 below the vendor's
reserve because the auctioneer misheard the bid. Which is correct?

- (a) The sale is void; the property did not sell
- (b) The sale stands and the vendor bears the $35,000
- (c) The sale stands, and the auctioneer is liable to the vendor for
  $35,000
- (d) The sale stands only if the vendor signs the contract

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**(c).** The auctioneer has **no authority** to sell below reserve, but if
they do they **bind their principal** — the contract formed at the **fall of
the hammer** and the buyer keeps it. The auctioneer is then **liable to the
principal for the amount below the reserve**, here $35,000. Option (d) is
wrong: the contract is not conditional on signing or on payment of the
deposit, and the auctioneer may sign on the purchaser's behalf.

</details>

**A3.** A buyer pays a **$150,000 deposit** on a completed house priced at
**$1,250,000**. Is this an instalment contract?

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**Yes.**

```
Purchase price                       $1,250,000
10% threshold      0.10 × 1,250,000 =  $125,000
Deposit paid                           $150,000   ← $25,000 over the line
Deposit as a % of price   150,000 ÷ 1,250,000 = 12.0%
```

A deposit **greater than 10%** of the contract sum converts the contract
into an **instalment contract**. The 20% threshold would only apply if this
were a **proposed lot**. On a proposed lot at the same price the line would
be $250,000 and a $150,000 deposit would be fine.

</details>

**A4.** True or false: the *Property Law Act 2023* (Qld) s 99 disclosure
statement tells a buyer whether the property has flooded and whether the
building is structurally sound.

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**False**, and it is false twice over. The statement carries express
**warnings** that there is **no warranty as to the structural integrity of
improvements**, and it **highlights information that is not included** —
**flooding history**, **structural soundness**, and **current or past
building approvals or zoning limits**. Those are the three gaps due
diligence exists to fill.

</details>

**A5.** Which of these attracts **criminal** penalties?

- (a) Misleading or deceptive conduct — ACL s 18
- (b) Unconscionable conduct — ACL Part 2-2
- (c) False representations about country of origin — ACL ss 254–258
- (d) All of the above

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**(c) only.** Section 18 carries **no criminal liability and no pecuniary
penalty** — remedies are damages, injunctions and compensatory orders.
Unconscionable conduct attracts **civil penalties, no criminal action**.
Country of origin representations attract **criminal and civil** penalties.

</details>

**A6.** An exclusive agency appointment is signed on **1 August 2025**. What
are the two dates that matter?

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

```
Appointment signed        Friday    1 August 2025
Seller may terminate      after 60 days  →  Tuesday 30 September 2025
Maximum term ends         after 90 days  →  Thursday 30 October 2025
```

The seller is **locked in for 60 days** and then has a **30-day terminable
tail**. The POA raised the maximum term from 60 days to **90**, and added
the right to terminate after **60**. A question asking "how long is the
seller committed?" wants **60**; one asking "how long can the appointment
run?" wants **90**.

</details>

**A7.** A commercial office building has a **net lettable area of 940 m²**.
The owner advertises it for lease without an energy rating. Breach?

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**No.** The Commercial Building Disclosure obligation applies to commercial
office space of **1,000 m² or more** of net lettable area. At 940 m² the
building is **60 m² under the threshold** and no BEEC is required. Watch for
questions that give an area just over the line — 1,050 m² is caught, and
then **all advertisements** must carry the energy rating.

</details>

**A8.** An agent shows a buyer through a house. The buyer says, "the block
next door is council parkland, isn't it — it can never be built on." The
agent knows a development approval was granted last month and says nothing.
Which is the buyer's strongest action?

- (a) Termination under PLA s 104 for defective disclosure
- (b) ACL s 18 — misleading or deceptive conduct by silence
- (c) Breach of an implied condition under PLA s 76
- (d) Negligence — the agent owed a duty to research the neighbourhood

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**(b).** *Demagogue v Ramensky*: failure to disclose something that ought to
be disclosed can be misleading and deceptive conduct, where there is a
**reasonable expectation of disclosure** — here, a buyer's false statement
left uncorrected by an agent who knew the truth and who could see the buyer
was relying on it. (a) fails because the adjoining lot's DA is not on the
s 99 list. (c) is about **title and encumbrances**, not neighbours. (d) is
not hopeless but is much weaker: the buyer would have to establish a duty
in respect of words and **reasonable reliance** — see *Butcher*.

</details>

**A9.** True or false: an agency relationship can only be created by a
written agreement.

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**False.** Agency can arise **five** ways: express agreement, implied from
conduct, **estoppel** (holding out), **ratification**, and **necessity**.
Note the separate point that a real estate agent who wants to **sue for
commission** needs a **signed appointment in the approved form (Form 6)** —
that is a statutory precondition to recovery, not a rule about how agency
comes into existence.

</details>

**A10.** Damages in contract and damages in tort. Which statement is right?

- (a) Both put the plaintiff where they would have been had the wrong not
  occurred
- (b) Contract restores the pre-contract position; tort compensates
  expectation
- (c) Contract compensates expectation; tort restores the position had the
  tort not occurred
- (d) Both are assessed at the date of trial

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**(c).** Contract puts the innocent party where they would have been **had
the contract been performed**. Tort puts them where they would have been
**had the tort not occurred**. And the clocks differ: the contract action
runs from the **breach**, the tort action from the **loss**. Contract
damages are assessed at the **date of breach**, so (d) is wrong.

</details>

## Part B — Short answer

**B1.** Distinguish a **lease** from a **licence** in one sentence, and give
the fact that decides it.

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

A lease grants an **interest in land** carrying **exclusive possession**; a
licence is a **personal permission** to use land and creates **no interest**
in it. The deciding fact is **exclusive possession** — not the payment of
rent, not the label the parties use. Where the owner retains the right to
use the space themselves (the Week 2 problem: Alex running his own meetings
in Anita's half-floor), there is no exclusive possession and it is a
licence.

</details>

**B2.** A buyer says the seller's agent "should have told us the house had
been a crime scene". Set out the two-step Queensland answer.

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**Step 1 — the disclosure statutes give nothing.** PLA 2023 s 99 lists what
must be disclosed and a stigmatised history is **not on the list**; the POA
imposes **no requirement to disclose the history of a property**. So there
is no right to terminate under s 104, and ***caveat emptor*** applies. The
position differs in **NSW** after ***Hinton v Commissioner for Fair
Trading*** [2006] NSWADT 257.

**Step 2 — consumer protection is the only route.** Whether silence breaches
**ACL s 18** is a **question of fact**: was there a **reasonable expectation
of disclosure** in the circumstances (*Demagogue v Ramensky*)? A direct
question answered falsely, or a half-truth, will usually get there. Bare
silence usually will not.

</details>

**B3.** What are the **three elements of negligence**, and which one is
split into a question of law and a question of fact?

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**Duty of care; breach of that duty; damage suffered as a result of the
breach.** The **breach** element splits: **that a standard of care was
owed** is a question of **law**; **whether the defendant failed to meet it**
is a question of **fact**.

</details>

**B4.** Name the three types of proximity, and state the foreseeability
test in the words the lecture uses.

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**Physical, circumstantial, causal.** Foreseeability: the harm must have
been a **real possibility, not far-fetched or fanciful**. The defendant
**need not foresee the precise loss to a specific person** — it is enough
that **some loss** could be suffered by a **class of persons of whom the
plaintiff was one**.

</details>

**B5.** Explain why an **option** can be protected by a caveat and a **first
right of refusal** cannot.

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

An option is **specifically enforceable**, so equity treats the grantee as
having an **equitable interest in the land** — and an interest in land is
what a caveat protects. A first right of refusal is a **pre-emptive right
only**: the holder must wait until the owner decides to sell, so there is
**no interest in the land**, equitable or otherwise, and nothing to caveat.
The option must also be **supported by consideration**.

</details>

**B6.** A managing agent is sued after a tenant is injured by a defect. The
agent inspected quarterly, logged the defect, engaged a licensed trade and
barricaded the area. Which element fails, and what is the one-line rule?

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**Element 2 — breach.** *Having taken all reasonable precautions, the
defendant is not liable if a person is injured.* Negligence is fault-based,
not strict; the question is what a **reasonable agent** would have done in
the circumstances, not whether someone was hurt.

</details>

**B7.** An agent sells a warehouse for **$920,000** on a **2.75%**
commission. Show the fee, and state the two threshold conditions on the
agent's right to sue for it.

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

```
Commission   0.0275 × 920,000  =  $25,300
Plus GST     25,300 × 1.1      =  $27,830
```

The agent may sue for commission and expenses only if:

1. the agent is **licensed**; and
2. the **appointment to act is signed and in the correct form** (Form 6).

Both are **gates, not factors** — fail either and the entitlement is gone
however clearly the agent was the effective cause. Under the POA the rate
itself is **deregulated and negotiable**, and there is **no requirement to
disclose to the seller how much commission is paid**.

</details>

## Part C — ISAAC problem questions

Use the five steps. State the **issue as a question**. Give a **section or a
case** at the authority step — it is a separate step from stating the law.

**C1.** Priya lists her Ashgrove house with **Marco** under an **exclusive
agency** signed on **1 August 2025**. Marco photographs the house, lists it
online and holds two open homes. On **10 September** Priya accepts an offer
from her sister's neighbour, who saw the online listing but negotiated
directly with Priya. Marco claims commission. Priya says she sold it herself
and, in any event, she "sacked him last week". Marco is licensed and holds a
signed Form 6.

*Advise Marco.*

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**Issue 1 — Is Marco entitled to commission where the seller negotiated the
sale herself?**

*State the law.* Under an **exclusive agency**, the selling agent is
entitled to the agreed commission on the sale **whether or not the agent was
the effective cause of the sale**. Effective cause matters for **open**
listings (s 20) and for **sole** agencies (s 23(3)) — not for exclusive
agencies.

*Authority.* **POA s 23(2)**, and the statutory example: R is paid in cases
1, 2, 3 **and 4** under an exclusive agency, case 4 being where **the seller
was the effective cause**.

*Application.* This is case 4 on the facts, and under an exclusive agency
case 4 still pays. Marco is licensed and holds a signed Form 6, so both
threshold conditions in ss 88–91 are met. Note that Marco does not even need
the argument, but he has one anyway: the buyer **saw the online listing**,
so Marco's marketing produced the buyer and he is arguably the effective
cause in any event.

*Conclusion.* Marco is entitled to commission at the appointment rate, plus
reimbursement of marketing expenses incurred within the scope of the
appointment.

**Issue 2 — Could Priya terminate the appointment before the sale?**

*State the law.* Under the POA an exclusive agency may run for a **maximum
of 90 days** and may be **terminated after 60 days**.

*Application.*

```
Appointment signed     1 August 2025
60 days elapse         30 September 2025   ← earliest termination
Sale                   10 September 2025   ← day 40
```

*Conclusion.* On **10 September** Priya was still inside the 60-day locked
period, so a purported termination "last week" was **ineffective**. The
appointment was on foot when the contract was made, and commission is
payable. **Ask Chris whether the 60/90-day periods run in calendar days from
signing** — the decks give the numbers but not the counting rule.

</details>

**C2.** Dev contracts to buy a warehouse at Rocklea from **Halcyon Pty Ltd**
on the standard REIQ commercial contract. Before signing, Halcyon's leasing
agent **Tomas** hands Dev an information memorandum showing a **passing rent
of $310,000 a year across four tenancies**. The IM carries a line at the
foot of the page: *"Prepared from information supplied by the vendor. The
agent has not verified this information and gives no warranty as to its
accuracy."*

Dev does no lease review. After settlement he discovers one tenant vacated
two months before the IM was produced, and Tomas knew. The actual passing
rent was **$228,000**. Dev also finds the property flooded in 2022, which
was not disclosed.

*Advise Dev.*

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**Issue 1 — Can Dev recover for the overstated rent, given the disclaimer?**

*State the law.* A person must not, in trade or commerce, engage in conduct
that is **misleading or deceptive or likely to mislead or deceive**. A
disclaimer does **not** automatically defeat liability: the court looks at
**the whole of the conduct in context** — the sophistication of the buyer,
whether the source of the information was identified, whether the agent
adopted the statement as their own, and whether independent advice was
engaged.

*Authority.* **ACL s 18** (*Competition and Consumer Act 2010* (Cth) Sch 2).
***Butcher v Lachlan Elder Realty Pty Ltd*** (2004) 218 CLR 592 — a
disclaimer protected an agent who was **merely passing on** a third party's
survey diagram to a buyer with their own solicitor.

*Application.* *Butcher* is distinguishable in the way that matters:
**Tomas knew the tenant had vacated**. He was not innocently relaying the
vendor's figure — he had actual knowledge that it was wrong when the IM was
handed over. A disclaimer of **verification** does not disclaim knowledge of
falsity, and s 18 requires **no fault at all**, so the state of Tomas's
belief only makes it worse. The shortfall is quantifiable:

```
Rent represented in the IM      $310,000
Actual passing rent             $228,000
Overstatement                    $82,000 a year   = 26.5% of the figure
                                                     represented
```

At a 7% capitalisation rate that overstatement is worth roughly $1.17m of
value, which is the measure of Dev's loss. Halcyon is liable as principal
for its agent's ACL breach (*Guthrie v Metro Ford*), and Tomas is liable
personally because s 18 binds "**a person**".

*Conclusion.* Dev has a strong s 18 claim for **damages** against both agent
and vendor, and an arguable **fraudulent misrepresentation** claim against
Tomas given his knowledge. He should also complain to the **Office of Fair
Trading**, which enforces the ACL in Queensland.

**Issue 2 — Can Dev do anything about the undisclosed 2022 flooding?**

*State the law.* The s 99 disclosure statement must contain the prescribed
information, but it also carries **express warnings about what it does not
cover** — **flooding history** is on the not-included list, along with
structural soundness and building approvals. ***Caveat emptor*** governs
what is left, and **due diligence** is the buyer's protection.

*Authority.* **PLA 2023 s 99** and the warnings in the approved form;
*caveat emptor*.

*Application.* Dev did **no due diligence** — no lease review, no flood
search, no searches at all on the facts. Flooding is precisely the risk the
disclosure regime tells him to investigate himself. Unless Dev asked about
flooding and was told something false, silence about it is not actionable.

*Conclusion.* **No remedy for the flooding.** The rent misstatement succeeds
because someone **said something untrue**; the flooding fails because nobody
said anything at all and the statute does not require them to. That contrast
is the whole of Topic 4 in two sentences.

</details>

**C3.** Nina signs a contract to buy a Toowong townhouse on **3 March**,
settlement **3 April**. On **14 March** a storm brings a tree through the
roof. Nina has no insurance. She emails the seller's conveyancing clerk
proposing a $60,000 reduction; the clerk replies *"noted, I'll pass it on"*.
Nobody responds. Nina attends settlement with a short cheque.

*Advise the seller.*

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**Issue 1 — Who bore the risk of the storm damage?**

Under the standard REIQ contract the property is at the **buyer's risk from
5pm on the first business day after the contract date** — so from 5pm on the
first business day after **3 March**, which is well before the storm on
**14 March**. **Nina bore the risk and should have insured.** There is no
right to a unilateral price abatement.

The one qualification: where a **dwelling house is so damaged before
completion as to be unfit for occupation**, the buyer has a statutory right
to **rescind by notice before completion** (*Property Law Act 1974* (Qld)
s 64, carried into the 2023 Act — **confirm the current section number**).
That right is to **terminate**, not to discount, and a tree through the roof
may or may not make the townhouse unfit. Nina did not exercise it.

**Issue 2 — Was the $60,000 reduction agreed?**

**No**, three times over: a conveyancing clerk has **no actual or ostensible
authority** to vary a land contract (ostensible authority must come from the
**principal's** conduct); *"noted, I'll pass it on"* is **not an
unconditional acceptance**; and a variation of a contract for the
disposition of land must be **in writing and signed** — **PLA 2023 s 7**.
Silence in response is **not acceptance**.

**Conclusion.** Tendering a short cheque is a **failure to complete**, and
time is of the essence. The seller may **terminate**, **forfeit the
deposit**, **sue for damages** (assessed at the date of breach, subject to
the duty to mitigate), or seek **specific performance**.

</details>

## Self-marking guide

If you are unsure whether an answer would score, check it against these
five. Each one is a mark examiners actually take off:

1. Did you **state the issue as a question**?
2. Did you give a **section number or a case name** — separately from
   stating the principle?
3. Did you **apply the law to these facts**, naming the parties, rather than
   restating the rule?
4. Where two readings are open, did you **give both and name the stronger**?
5. Did you check the **threshold conditions** before the merits — licensed
   agent, signed Form 6, contract in writing and signed, which type of
   listing?
