## 3.1 The Property Law Act 2023 (Qld)

The PLA 2023 replaces the *Property Law Act 1974* (Qld). Its headline change
is **seller disclosure**:

> Moves away from **"buyer beware"** to **positive obligations on the
> seller**. This involves a change in practice for participants in property
> transactions.

Scope of the disclosure regime:

- The requirements apply **only to contracts entered into after the
  commencement date**.
- **Implied into all contracts and cannot be contracted out of — s 98.**
- Applies to **all existing lots**, including Community Title Scheme lots.
- Does **not** yet apply to **proposed lots**, i.e. "off the plan" sales.
- Applies to **both residential and commercial** sales.

> **The Week 3 slide contradicts itself inside four lines and both readings
> are now stale.** It says the PLA 2023 "becomes valid 1st August 2025",
> then two bullets later says "The commencement date hasn't been identified
> yet", and a third slide says "The format of the disclosure statement is
> being developed". A deck cannot both fix a commencement date and say one
> has not been set. The safe exam approach is to state the **substance** of
> the regime and its section numbers, and to say the commencement date and
> approved form should be confirmed — do not put a date in an answer. Check
> Canvas and ask Chris what is in force for your cohort.

### Exceptions to seller disclosure

Disclosure is **not** required where:

- The sale is **between family members**
- The buyer is the **State or Commonwealth Government**, or the land is
  being **compulsorily acquired**
- The buyer is a **statutory authority or a publicly listed company**
- The sale is **court ordered**
- The sale is **between co-owners or neighbours for a boundary
  realignment**

> **Every exception describes a buyer who does not need protecting.**
> Governments, statutory authorities and listed companies have their own
> lawyers; family members and neighbours already know the property; a court
> is supervising. If you have to reconstruct the list under exam pressure,
> reason from that principle rather than trying to recall five bullets.

### What must be disclosed — s 99 PLA

**Prior to the buyer signing the contract**, the seller must give the buyer
**a disclosure statement and each prescribed certificate applicable to the
lot**. The statement must:

- be in an **approved form**;
- include the required information, **true at the time it is given**; and
- be **signed by the seller**.

Disclosure may include disclosure required under other Acts — for example
the BCCMA.

**Information in the statement:**

- Name of seller, address and real property description
- **Unregistered encumbrances**
- Impacts from proposals for transport infrastructure, or notice of
  intention to resume
- Zoning and heritage details
- Contamination
- Applications under the *Neighbourhood Disputes (Dividing Fences and
  Trees) Act 2011* (Qld)
- Rates and water information

**Warnings in the statement:**

- Possible **statutory easements**
- **No warranty as to the structural integrity** of improvements
- Rates and charges may differ from the amounts imposed on the seller

**Information expressly *not* included** — and the statement must say so:

- **Flooding history**
- **Structural soundness**
- Current or past **building approvals or zoning limits**

**Certificates annexed:**

- Title and registered plan
- Notices under building and planning Acts
- Notices to complete work on the property
- Documents relevant to heritage registers
- Notice of intention to resume
- Pool compliance certificates
- Material related to a Community Title Scheme

> **The three exclusions are the single most examinable fact in this
> topic.** Flooding history, structural soundness and building approvals are
> *not* disclosed — and two tutorial questions (Week 6 Q1 and Q2) are built
> on exactly that. The PLA does **not** hand the buyer everything they need.
> Caveat emptor survives in the gaps, which is precisely why the buyer runs
> due diligence (§3.6).

### What happens if the seller does not comply — s 104

The buyer may **terminate the contract** where:

- the seller **does not give** a disclosure statement; **or**
- the statement is **incomplete or inaccurate**, **and** the buyer is **not
  aware** of the correct state of affairs, **and** the buyer **would not
  have signed** the contract had they been aware.

> **Limb two has three cumulative elements, not one.** An inaccurate
> statement alone does not give a right to terminate. The buyer must also
> have been ignorant of the truth **and** be able to say the inaccuracy was
> material to their decision to sign. A buyer who already knew, or who would
> have signed anyway, gets nothing under s 104.

### Implied conditions — s 76 PLA

Reproduced on the Week 3 slide as an image of the Act. The conditions
implied into a contract for the sale of land:

| s 76 | Condition |
|---|---|
| (1)(a) | The seller must give the buyer, **at the seller's cost**, a copy of each document in relation to which a **caveat** is registered that is in the seller's possession |
| (1)(b) | The seller must, **at the seller's cost**, remove any objection to the registration of any document required to give effect to the contract — **unless** (i) the objection arises from the **buyer's own act or omission**, or (ii) the objection **may have been discovered by a reasonable person in the buyer's position** and was **not raised by the buyer before settlement** |
| (1)(c) | If the seller is required to transfer the land **free from encumbrances**, the seller must on settlement **discharge encumbrances out of the purchase money** and, for encumbrances registered on title, **give the buyer a release in registrable form** |
| (2)(a) | If the sale is **not an e-conveyance**, payment may be made by **cheque drawn on a financial institution** |
| (2)(b) | Settlement must take place at the **land registry office** at which the document may be lodged — or, if more than one, the **nearest** to the land |
| (3) | **The section applies subject to the terms of the contract** |

> **s 76(3) is the sting: these are defaults, not guarantees.** Every
> condition in s 76 yields to the express terms of the contract. Compare
> s 98, which makes the **disclosure** obligations non-excludable. So the
> disclosure regime cannot be drafted away and the implied conditions can.
> Getting that pair the wrong way round is a clean loss of marks.

### Contracts for land must be in writing

Section 7 PLA 2023, quoted from the Week 3 slide image:

> **7 Contracts for disposition of land not enforceable unless in writing**
> A contract for the disposition of land is not enforceable by action in a
> proceeding unless—
> (a) the contract is in writing or some memorandum or note of the contract
> is recorded in writing; and
> (b) the contract or the memorandum or note of the contract is signed by
> the party against whom the contract is sought to be enforced.

Note the two limbs: **writing** *and* **signature by the party to be charged**.

> **Two speaker notes in the decks still cite "section 59 of the PLA".**
> The Week 3 and Week 5 lecture notes on commercial contracts both say the
> contract must be in writing "according to section 59 of the PLA". Section
> 59 was the **PLA 1974** provision. Under the **PLA 2023 it is s 7** — and
> three other sources agree: the Week 3 slide quotes s 7 directly, the Week
> 5 tutorial answer key says "S7 of the Property Law Act stipulates this",
> and the Week 6 tutorial offers "Section 7 of the Property Law Act" as the
> correct MCQ option. **Answer s 7.** The s 59 notes are left over from the
> old Act.

### The REIQ standard contracts

Contracts are usually on REIQ standard forms:

- Contract for **Houses and Residential Land**
- Contract for **Residential Lots in a Community Titles Scheme**
- Contract for **Commercial Land and Buildings**
- Contract for **Commercial Lots in a Community Titles Scheme**

The parties are usually brought together by real estate agents, and in the

(prohibition on engaging in legal practice when not entitled) appears in the

## 3.2 Instalment contracts

**What creates one.** A contract may become an instalment contract through:

- payment of **greater than 10%** of the contract sum by way of deposit
  (**20% for a proposed lot**); or
- payment of **interest** on outstanding purchase monies.

**What the PLA 2023 clarified.** An instalment will **no longer** include:

- option fees;
- amounts the buyer pays **after entering into a contract** for rent,
  outgoings, holding costs, interest on the purchase price, and extension
  fees.

### Worked example — the option fee / deposit trap

The Week 3 speaker notes set this out as a live drafting problem. If an
option fee is to form part of the deposit, the combination can push you over
the 10% line and convert the contract into an instalment contract by
accident.

```
Purchase price                            $800,000
10% threshold          0.10 × 800,000  =   $80,000

Option fee paid on grant of the option        $5,000   ( = 0.625% of price)
Maximum further deposit before breach     80,000 − 5,000
                                        =  $75,000
```

Take a deposit of $80,000 *on top of* the $5,000 option fee and the total is
$85,000, which is 10.625% of the price — over the line.

The notes give the two drafting fixes:

1. Make the option fee **non-refundable only if the option is not
   exercised**; and
2. If the option fee **is** to form part of the deposit, ensure the **entire
   amount does not exceed 10%** of the purchase price.

> **The 20% figure only applies to proposed lots.** For an existing lot the
> line is 10%. On a $2,000,000 apartment that is $200,000 for a completed
> lot but $400,000 for an off-the-plan one. Read the question for the word
> "proposed" before you pick a threshold.

> **The 5% answer in the MCQs is a distractor and appears in two different
> tutorials.** Both the Week 4 and Week 5 tutorial quizzes offer "more than
> 5%" as an option. It is never right. The statutory trigger is **more than
> 10%**, and the option describing a third of the price plus transfer of the
> property describes something else entirely.

## 3.3 Commercial contracts

- The principles of **common law contracts and the POA provisions apply**.
- The principles relating to an **auction apply to commercial contracts too**.
- Commercial contracts differ from residential in that **solicitors are
  usually engaged to prepare the contracts**, and (per Week 5) **the due
  diligence is far more complex**.

### The professional team

| Professional | Role |
|---|---|
| **Solicitor** | Draft the contract, ensure PLA disclosure is adhered to, provide legal support for the transaction / conveyance |
| **Accountant** | Tax implications and **GST** requirements |
| **Engineer / building surveyor** | Physical condition of the building, compliance with building regulations, contamination |
| **Environmental consultant** | Assess the energy rating *(added in Week 5)* |
| **Valuer** | Assess projected returns and inform on purchase price |
| **Planner** | Compliance, and advice on future **development potential** |

The **first document or representation by the seller** in a commercial deal
is usually an **Information Memorandum (IM)**.

## 3.4 Commercial building disclosure — BEEC and NABERS

The **Commercial Building Disclosure** program commenced **1 November
2010**. It is a **national** program.

**The obligation:** disclose energy efficiency information to prospective
buyers or tenants when the space is **sold, leased or subleased**.

**Who it applies to:** owners and lessors of **commercial office space with
a net lettable area of 1,000 m² or more**.

**What must be disclosed:** the complete **Building Energy Efficiency
Certificate (BEEC)**, which comprises:

1. **NABERS energy base building rating**
2. **Tenancy lighting information**
3. **Generic energy efficiency guidance**

**All advertisements** about the sale, lease or sublease must include an
**energy rating**.

Practical detail from the speaker notes:

- **NABERS** = National Australian Built Environment Rating System. The
  point is that investors can compare buildings across Australia on an equal
  measure. The lecturer's stated flaw: **ratings are applied more strictly
  in Victoria** than the rest of the country — a building rated 2 in
  Victoria is likely to achieve 3 in Sydney.
- Disclosure happens **very early** in the transaction because the ratings
  must be registered **before any offer to sell or lease**. A copy of the
  BEEC can be obtained from an online registry.
- **A BEEC remains valid for 12 months from the date of issue.**

### Exemptions and exceptions

| Exempt | Note |
|---|---|
| **Newly constructed offices** where the certificate of completion is **less than 2 years old** | |
| **Strata titled offices** | |
| Sale of a building **through the sale of shares or units**, or the sale of a **partial interest** | The speaker notes flag this: it "transfers the economic benefits of ownership but is still not caught" |
| **Short-term leases and subleases of 12 months or less** | |
| **Assignment of a lease** | Even a **long term** lease |
| **Mortgagee exercising the power of sale** | |
| Offices used for certain uses that cannot obtain a rating | e.g. police operations |

> **The exemptions are what make the Week 4 tutorial MCQ answerable, and
> they rule out the tempting "all of the above".** That question asks when
> an energy rating must be displayed and offers: (a) when advertising or
> providing information about a commercial premises for sale or lease;
> (b) for all newly constructed office tenancies and buildings; (c) when a
> lease is assigned; (d) all of the above. **(b) and (c) are both on the
> exemption list** — new buildings under two years old, and lease
> assignments even of long leases. So only **(a)** survives. Working through
> §3.4 turns a guess into a certainty.

The lecture also poses these for discussion, which is quiz-shaped: what are
the benefits of the legislation, what are the negative consequences and to
whom, and would a similar system for residential property be beneficial?

## 3.5 Option contracts

**Uses.** A property developer may use an option to **secure a site**. For a
commercial tenant, it might be the **right to renew a lease**.

### Terminology

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| **Option in gross** | An option standing alone, **not collateral to another grant** such as a lease |
| **Call option** | The **grantor** gives the **grantee** a call option **to buy** the property from the grantor |
| **Put option** | The **grantee** gives the **grantor** a put option **to sell** the property to the grantee |

Both put and call options may be conferred by instrument as a discrete
subject matter, or contained in a lease. Options in leases may be options to
**renew the term** or options to **purchase the reversion**.

### Option versus first right of refusal

| | Option | First right of refusal |
|---|---|---|
| Nature | **Specifically enforceable** — creates an **equitable interest in the land** | A **pre-emptive right** only. **No interest in the land**, equitable or otherwise |
| Consideration | **Must be supported by consideration** | — |
| Who moves first | The holder **can take the initiative** and demand to buy | Must **wait until the owner wishes to sell** |
| If not exercised | — | The right is **lost forever** |
| Protection | Because an equitable interest is granted, **a caveat can be lodged** | Nothing to caveat |

> **"Creates an equitable interest → caveatable" is the chain that carries
> the marks.** The option is specifically enforceable, so equity treats the
> grantee as having an interest in the land, so a caveat can be lodged to
> protect it. A first right of refusal gives none of this. If a developer
> asks how to secure a site for two years without buying it, the answer is a
> call option plus a caveat — not a right of first refusal.

### Other option points

- **Options over unregistered land** must satisfy the disclosure
  requirements under the *Land Sales Act 1984* and the BCCM Act **before**
  entry into the option.
- **Options in residential leases:** options to purchase may be contained in
  residential leases, but are **better placed in separate option contracts**
  — *Mark Bain Constructions Pty Ltd v Tim Barling, Alex Wilson and Timothy
  Scott* [2006] QSC 48.

**Purchaser's checklist when considering an option:**

1. Seek legal advice.
2. Is it a **put and call** or a **call** option?
3. Is the consideration appropriate, and is it refundable if the option is
   not exercised?
4. How are the buyer's rights protected if the option runs for a long
   period? **Is it a caveatable interest?**
5. Can the buyer **nominate a third party** under the option?
6. Is there compliance with the **Land Sales Act** and the **BCCM Act**?

## 3.6 Due diligence

**Caveat emptor** — "let the buyer beware" — is described in the Week 5
lecture as **the overriding doctrine that covers all real estate
transactions**. It was first applied to real estate transactions in England
in the **16th century**. Generally the seller **does not warrant anything**
unless they have made a specific promise or commitment. The problem is
**latent defects** — those the buyer could not appreciate through a physical
inspection.

> The purchaser must take all necessary steps to discover as much as they
> can before entering into the transaction. **Due diligence is the process
> of discovery.**

Definition (Investopedia, quoted in the lecture):

> "1. An investigation or audit of a potential investment. Due diligence
> serves to confirm all material facts in regards to a sale. OR 2.
> Generally, due diligence refers to the care a reasonable person should
> take before entering into an agreement or a transaction with another
> party."

**The extent of due diligence depends on the nature of the property.** Due
diligence can be for acquisition, occupation, disposal or development —
this unit focuses on **acquisition**.

Three legs: **technical, legal, financial.**

### Benefits of due diligence (RICS)

- Gaining an understanding of the **condition and design** of the building
- Establishing the **suitability of the property for its intended use**
- Understanding the need for, and **quantifying, future costs and
  liabilities**
- Providing a level of **protection for institutional investors**
- Providing a **solid foundation for price negotiations and allocation of
  risk**

The speaker notes make the framing explicit: due diligence is **primarily
about risk management** and ensuring the purchaser is buying what they
expect and that the property is reasonably fit for purpose. **Most
properties have some defects** that may affect performance.

### Technical due diligence — the RICS 18 components

| # | Component | What it covers |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **General property description** | Name and address; orientation; overview of surrounding areas including zoning, building usage and infrastructure; description of buildings and improvements; age of construction and alterations; the building area |
| 2 | **Access** | Access to all areas of the building *(the lecturer notes you will not have building access for the assignment)* |
| 3 | **Structure** | Structural components, adequacy of design and limitations; whether the building is **fit for purpose**; maintenance required |
| 4 | **Building fabric** | Components of construction; fit for purpose or limiting |
| 5 | **External areas** | Improvements, condition, fitness for purpose |
| 6 | **Building services** | Identification and assessment; compliance with standard codes |
| 7 | **Environmental issues** | Preliminary or detailed. Hazardous materials, geotechnical issues, acoustics, air quality |
| 8 | **Sustainability** | Environmental / sustainability rating tools applicable to the property type |
| 9 | **Planning** | Planning controls and council files, compliance with schemes |
| 10 | **Statutory compliance review** | Building code compliance; compliance with access legislation |
| 11 | **Heritage significance** | Investigation of heritage registers |
| 12 | **Floor space ratio, car parking ratio and efficiency** | |
| 13 | **Identification surveys** | Where the buildings and improvements sit on the site; easements |
| 14 | **Tax depreciation and capital allowances** | Allowances available for capital improvements |
| 15 | **Reinstatement cost assessment** | Cost to reinstate the improvements |
| 16 | **Capital expenditure forecasts (CAPEX)** | Indicative forecasts over a **5 or 10 year** period |
| 17 | **Operational expenditure assessment** | Current outgoings, normally obtained from the seller. **May be covered by the tenants depending on the nature of the lease** |
| 18 | **ESG** | Environmental sustainability, Social sustainability and Governance |

> **Item 17 is where technical due diligence collides with the lease.**
> Whether outgoings are the landlord's cost or recovered from tenants is a
> lease question, and it changes net income and therefore value. This is the
> hinge between due diligence and the lease terms in note 05 — a building
> with $400,000 of outgoings is a completely different investment depending
> on whether the leases are gross or net.

### Legal due diligence — 13 items

1. **Title and tenure** — evidence of title and tenure; leases and whether
   they are **registered on title**; status of rent reviews; check the
   **survey plan** for evidence of trespass
2. **Boundaries** — poorly defined boundaries; is the building contained on
   the site or is there an **encroachment** from a neighbour
3. **Guarantees and warranties** — **can they be transferred?**
4. **Leases** — status; evidence of breaches; rent review, options and
   dispute resolution clauses
5. **Insurances** — adequate pre-settlement
6. **WHS** — compliance with WHS codes
7. **Vendor company searches** — confirm the vendor has the right to sell
8. **Unregistered documents** — any unregistered documents or interests
9. **Local authority notices / demands**
10. **Statutory notices** — statutory notices, authority charges, covenants
11. **Development approvals / permits** — is the development compliant
12. Is the agreement **subject to satisfactory due diligence**?
13. **Contractual conditions** — are the terms of the contract agreeable

The Week 3 deck adds the buyer's own title checklist:

- **Search title and the registered plan** and confirm: the seller is the
  registered owner; the details in the contract match the search results;
  there are **no undisclosed encumbrances**
- **Check registered leases**
- **Review the Community Management Statement** if applicable
- **Ask for details of any unregistered encumbrances** — the notes list
  these as **building covenants, easements, equitable mortgages, leases**
- Where the seller is a **corporation**, **search ASIC**, confirm the name
  and **ACN**, and **check for company charges**

And for a Community Title lot: ensure the **information sheet is attached as
the top sheet** of the contract of sale, and check body corporate records to
confirm the seller has complied with their BCCM Act disclosure obligations.

> **Unregistered encumbrances are the reason the buyer must *ask*.**
> A title search shows registered interests. Building covenants, easements
> not on title, equitable mortgages and unregistered leases will not appear —
> and they are the first line item in the PLA s 99 disclosure statement for
> exactly that reason. The seller must disclose them; the buyer must still
> ask.

### Legal due diligence when purchasing (the Week 3 list)

- Check the **seller's compliance with warranties and disclosure
  obligations**
- **Review leases** to ensure legal compliance, particularly the **Retail
  Shop Leases Act**
- Review the **lease schedule** to ensure all information is disclosed and
  accurate
- Review the **important provisions of the lease**: rent review, term and
  options, outgoings, recovery, demolition and relocation, use, restrictions
  on redevelopment, incentives

### Financial due diligence — 12 items

1. **Asset schedules** — confirm what is included and excluded in the
   transaction
2. **Bank guarantees** — confirm security deposits, bank guarantees and
   releases
3. Confirm **CAPEX** expenditure and **update the DCF analysis**
4. **Income analysis** — review the lease summary for **gross and net rent**;
   is rent up to date; any disputes or arrears?
5. Obtain an **independent valuation**
6. **Land tax** — confirm the land tax position
7. **Mortgages** — confirm all mortgages and liens on property assets
8. Confirm **Opex**
9. **Profit and loss statement** — review for abnormalities
10. **Rate notices** — review the current rate notice
11. **Sinking funds** — check contributions and anticipated expenditure
12. **Additional income streams** — any side arrangements producing extra
    income

## 3.7 Off-the-plan sales

**What they are.** Apartments advertised for sale **before the building has
been constructed and the lot is created**. The final appearance is often
included in advertising material. There is **no ability to view the finished
product** before entering the contract. Authority: *Mark Bain Construction v
Avis*. The traditional advantage was a **slightly reduced purchase price in
exchange for a guaranteed sale**.

**The six questions the lecture says a purchaser must ask:**

1. Can changes be made to the **finishes / appliances**?
2. What happens if the building is **finished earlier or delayed**?
3. Is the **deposit secure** if the building does not proceed?
4. Can the purchaser's interest be **transferred prior to completion**?
5. Is there a **sunset clause**?
6. What happens if the **developer goes into liquidation**?

From the speaker notes: where there are significant delays the **developer
may be able to pull out**, leaving the purchaser with very limited remedies.
A **sunset clause** means that unless the property is completed by a date,
the contract is cancelled. And in **Queensland and New South Wales there is
no requirement for developers to take out home warranty insurance for
large-scale apartment buildings**.

**Conditionality.** Off-the-plan contracts are often conditional on:

- the developer generating **enough pre-sales** to make the project viable
  so it can **secure funding**; and
- the developer obtaining **satisfactory development approvals**.

> **Read who owns the condition.** The speaker notes say that if the
> developer cannot satisfy these conditions, **it is the developer, not the
> purchaser, who has the right to terminate**. A pre-sale condition looks
> like a protection for buyers and is nothing of the kind — it is an exit
> the developer holds and the buyer does not.

**Buyers should seek advice on:** tax, stamp duty, additional legal fees,
and the fact that **when reselling you remain bound to the developer**.

**Law to be aware of:** common law misrepresentations, the Australian
Consumer Law, the *Land Sales Act*, and the BCCM Act — noting the developer
might be in **control of the body corporate**.

### BCCM disclosure and the extra termination rights

Part 2 of the BCCM requires an **information sheet** and a **disclosure
statement**. Changes required by the *Body Corporate and Community
Management Amendment Act 2011* (Qld) may give buyers the right to terminate
otherwise enforceable contracts.

**Key changes to disclosure:**

- **Interest schedule lot entitlements must be equal to market value** of
  the lot
- Must **state the contribution schedule principle**

**Additional termination rights.** A buyer may terminate a contract of sale
**prior to settlement** where:

1. A **new CMS is recorded** after the contract is entered into but before
   settlement, and the buyer **would be materially prejudiced** if compelled
   to complete;
2. The **copy of the CMS attached to the contract differs** from the CMS
   most recently advised to the buyer, and the buyer would be **materially
   prejudiced**; or
3. The seller is the **original owner** for the scheme and the buyer
   **reasonably believes** the **contribution schedule lot entitlements
   (CSLE)** are inconsistent with the principle on which they were decided,
   and reasonably believes they would be **materially prejudiced**.

> **All three termination rights turn on "materially prejudiced".** None of
> them is automatic. A changed CMS is not, by itself, a right to walk — the
> buyer must be able to point to real prejudice. Note also that limb 3 uses
> a *reasonable belief* standard rather than requiring the buyer to prove
> the entitlements are actually wrong.

**Settlement** usually occurs **14 days after the title is issued**. The
developer usually has a statutory timeframe in which to finish the work and
settle — the **sunset date**.

### Land Sales Act 1984 (Qld)

**Objects of the Act:**

> (a) to facilitate property development in Queensland; and
> (b) to protect the interests of consumers in relation to property
> development; and
> (c) to ensure that proposed allotments and proposed lots are clearly
> identified; and
> (d) to achieve the objects mentioned in paragraphs (a) to (c) without
> imposing procedural obligations on local governments or the authority in
> addition to their or its obligations under the Planning Act or the ULDA
> Act.

**Application.** The Act regulates the sale of land (**proposed
allotments**), protecting consumers who are at a particular disadvantage
**because the lot has not yet been created**. From the notes: in the 1970s
there were many unscrupulous practices involving the sale of unregistered
land, and unregistered land **remains at risk of misdescription** between
contract and settlement. **Caveat emptor is now considered quite outdated —
the emphasis is on disclosure to the purchaser.**

### The lecturer's self-study questions, answered

The Week 3 deck sets three self-study questions and answers them in the
speaker notes:

| Question | Answer from the notes |
|---|---|
| What are the disclosure requirements when a developer sells land prior to registration, or a lot in a community title scheme? | A **body corporate statement issued in compliance with the Act** |
| Can the developer vary the offering once the contract has been entered into? | **Yes** |
| How long does the developer have to issue a registrable lot? | Governed by the **sunset clause** |

And a fourth: *how would a valuer approach a valuation of an apartment off
the plan for mortgage and security purposes?* The notes answer: the **going
sale price on completion — not what you paid for it**. Land does not
depreciate but improvements do, so you want the **highest land-to-asset
ratio possible**. The developer wants the opposite — as many apartments on
the land as possible, which means more tenants than owner-occupiers, and
more maintenance.

> **That last answer contains the whole off-the-plan risk in one line.**
> The valuation is done at completion on completion-day evidence, not on
> your contract price. If the market moves or the developer packs the site,
> the bank's valuation can land below the price you are contractually bound
> to pay — and you must find the difference in cash.

## 3.8 What you should expect from your solicitor

A solicitor should:

- Act **ethically** and comply with the **Client Care rule of the Queensland
  Law Society** and the *Legal Profession (Solicitors) Rule 2007*
- Have a **defined scope of appointment**, not outside their expertise —
  there is a **division between legal and commercial advice**, and
  commercial advice should come from another professional
- Keep the client **fully informed** of the progress of the transaction
- Keep the client **abreast of any rights to terminate or opportunities for
  compensation**
- **Only act on instruction** from the client
- **Not engage in any conflict of interest** — acting for both buyer and
  seller is at risk of conflict
- **Comply with time limits** in the contract
- Exercise **professional knowledge, skill and judgment**
- Warn the client of **inherent risks** unless the client is already aware
- Advise of **adverse terms, critical time periods and opportunities to
  terminate**
- Stay aware of **changes in legislation, changes to approved forms (e.g.
  BCCMA forms) and court decisions** affecting conveyancing

**Delegation:** parts of the transaction may be delegated to employees such
as paralegals, **but responsibility for any errors, omissions or mistakes
remains the solicitor's**.

From the speaker notes: a solicitor should **definitely not act for both
parties** when one party pays or offers to pay the other party's legal fees,
or provides some other financial inducement. If a solicitor does accept
instructions from both parties, **full and frank disclosure is required**.

And a practical observation worth keeping: **solicitors frequently have very
little to do with the contract until it becomes unconditional**, particularly
in residential deals where contracts are often drafted by real estate agents.

> **"Responsibility remains the solicitor's" is the same rule as agency's
> "cannot delegate responsibility, may delegate the work".** Both the agent
> (Topic 2) and the solicitor may hand the *task* to an employee and neither
> can hand over the *liability*. If a question involves a paralegal missing
> a deadline or an assistant misfiling a notice, the principal professional
> wears it.

**Q1.** A seller gives a disclosure statement that omits a registered
easement the buyer already knew about from their own title search. Can the
buyer terminate under s 104?

**A.** No. Limb two of s 104 requires the statement to be incomplete or
inaccurate **and** the buyer to be unaware of the correct state of affairs
**and** the buyer would not have signed had they known. The buyer knew, so
the second element fails.

**Q2.** Buyer discovers after settlement that the house floods. Any claim
under the PLA disclosure regime?

**A.** No. **Flooding history is expressly excluded** from the disclosure
statement — the statement must highlight that it is not included. This is
caveat emptor territory, so it was a due diligence item for the buyer.

**Q3.** A $1,500,000 existing house. The buyer pays a $160,000 deposit. Is
this an instalment contract?

**A.**

```
10% of 1,500,000 = $150,000
Deposit paid     = $160,000  → exceeds the threshold
```

Yes — payment of greater than 10% of the contract sum by way of deposit.
(If it were a **proposed** lot the threshold would be 20% = $300,000 and it
would not be.)

**Q4.** An owner sells a 900 m² NLA suburban office building. Must they
obtain a BEEC?

**A.** No. The obligation applies to commercial office space with a net
lettable area of **1,000 m² or more**. 900 m² is below the threshold.

**Q5.** A tenant assigns a 10-year lease over 4,000 m² of office space. BEEC
required?

**A.** No. **Assignment of a lease is exempt — even a long term lease.**

**Q6.** A developer wants to tie up a site for 18 months while they test
feasibility, and wants protection against the owner selling to someone else.
Option or first right of refusal?

**A.** A **call option**. It is specifically enforceable, creates an
**equitable interest in the land**, and can therefore be protected by
lodging a **caveat**. A first right of refusal creates no interest, cannot
be caveated, and leaves the developer waiting for the owner to decide to
sell. Watch the option fee against the 10% instalment threshold.

## Summary

- The **PLA 2023** replaces buyer-beware with **positive seller disclosure**:
  s 99 statement plus certificates **before signing**, **s 98** makes it
  non-excludable, **s 104** allows termination on the three-limb test.
- The statement **excludes flooding history, structural soundness and
  building approvals** — the gap that keeps caveat emptor alive.
- **s 76** implies conditions into land contracts but **s 76(3) makes them
  subject to the contract**; **s 7** requires **writing and signature** by
  the party to be charged (the old s 59 numbering is from the 1974 Act).
- **Instalment contract** trigger: deposit **> 10%** (20% for a proposed
  lot) or interest on outstanding purchase monies. Option fees are no longer
  caught, but option fee + deposit can still breach 10%.
- **Commercial building disclosure**: office NLA **≥ 1,000 m²**, disclose
  the **BEEC** (NABERS base building rating + tenancy lighting + generic
  guidance), valid **12 months**; exemptions include buildings **< 2 years**
  old and **lease assignments**.
- **Call option** = grantee may buy; **put option** = grantor may sell. An
  option is specifically enforceable, creates an **equitable interest**, and
  is **caveatable**; a first right of refusal is none of those.
- Due diligence has **three legs — technical (RICS 18 components), legal (13
  items) and financial (12 items)** — and exists because the seller warrants
  nothing beyond their express promises and the statutory disclosure.
- Off the plan: watch the **sunset clause**, who owns the conditions, the
  **BCCM material-prejudice termination rights**, and the valuation risk
  that the bank values at completion, not at your contract price.
