# TOPIC 1 — The Legal System and Contract Law

Week 1. Where Australian law comes from, and then the whole of contract law
compressed into one lecture. Everything later in the unit is an application
of this: a sale contract, an agency agreement and a lease are all contracts
first and property documents second.

## 1.1 Where law comes from

**The Constitution** is the prime source of law. It established the powers
of the Commonwealth and the States and set up the arms of government.

| Arm | Function |
|---|---|
| **Legislature** | Prime responsibility in the Australian legal system. Laws made by legislatures — Commonwealth, States and Territories |
| **Executive** | Prime function is to **administer** the law |
| **Judiciary** | **Creates common law** through decisions on legal cases. Also delegated the task of creating rules and regulations (subordinate legislation) |

Australia has **no Bill of Rights**. The 1898 Constitutional Convention
considered whether Australia should have one modelled on the 14th Amendment
to the United States Constitution and declined. There are various individual
rights recognised in the Constitution, but nothing like a Bill of Rights.

### Common law and the doctrine of precedent

Common law is law made **through the courts**, as opposed to legislation
made by parliament. It rests on the **doctrine of precedent**:

> Cases with the same material facts will be decided in the same way,
> **provided the decision is from a higher court in the same hierarchy.**

Those last six words are the whole test. A Queensland court is bound by
decisions of higher Queensland courts and by the High Court. It is *not*
bound by a New South Wales decision — that is a different hierarchy, so a
NSW judgment is persuasive only.

> **This is why the stigmatised-property answer in Topic 4 is what it is.**
> *Hinton v Commissioner for Fair Trading* is a NSW tribunal decision, and
> *Stambovsky v Ackley* and *Reed v King* are American. None of them bind a
> Queensland court. That is not a technicality — it is the reason the Week 5
> tutorial answer is "no duty to disclose in Queensland" even though NSW
> reaches the opposite result on nearly identical facts.

### The court hierarchy (Queensland)

From the Sir Harry Gibbs Legal Heritage Centre diagram in the Week 1 deck:

| Court | Civil jurisdiction | Criminal / other |
|---|---|---|
| **High Court of Australia** | Interprets the Constitution; settles disputes between States, Territories and the Commonwealth; hears appeals from Supreme, Family and Federal Courts | — |
| **Court of Appeal** | Hears appeals from Supreme / District Courts | — |
| **Supreme Court** | Civil matters **over $750,000** | Serious criminal cases (murder, manslaughter) |
| **District Court** | Civil matters **over $150,000**; appeals from magistrates | Serious criminal cases (rape, armed robbery, fraud) |
| **Magistrates Court** | Civil matters **less than $150,000** | Summary offences; committal hearings |
| **Federal Court** | Breaches of Federal legislation; reviews decisions of Federal Government Officers; appeals from Federal Circuit Court | — |
| **Federal Circuit Court** | Judicial review of administrative decisions; bankruptcy; family law relief up to $750,000 | **Does not** deal with criminal matters |

### Divisions of the law

| Public law | Private law |
|---|---|
| Criminal law | Contract law |
| Administrative law | Tort law |
| Constitutional law | Trusts law |
| | Family law |

And the split that actually gets examined:

| | Criminal law | Civil law |
|---|---|---|
| Between whom | Victim and perpetrator, where the act is so serious it is an affront to society | Individuals |

## 1.2 What a contract is

Essentially an agreement. The most common form of legal transaction. It is
all about **enforcing promises** — more than a moral obligation. It comes
from a foundation of common law, and **privity of contract** applies: only
the parties to a contract can enforce it.

Contracts do **not** have to be in writing. Oral contracts are enforceable.
The exceptions are **contracts for the sale of land** and **guarantees** —
and the land exception is s 7 of the *Property Law Act 2023* (Qld), which
you meet properly in Topic 3.

### Offer and acceptance

**Display of goods is an invitation to treat, not an offer.**

*Pharmaceutical Society of Great Britain v Boots Cash Chemists* [1953] 1 QB
401. Boots ran a self-service pharmacy. The question was whether the sale of
medicinal products was completed when the customer picked the item off the
shelf, or at the checkout under the pharmacist's supervision. **Held:** the
agreement is only concluded when the customer presents the goods to the
cashier and the cashier accepts. The display is an invitation to treat; the
customer's presentation of the goods is the offer; the cashier's acceptance
forms the contract.

**An advertisement can be a unilateral offer to the world.**

*Carlill v Carbolic Smoke Ball Company*. The company advertised that it
would pay **£100** to anyone who used its smoke ball as directed and still
caught influenza. Mrs Carlill did, and did. The company argued the ad was a
mere invitation to treat. **Held:** it was a unilateral offer made to the
world, and Mrs Carlill's performance of the conditions was the acceptance.
The company was bound to pay.

> **Boots and Carlill are not in conflict — they are the two ends of one
> rule.** The question is always whether the words show an intention to be
> bound *without further negotiation*. A shelf price does not; a promise to
> pay a stated sum on a stated condition, backed by a deposit lodged at a
> bank, does. If an exam asks whether marketing material is an offer, this
> is the distinction to run.

### Rules of acceptance

- Acceptance of an offer **cannot be revoked** — it signifies agreement.
- Acceptance must be **unconditional**.
- **Only the person to whom the offer was made** may accept.
- Acceptance must be **communicated**.
- Acceptance occurs **when communicated**. The time of acceptance is the
  time of formation of the contract.
- The **place of acceptance is the place where the contract is formed**.

For acceptance by email, the *Electronic Transactions Act 1999* (Cth) s 14
fixes the **time of dispatch**: the time when the electronic communication
leaves an information system under the control of the originator, or — if it
never leaves that system — the time it is received by the addressee.

> **"Acceptance must be unconditional" is the sentence that decides the Week
> 4 tutorial problem.** A reply that says "that should be OK but I will have
> to speak to my boss — if it is not OK we will let you know" is by its own
> terms conditional and by its own terms not yet communicated. It is not
> acceptance. Silence afterwards does not convert it into one.

### Agreement — *consensus ad idem*

The modern approach:

- Parties must be in complete agreement.
- Acceptance of the offer means an agreement has been reached.
- **Freedom of contract is vital.**

### Intention to create legal relations

- A contract demands that the parties intend it to be **enforceable**.
- **Commercial** agreements are **presumed** to be legally binding.
- **Social and domestic** agreements are **presumed not** to be legally
  binding — but the presumption may be rebutted on the facts.

*Todd v Nicol* [1957] SASR 72. Mrs Nicol lived in South Australia; the Todds
(her sister-in-law and niece) lived in Scotland. Nicol wrote promising free
accommodation and to leave them the house in her will. They came. An
argument developed and Nicol asked them to leave. There is a general
presumption that family agreements are not contractually binding, but an
exception was made here because of the **extent of the action required to
accept** — the Todds had to leave their jobs, sell property, say goodbye to
their friends and move to the other side of the world.

*(The Week 1 slides spell the case both "Todd v Nicol" and "Todd v Nichol".
"Nicol" is the spelling on the case-detail slide; use that.)*

### Consideration

- Consideration amounts to an **exchange of promises** — the price for the
  offeror's promise.
- The person who wants to **enforce** the contract must show there was
  consideration.
- **Past consideration is not good consideration.**
- Consideration **does not have to be adequate, but must be sufficient.**
- **Illusory promises** are not consideration.
- Consideration may be a promise **to do** something or **not to do**
  something — for example, a promise not to sue in negligence.

### Estoppel — enforcing a promise without consideration

Equitable or promissory estoppel is the equitable route for a promisee to
enforce another's promise.

*Waltons Stores (Interstate) Ltd v Maher* (1988) 164 CLR 387 (HCA). Maher
and Waltons negotiated a lease: the existing building was to be demolished
and rebuilt to Waltons' specifications. The lease was exchanged and verbally
approved, and Waltons' solicitor said "we will let you know tomorrow if
these are not approved". The Mahers heard nothing, and demolished **40%** of
the building. Waltons then pulled out.

**Issue:** could Waltons walk away because no valid contract existed?
**Held:** no. Waltons was **estopped** — it knew the Mahers were acting to
their detriment, and did nothing to intervene. That inaction encouraged the
Mahers to act as they did.

> **The lecture slide says estoppel "applies only as a shield", and then
> cites the case that is famous for the opposite.** *Waltons Stores* is the
> High Court decision in which estoppel founded a **cause of action** — the
> Mahers were plaintiffs, not defendants resisting a claim. If a question
> asks how you assert rights with no consideration, the answer is promissory
> estoppel *and Waltons Stores is the authority*, so the "shield only"
> phrasing cannot be taken at face value. Ask Chris to reconcile the slide
> with the case before the quiz; the safe exam line is that in Australia,
> after *Waltons Stores*, equitable estoppel can support a claim, not merely
> a defence.

### Capacity

| Party | Position |
|---|---|
| **Minors** | Contracts for **necessities** and **beneficial services** are binding; contracts for **luxuries** are not |
| **Corporations** | Fully capable of forming binding contracts. Persons contracting with company agents are entitled to assume the agent has authority to bind the company |
| **Drunks** | Contracts are *prima facie* valid. Can repudiate **if** they were incapable of forming a binding contract **and the other party was aware of that** |
| **Unsound mind** | Same two-limb test as above |

> **Both incapacity limbs must be satisfied.** Being drunk is not enough —
> the other party must have known. A vendor who has no idea the buyer is
> impaired keeps the contract.

**Q1.** A supermarket displays a chocolate bar at $3. Is that an offer?

**A.** No — an invitation to treat (*Boots*). The customer makes the offer
at the checkout; the operator accepts it.

**Q2.** A vendor's solicitor emails acceptance of an offer from Brisbane to
a buyer in Melbourne. Where and when is the contract formed?

**A.** Acceptance occurs when communicated, and the place of acceptance is
the place where the contract is formed. Under the *Electronic Transactions
Act 1999* (Cth) s 14 the time of dispatch is when the email leaves the
originator's information system.

**Q3.** Your uncle promises to give you his investment unit if you finish
your degree, and you do. Contract?

**A.** Domestic agreements are presumed not to be legally binding, but the
presumption is rebuttable — *Todd v Nicol* rebutted it where acceptance
required a substantial, detrimental change of position. Finishing a degree
you were already enrolled in is weaker than emigrating. Also fatal: a
contract for the disposition of land is unenforceable unless in writing and
signed (Topic 3, PLA s 7).

**Q4.** Your neighbour says "if you agree not to sue me over the fence, I
will pay for the survey." Is your promise consideration?

**A.** Yes. Consideration may be a promise **not** to do something,
including a promise not to sue in negligence.

## 1.3 Interpreting a contract

The court's job:

- Look for the **intention of the parties**.
- Courts will **not rectify errors or re-write** contracts.
- A contract is made up of **terms**, some more important than others.
- Terms must be present **when the parties agreed** — terms cannot be
  communicated retrospectively.
- Contracts do not have to be in writing, but it is prudent.

### The parol evidence rule

A written contract is treated as expressing the **complete** contract, so
external evidence of the parties' intention cannot be considered. The rule
excludes oral statements and additional terms.

**Exceptions:**

- Evidence of custom or trade usage
- The written contract was subject to an unfulfilled condition subsequent
- The contract was not wholly in writing
- Need to resolve **ambiguity** in language
- To identify the subject matter or a party to the contract
- The contract does not operate because of some defect
- It does not accurately reflect the intention of the parties

### Express and implied terms

**Express terms** are what was agreed, orally or in writing. Whether a
pre-contractual statement became a term is a question of fact.

**Implied terms** are not expressed but were intended to be part of the
contract. A court will imply a term to give **commercial efficacy** where:

- it is just and equitable to do so;
- the contract is ineffective without the term;
- it "goes without saying"; and
- it does not contradict the express terms.

A court will also imply a term by **custom or trade usage**, but only where
the custom is so well known that everyone making such a contract would be
presumed to have imported it. A term may be implied even if a party is
unaware of the custom — but never in contradiction of an express term.
Terms may also be implied **by statute** (in this unit, the PLA and the POA).

### Interpreting the words

Words are given their **ordinary meaning in the context of the contract at
the time of making the contract** — not a strained meaning.

**Exclusion clauses** get special treatment:

- Clauses that **deny facts** will be set aside.
- Courts are influenced by a party's **ability to look after themselves**.
- Where there is ambiguity, courts apply the **contra proferentem rule** —
  ambiguous terms are interpreted **against the party who drafted** the
  contract, which discourages vague or unfair drafting and matters most
  where bargaining power is unequal.
- Exclusion clauses are examined in light of the contract **as a whole**.
- Negligence *may* be covered by an exclusion clause, but **if the words of
  exclusion could reasonably be applied to protect against some other
  liability, negligence will not be covered.**

> **The negligence carve-out is the fiddly one.** A clause saying "the
> vendor accepts no liability for any loss" will be read as excluding the
> *other* liabilities it could plausibly cover, and therefore as **not**
> excluding negligence. To exclude negligence you must say "negligence".
> This is the drafting point behind *Butcher v Lachlan Elder Realty* in
> Topic 4, where a specific, prominent disclaimer worked precisely because
> it was specific about what the agent was and was not doing.

## 1.4 Apparent contracts — where agreement fails

Five vitiating factors: **misrepresentation, mistake, duress, undue
influence, unconscionable conduct.**

### Misrepresentation

A misrepresentation is a statement that is not true. Three kinds, with three
different remedies:

| Type | What it is | Remedy per the Week 1 deck |
|---|---|---|
| **Fraudulent** | Made deliberately | Avoid the contract |
| **Innocent** | Accidental but untrue | Rescission of the contract |
| **Negligent** | Breaches a duty of care | Sue in tort / negligence |

A misrepresentation may or may not be contractually significant.

> **The Week 1 slide's statutory reference is out of date.** It says the
> remedy for fraudulent misrepresentation includes "remedies under Trade
> Practises Act s.52". The *Trade Practices Act 1974* (Cth) was replaced by
> the *Competition and Consumer Act 2010* (Cth), and **s 52 TPA is now s 18
> of the Australian Consumer Law** in Schedule 2 — the Week 4 lecture says
> so expressly. Cite **ACL s 18**. A quiz answer citing "s 52 TPA" is
> citing a repealed provision.

### Mistake

Under common law, parties must live with their mistakes. Equity gives relief
on the ground that a mistake **prevented agreement**.

| Type | Definition | Effect |
|---|---|---|
| **Common** | Both parties mistaken about the **same** thing | If about the **existence** of the subject matter — no obligation to perform. If about the **quality** — the contract cannot be avoided, but equity may intervene |
| **Mutual** | Apparent agreement but no consensus; each party mistaken as to the **other's intention** | No agreement |
| **Unilateral** | One party mistaken as to the **terms or effect**; the other party **knows** of the mistake | Relief available |

> **Miscalculating your own investment returns is not a "mistake" in this
> sense.** All three categories concern mistakes about the *contract* — its
> subject matter, its terms, the other party's intention. A buyer who
> realises their yield spreadsheet was wrong has made a mistake about the
> **wisdom of the bargain**, which is exactly what "parties must live with
> their mistakes" means. They must complete.

### Duress

Agreement obtained through coercion — physical or emotional. Remedy is
**rescission**, voiding the contract.

### Undue influence

An equitable remedy where there is an **abuse of influence**. Undue
influence is **presumed** in certain relationships:

- Parent and child
- Guardian and ward
- Solicitor and client
- Trustee and beneficiary
- Doctor and patient
- Religious leader and disciple
- Any relationship of confidence

Where the presumption applies, the **stronger party must prove that undue
influence did not exist**. The court will set aside a contract affected by
undue influence. Legislative remedies are growing, particularly for the
elderly and those in care.

### Unconscionable conduct

The stronger party exploits a **special disadvantage** in a way that is
against good conscience. Equity intervenes — rescission.

Special disadvantage arises from: illness, ignorance, inexperience, impaired
faculties, financial need, language difficulty, emotional dependence.

The **weaker party** must show three things:

1. the special disadvantage existed;
2. it was **evident to the stronger party**; and
3. the weaker party was **unable to judge** whether the transaction was in
   their own best interest.

> **Undue influence and unconscionable conduct look alike and reverse the
> onus in opposite directions.** In undue influence with a presumed
> relationship, the **stronger** party must disprove it. In unconscionable
> conduct, the **weaker** party must prove all three limbs. Get the onus
> backwards and the whole answer collapses.

## 1.5 How contracts end

Six routes: **performance, agreement, frustration, illegality, termination,
rescission.**

**Performance** — the usual method. Common law requires strict and entire
performance, but:

- *De minimis non curat lex* — the law does not concern itself with trifles.
- **Substantial performance** may be enough.

**Agreement** — the parties can mutually agree to terminate and abandon
contractual rights, or agree to substitute a new contract for an existing
one.

**Frustration** — an intervening event that makes the agreed performance
impossible. The event must be **post-contractual**, must change the nature
of the contract, **neither party is responsible** for it, and it would be
unjust to hold the parties to the original bargain. The effect is to release
both parties from their obligations.

**Illegality** — a contract for an illegal purpose is unenforceable for
public policy reasons. Any contract to breach a statute is **void**.

**Breach:**

| | Actual breach | Anticipatory breach |
|---|---|---|
| What happens | Failure to perform | Indicated intention not to perform |
| Innocent party's position | Discharged from their obligations | May rescind |

And breach is graded by the importance of the term:

| Term breached | Remedy |
|---|---|
| **Condition** (essential term) | **Termination and damages** |
| **Warranty** (less important) | **Damages only** |

> **Condition versus warranty is the highest-value distinction in the whole
> topic.** It is what makes the Week 4 tutorial answer work: a buyer who
> tenders $100,000 short at settlement has breached an essential term, so
> the seller may terminate *and* claim damages. A buyer who complains about
> a scuff on a skirting board has at most a warranty claim, so the remedy is
> damages and they still have to settle. Substantial performance is the
> bridge — if the seller has substantially performed, the buyer must
> complete and sue separately.

## 1.6 Remedies

**Specific performance**

- An **equitable** remedy — at the discretion of the court.
- A court order to perform the obligations of the contract.
- Used when damages are an **inadequate or inappropriate** remedy.

Land is the classic case: every parcel is unique, so money is not an
adequate substitute for the parcel you bargained for.

**Damages**

- Compensation, **not punitive**.
- The innocent party is put into the position they would have been in **had
  the contract been performed**.
- Available for: loss of money, loss of business reputation, physical
  injury, and distress (**in a non-commercial contract**).
- The plaintiff is under a **duty to mitigate** loss.
- Damages are **assessed at the date of breach**.

> **Contract damages and tort damages point in opposite directions.** The
> Week 4 lecture makes the comparison explicit: contract puts you where you
> would have been had the contract been **performed** (expectation); tort
> puts you where you would have been had the tort **not occurred**
> (restoration). And the clocks differ — a contract action runs **from the
> breach**, a tort action **from the loss**. A question that gives you a
> date of breach and a later date of discovery is testing exactly this.

## Summary

- The Constitution creates three arms of government; the **judiciary makes
  common law** through precedent, and precedent binds only **down the same
  hierarchy** — NSW and US authority is persuasive at best.
- Civil standard is the **balance of probabilities**; criminal is **beyond
  reasonable doubt**.
- Formation needs **offer, acceptance, intention and consideration**, plus
  **capacity**. Displays are invitations to treat (*Boots*); advertisements
  can be unilateral offers (*Carlill*).
- Acceptance must be **unconditional and communicated**; silence is not
  acceptance.
- Consideration must be **sufficient, not adequate**, and never **past**.
  Without it, **equitable estoppel** (*Waltons Stores*) is the fallback.
- Written contracts are read via the **parol evidence rule**, with terms
  implied for **commercial efficacy** or by custom or statute. Ambiguity is
  read **contra proferentem**.
- Five vitiating factors. Watch the **onus**: presumed undue influence
  reverses it onto the stronger party; unconscionable conduct leaves it on
  the weaker party.
- Contracts end by performance, agreement, frustration, illegality,
  termination or rescission. **Breach of condition → terminate + damages;
  breach of warranty → damages only.**
- Remedies: **damages** (compensatory, mitigated, assessed at breach) and
  **specific performance** (equitable, discretionary, and the natural remedy
  for land).
