# TOPIC 2 — Agency Law and Real Estate Agents

Week 2. Why the law regulates acting through other people, the fiduciary
duties that flow from it, and then the specific rules the *Property
Occupations Act 2014* (Qld) imposes on real estate agents — listings,
commission, trust accounts and auctions.

## 2.1 Why agency exists

> "Due to the scale and modern business life it is necessary to conduct our
> business through others."

The law sets up rules around the practice to protect the parties. Those
rules cover four things, and they are the structure of the whole topic:

1. The **nature** of the relationship between Principal and Agent
2. The **significance of what can be done** in the relationship
3. The **fiduciary nature** of the relationship
4. **Termination** of the relationship

Three bodies of law apply at once:

- **Common law** — judge-made law
- **Contract law**
- **Relevant statutes** — for real estate agency practice in Queensland,
  the *Property Occupations Act 2014* (Qld)

## 2.2 Three types of agency

| Type | Scope | Example from the lecture |
|---|---|---|
| **Universal** | The agent has **unrestricted power** to act on behalf of the principal | A power of attorney |
| **General** | Power over **particular activities** — may be business only, and maybe only some business activities | A business accountant |
| **Special** | Power to act as agent for a **particular transaction** | A **real estate agent** — only specific powers to act in that sale or leasing transaction |

> **A real estate agent is a *special* agent, and that is the whole risk
> profile.** Their authority is confined to the particular transaction they
> were appointed for. Anything outside it — varying a price, agreeing to
> extend settlement, waiving a condition — is beyond their authority unless
> the appointment says otherwise. "The agent said it was fine" is not a
> defence when the agent had no power to say so.

### The real estate agent's position

- The RE agent is engaged as part of buying or selling land.
- The relationship is defined by **the agreement, the law of agency, and the
  Property Occupations Act**.
- **Most typically the seller engages the RE agent** to act as their agent.
- The RE agent will generally have **more contact with the buyers** than
  with the sellers.

That last point is the structural tension in the whole industry: the person
the agent talks to most is not the person the agent owes duties to.

### Is this person an agent?

| Role | Agent? |
|---|---|
| **Employee** | Not necessarily — but they **may be**, depending on the nature of the employment. If they enter into contracts or transactions on behalf of their principal, they are |
| **Independent contractor** | Depends on the arrangement. A contractor who is a house painter is unlikely to have any authority; there could be an agency component in other arrangements |
| **Partnership** | A partner can take the role of **agent of the firm** |
| **Company officer** | A company is no more than a collection of documents; people must act on its behalf. This takes the form of an **agency relationship** |

**Indicators of an agency relationship** — three diagnostic questions:

1. Does the agent keep all the profits?
2. Is the agent paid a **commission**?
3. Does the agent have an **obligation to account** to the principal for
   sales?

## 2.3 The fiduciary dimension

> "A relationship in which one party places special trust, confidence, and
> reliance in and is influenced by another who has a fiduciary duty to act
> for the benefit of the party."
> — *Merriam-Webster Legal Dictionary*, quoted in the Week 2 deck

### The six duties of an agent

An agent must:

1. Always act in the **Principal's best interest**
2. Never accept payment from another party (**secret commissions**)
3. Give **personal attention** to the Principal
4. **Follow the Principal's instructions**
5. Exercise **due care, skill and diligence**
6. **Account** for all transactions

Taken one at a time:

**1. Act in the Principal's best interests.** The agent must never allow the
duty to the principal and their own personal interest to conflict. **Even a
perception of conflict is a problem, even if the conflict never
eventuates.** Trade *for* a client, not in competition with them. The
lecture refers to the RICS guidance on what counts as a conflict and how it
must be handled.

**2. Never accept payment from another party.** Not without the principal's
consent. The ultimate breach of fiduciary duty is a **secret commission**,
which is also covered by the *Criminal Code Act 1899* (Qld). **It is
irrelevant whether the agent was actually influenced by the payment.**

Where a secret commission is taken, the principal may:

- **terminate** the contract and not pay commission;
- **continue** with the transaction, and the agent must pay the commission
  over to the principal; or
- treat the transaction as **void *ab initio***.

The principal may also **sue the agent and the third party** if the
transaction was altered by the secret commission.

> **"It is not relevant whether or not the agent was influenced by this
> payment" is the sentence that wins the secret-commission question.** You
> do not have to prove the bribe changed the advice. The taking of an
> undisclosed benefit is itself the breach. That is why disclosure cures it
> entirely — a *disclosed* commission consented to by the principal is not a
> secret commission at all.

**3. Give personal attention.** Agency is a **personal contract**, so the
agent must act in person — deemed to be the licence holder (the licensee).
The agent **cannot delegate responsibility**, but may delegate the **work or
activities** of the agency agreement to an employee.

**4. Follow instructions.** The authority given to an agent is **limited**.
An agent cannot bind the principal beyond that limit. Identifying the
**scope of the appointment** is critical.

**5. Due care, skill and diligence.** Generally the agent is under **no
special** duty of care, skill and diligence — the usual standards apply. The
**exception** is where the agent **professes to have special skills and is
engaged for that purpose** (a tax accountant, for instance), in which case
the standard rises to match the profession claimed.

**6. Account for all transactions.** Agents must keep **separate accounts**
so they can account for transactions relevant to the principal. **This is
why agents keep trust accounts.**

*Farah Constructions Pty Ltd v Say-Dee Pty Ltd* (2007) 230 CLR 89. A joint
venture between Farah and Say-Dee to redevelop a property; Farah was
responsible for obtaining development approval and acquired information
about the potential to acquire adjacent properties. The High Court held
Farah **did not** breach its fiduciary duty by failing to disclose the
opportunity to acquire the adjacent properties, and clarified recipient
liability under *Barnes v Addy*: it **rejected a restitutionary / unjust
enrichment basis** for knowing receipt, and emphasised that the recipient
must have acted **dishonestly or fraudulently**. The organising question is
whether there was **informed consent** to what occurred.

## 2.4 How an agency relationship is created

Five routes. This is a standard MCQ.

| Route | How it works |
|---|---|
| **Expressly** | By agreement — a deed, written contract, or verbal contract; or by a positive act that invests the agent with authority to bind the principal |
| **Impliedly** | Implied authority alongside actual authority, arising through necessity or legal presumption |
| **Estoppel** | A person's conduct creates the impression of an agency relationship |
| **Ratification** | Retrospective authorisation of conduct |
| **Necessity** | Implied authority arising from the circumstances |

**Estoppel and ostensible authority.** Ostensible (or apparent) authority
is where a **third party reasonably believes** an agent has authority, even
though the agent has no actual authority. Critically, the belief must be
**based on the principal's actions or representations — not on the agent's
own claims.** If the principal's conduct leads a third party reasonably to
conclude the agent has power to act, the principal can be bound.

**Ratification.** Occurs where the agent has **exceeded** the scope or
existing limits of authority and the principal **takes a benefit** from the
transaction. Once ratified, it is as though the activities were always
within the agent's authority.

**Necessity.** Arises where someone needs to **preserve property or
safeguard the interests of another** and it is **not possible to get
authorisation**.

> **Ostensible authority is created by the principal, never by the agent.**
> This is the point that decides the receptionist limb of the Week 4
> tutorial. A receptionist who says "that should be OK" is making a claim
> about her own authority — that is precisely what cannot found ostensible
> authority. You would need a representation *by the firm* that receptionists
> may vary contracts, and no firm makes one.

## 2.5 The agent's rights

Three: **remuneration, indemnity, lien.**

**Remuneration.** Because of the fiduciary relationship, the strict legal
position is that the agent **cannot be paid unless the contract specifically
provides**. However, when a **professional** agent is engaged, the
presumption is that payment should be made **on completion of the duties**.
An agent is entitled to be **reimbursed for expenses irrespective of whether
the agency is gratuitous**.

**Indemnity.** Where an agent has incurred liabilities on behalf of a
principal **while acting in the course of an authorised transaction**, the
agent has a right to be relieved of those liabilities, and indemnity from
legal action taken while acting on behalf of the principal.

**Lien.** Where an agent is in **possession of goods belonging to the
principal**, those goods may be the subject of a lien — the agent may use
them as security to cover the principal's unpaid debts. The Week 3 tutorial
adds that this is a **general law right that has not been abrogated by the
POA**, and that it extends to property, goods, documents or money held on
behalf of the principal.

## 2.6 Who is liable when it goes wrong

### Fraud and the disclosure ladder

The test is what the agent disclosed about the principal:

| What the agent disclosed | Who the third party can sue |
|---|---|
| **Existence and name** of the principal | **P** liable, not A |
| **Existence but not the identity** of the principal | **P** liable, not A |
| **Existence not disclosed at all** | Third party may sue **P or A** |

The agent is separately liable **to the principal** if they breach the
agency agreement, breach their fiduciary duty, or are negligent.

### Misrepresentation by the agent

- **Fraudulent misrepresentation:** P is liable if P **sanctioned** the
  fraud; otherwise P could argue the fraud was **outside the scope** of the
  agency agreement.
- If the misrepresentation was **within the scope of the authority**, the
  third party may sue **P**.
- If the agent **breaches the Australian Consumer Law, P is also liable** —
  *Guthrie v Metro Ford Pty Ltd*.
- If the agent **fails to pass on money** to P, usually there is **no need
  for the third party to repay** the money.

> **Two of the three sit together, and the middle one is the trap.** The
> principal is liable where they sanctioned the fraud (a) **and** where the
> conduct fell within the agent's authority (c) — but not where the fraud
> was outside the scope (b). The MCQ answer is "(a) and (c) only", not "all
> of the above".

### Termination of the agency relationship

- Agreement
- Performance of the agency, or end of its duration
- Revocation
- **Death, insanity, bankruptcy**
- Frustration or subsequent illegality

## 2.7 Real estate agents and the Property Occupations Act 2014

The real estate industry is regulated by the **POA 2014**, which regulates
**auctioneers, real estate agents and residential letting agents**. An agent
must be appointed using **Form 6**.

### What changed when the POA came in

| Change | Detail |
|---|---|
| Licensing | **Property developers no longer need a licence** |
| Auctioneers | Need an **auctioneer's licence** to hold auctions; "trainee auctioneer" is no longer a category; **no trainee period** required |
| Display | No longer need to display your licence in your place of business; no longer need a sign with your name and status as licensee |
| But | **Still must display the licence at the site of an auction** and show it to any client who requests it |
| Exemption | **No licence needed if you are the owner of a large-scale non-residential property** (e.g. a shopping centre), or an organisation managing property it owns |
| Place of business | Either a **licensee or a registered salesperson** can be in charge (except the registered office of the principal licensee) |
| Records | **No employment register** required |
| Commission | **Deregulated** — negotiable with the client; **no requirement to disclose to the seller how much commission is paid**; will be either a set percentage or a set price |
| Exclusive agency | Maximum term **90 days, not 60**, and **can be terminated after 60 days** |

> **The 90/60 pair is two different numbers doing two different jobs.**
> 90 days is the **maximum term** the appointment can run. 60 days is the
> point **after which the seller may terminate**. So the seller is locked in
> for 60 days and has a 30-day terminable tail. A question that asks "how
> long is the seller committed for?" wants **60**, not 90.

### Types of listing

| Listing | Entitlement to commission | POA |
|---|---|---|
| **Open** | Agent must be the **effective cause of sale** | s 20 |
| **Sole agency** | Agent is entitled **unless the seller was the effective cause of the sale** | s 23 |
| **Exclusive agency** | Agent is entitled **whether or not the agent was the effective cause of the sale** | s 23 |

Section 23 POA, quoted in full in the Week 3 tutorial, spells this out with
a worked example. S owns a residence and appoints R to sell it. B buys it.
Depending on who was the effective cause:

| Case | Effective cause of sale | Exclusive agency: R paid? | Sole agency: R paid? |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | R (the appointed agent) | Yes | Yes |
| 2 | X (another property agent) | Yes | Yes |
| 3 | M (S's mother) | Yes | Yes |
| 4 | **S (the seller)** | **Yes** | **No** |

> **The lecture's one-line description of sole versus exclusive agency does
> not match the section the tutorial quotes.** The Week 2 lecture and the
> Week 3 tutorial both say the difference "is that under exclusive agency
> there is a prohibition on the vendor from selling their own property".
> Section 23(1) says the opposite: *"The only difference between an
> exclusive agency and a sole agency is the extent of the entitlement of a
> selling agent to receive an agreed commission."* Nothing in s 23 prohibits
> the vendor from selling; it just means that under an **exclusive** agency
> the vendor pays commission anyway, and under a **sole** agency the vendor
> who is the effective cause does not. Learn the section, not the slide, and
> ask Chris to confirm before the quiz.

### Effective cause of sale

There must be a **causal connection between the agency and the sale**,
determined on the facts. The Week 3 tutorial adds the extension that matters:

> Where an agent introduces a purchaser to a property and the initial
> negotiations do not ripen into a contract, **if the property is sold by
> the vendor or another agent on substantially the same terms some time
> later, the first agent may still be the effective cause of sale.** It
> depends on the events that have occurred since the initial introduction.

Authority: *Century 21 Plateau Lifestyle Real Estate v Hall* [2020] NSWCA.

### Commission and reimbursement — ss 88–91 POA

- Under general law an agent has a right to be reimbursed for **expenses or
  losses suffered for acts wholly within the terms of the agency**.
- The agent may draw transaction expenses from money held in the agent's or
  auctioneer's **trust account** — with a duty to **account and repay excess**.
- An agent is **not** entitled to reimbursement for activities **outside the
  scope** of the appointment.
- The agent also has a right to a **lien**.

**An agent may sue for commission and expenses provided that:**

1. the agent is **licensed**;
2. the agent's **appointment to act is signed and in the correct form**
   (Form 6); and
3. the type of listing supports the entitlement.

Commission is **lost** for breach of duty — and the agent may also be liable
in damages.

> **Points 1 and 2 are threshold conditions, not factors to weigh.** An
> unlicensed agent, or an agent without a signed Form 6, cannot sue for
> commission at all, no matter how clearly they were the effective cause.
> Check them first in any problem question — if either fails, the answer is
> short.

## 2.8 Auctions

- Before the auction, potential purchasers are advised of the conditions of
  sale and whether the property is subject to a **reserve**.
- **The contract is formed at the fall of the hammer.** It is **not**
  conditional on signing the contract or paying deposit monies.
- The **auctioneer may sign the contract on behalf of the purchaser**,
  unless they are selling their own property.

**The auctioneer's authority.** An auctioneer is an **agent for the vendor**
and is bound by the authority the vendor gives them. An auctioneer has **no
authority to sell below the reserve** — but if they mistakenly do, **they
will bind their principal** and will be **liable to the principal for the
amount below the reserve**.

Under the POA, agents **can state that a reserve price exists** for a
residential auction but **must not state what the reserve price is**.

> **"Binds the principal but is liable to the principal" is the sentence to
> memorise.** The buyer gets their contract — the vendor cannot unwind the
> sale because the auctioneer went under the reserve. The vendor's remedy is
> against the auctioneer, for the shortfall. Both halves must be in the
> answer.

**Q1.** An agent sells a client's warehouse and separately receives a
"marketing rebate" from the buyer's broker. The agent genuinely got the
client the best price on the market. Breach?

**A.** Yes. The agent must never accept payment from another party without
the principal's consent, and **it is irrelevant whether the agent was
influenced**. The principal may terminate and refuse commission, or continue
and require the agent to hand over the payment, or treat the transaction as
void *ab initio* — and may sue the agent and the third party if the
transaction was altered by it. Also a *Criminal Code Act 1899* (Qld) issue.

**Q2.** A vendor under an **exclusive** agency sells the property herself to
a neighbour. Does the agent get paid?

**A.** Yes. Under an exclusive agency the agent is entitled to commission
**whether or not the agent was the effective cause of the sale** — s 23(2)
POA, case 4 in the statutory example.

**Q3.** Same facts, but a **sole** agency.

**A.** No. Under a sole agency the agent is not entitled where **the seller
was the effective cause** — s 23(3), case 4.

**Q4.** An auctioneer, distracted, knocks a property down at $20,000 below
the vendor's reserve. What happens?

**A.** The sale stands — the auctioneer's mistake **binds the principal**,
and the contract formed at the fall of the hammer. The vendor's remedy is
against the auctioneer, who is **liable to the principal for the amount
below the reserve** ($20,000).

**Q5.** A sales assistant with no authority tells a buyer the vendor will
throw in the ride-on mower. The vendor has never held the assistant out as
having that power. Bound?

**A.** No. Ostensible authority must be based on **the principal's** actions
or representations, **not the agent's own claims**. Without ratification —
the vendor taking a benefit from the varied transaction — there is nothing
to bind them.

## Summary

- Agency is regulated by **common law, contract law and the POA 2014**
  simultaneously. A real estate agent is a **special agent** — authority
  confined to the one transaction.
- Six fiduciary duties. The examinable ones are **no secret commissions**
  (influence is irrelevant; disclosure cures it) and **accounting**, which
  is why trust accounts exist.
- Agency arises **expressly, impliedly, by estoppel, by ratification, or by
  necessity**. Ostensible authority comes from the **principal's** conduct.
- The agent's rights are **remuneration, indemnity and lien**.
- Liability for the agent's fraud depends on **what was disclosed about the
  principal**, and on whether the conduct was **within the scope**. P is
  liable where P sanctioned it *or* it was within scope.
- Listings: **open** — effective cause required; **sole** — paid unless the
  *seller* was the effective cause; **exclusive** — paid regardless. Max
  term 90 days, terminable after 60.
- No commission without a **licence** and a **signed Form 6**.
- At auction the contract forms **at the fall of the hammer**. An auctioneer
  selling under reserve **binds the vendor** and **owes the vendor the
  shortfall**.
