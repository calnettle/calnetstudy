## Week 2 — the legal system and contract formation

### Australian legal system questions

**1. What role do judges play in the development of the law?**

Judges make **common law**. In deciding a case they state the *ratio
decidendi*, which under the doctrine of **precedent** binds lower courts in
the same hierarchy on materially similar facts. They also **interpret
statute** — the meaning Parliament's words carry in practice is the meaning
the courts give them. Judges do not legislate at large: they decide the
dispute in front of them, and the law develops incrementally, case by case.

**2. Would the Supreme Court of Queensland be bound by a decision of the
Supreme Court of NSW?**

**No.** Precedent binds only **down the same hierarchy**. The Supreme Court
of NSW is in a different State hierarchy, so its decisions are
**persuasive** only. The Queensland Supreme Court **is** bound by the **High
Court of Australia**, which sits above every State and federal court.

**3. What is the ultimate law-making body in Australia?**

**Parliament** — the legislature. Statute prevails over common law: where an
Act and a judge-made rule conflict, the Act wins, and Parliament can abolish
or rewrite a common law rule at will. Within its heads of power under the
Constitution, the **Commonwealth Parliament** prevails over State Parliaments
where the two are inconsistent.

**4. What institutions were created by the Constitution?**

The **three arms of government**: the **Parliament** (the legislature), the
**Executive**, and the **Judiciary** (the High Court and the federal
courts). The Constitution also created the **federation** itself, dividing
power between the Commonwealth and the States.

**5. What political institution exercises the executive power under the
Constitution?**

Formally the executive power is vested in the Crown and exercised by the
**Governor-General**. In political practice it is exercised by the
**Executive Council / Cabinet** — the ministry, drawn from the party with a
majority in the lower house.

> **The trap in questions 3 and 5 is the word "ultimate".** People answer
> "the High Court" to question 3 because the High Court is the final court
> of appeal. It is the final word on **what the law means**, not on **what
> the law is** — Parliament can pass an Act tomorrow that overrides any High
> Court decision (short of a constitutional one). Court hierarchy and
> legislative supremacy are two different ladders.

### Contract questions

**1. Your baby sister takes a chocolate from the shelf and eats most of it.
The checkout operator says you must pay. What do you do?**

Run formation. A **display of goods is an invitation to treat**, not an
offer (*Pharmaceutical Society of Great Britain v Boots*). The offer is made
by the customer at the till and accepted by the operator. You never offered
to buy the chocolate, so **there is no contract for the chocolate** and no
contractual obligation to pay for it. The baby cannot contract either — an
infant lacks **capacity** and there is no intention to create legal
relations.

The store's real complaint is not contractual: the goods were still the
store's property and have been consumed. Its remedy, if it pursued one, lies
in **tort** (damage to or conversion of goods). In practice, you pay.

> **Answer the question that was asked, in two halves.** "What do you do" is
> a practical question; "are you legally obliged" is the legal one, and the
> answers differ. A response that only says "I would pay" scores nothing on
> the law, and a response that only says "no contract, walk away" misses
> that the store is not without a remedy.

**2. Could a parent sue a child for not feeding and walking the dog when
they promised they would?**

**No.** **Domestic and social agreements are presumed not to be intended to
create legal relations** (*Balfour v Balfour*). The presumption can be
rebutted where the arrangement is formal, commercial in character, or the
parties are estranged — but a chores promise inside an intact household will
not rebut it. There is also no consideration moving from the parent.

**3. At what stage do negotiations become a contract?**

When an **offer** is met by **unconditional acceptance communicated to the
offeror**, and the other elements are present: **intention** to create legal
relations, **consideration**, and **capacity**. Everything before that is
invitation to treat, negotiation, counter-offer or a request for
information — a counter-offer **kills** the original offer. For a contract
for the **disposition of land**, formation is not enough for enforcement:
the contract must also be **in writing and signed by the party to be
charged** — **PLA 2023 s 7**.

**4. Is there any way to assert contractual rights where you have not
provided consideration?**

**Yes**, two ways:

- a promise made in a **deed** (under seal) is enforceable without
  consideration; and
- **promissory estoppel** — where a clear promise was made, the promisee
  **relied** on it to their detriment and it would be **unconscionable** to
  allow the promisor to resile. See Topic 1, §1.2.

**5. You buy a property expecting it to appreciate, then realise before
paying that you miscalculated. Can you refuse to go further?**

**No.** This is a mistake about **the wisdom of the bargain**, not about the
subject matter, the terms, or the other party's intention — so it is not
common, mutual or unilateral mistake, and equity gives no relief. At common
law **parties must live with their mistakes**. Refusing to complete is
repudiation: the seller may terminate, forfeit the deposit and sue, or seek
**specific performance**, which is readily available for land because every
parcel is unique.

## Week 3 — Samantha, Steve and the sole agency

**The facts.** Samantha owns and lives in a house at Windsor and wants to
sell. She consults **Steve**, a local agent, signs **an authority to sell**
at listing, and Steve is appointed under a **sole agency**. Steve has the
house photographed, puts an advertisement in his agency window, advertises
on a popular real estate website, and erects a **'for sale' sign** at the
front of the property.

**Georgina** drives past, sees Samantha in the front garden, and approaches
her about the house. Samantha invites her to inspect, and they agree a
price. Georgina then calls her brother **Daniel**, an agent in Gympie, who
acts as **stakeholder**, prepares the contract and holds the deposit in his
trust account.

*Advise Steve whether he is entitled to commission and reimbursement for
expenses.*

### Issue 1 — Is Steve entitled to commission under the sole agency listing?

```
I  Is Steve entitled to commission under the sole agency listing?

S  Where the owner has been involved in the sale and the listing is a SOLE
   agency, the agent is entitled to commission if the agent — not the
   seller — was the 'effective cause of sale'. Threshold conditions apply
   first: the agent must be licensed and the appointment must be signed and
   in the approved form.

A  Property Occupations Act 2014 (Qld) s 23, and ss 88–91 for the right to
   sue. Effective cause of sale: Century 21 Plateau Lifestyle Real Estate
   v Hall [2020] NSWCA.

A  (below)

C  (below)
```

**State the law, in the section's own words.** Section 23(1) POA:

> The **only** difference between an exclusive agency and a sole agency is
> the extent of the entitlement of a selling agent to receive an agreed
> commission or other reward on the sale of particular property.

Section 23(2): under an **exclusive** agency the agent is entitled **whether
or not** the agent was the effective cause. Section 23(3): under a **sole**
agency the agent is **not** entitled **if the seller was the effective
cause**. The statutory example runs four cases — the agent R, another agent
X, the seller's mother M, and the seller S. Under an exclusive agency R is
paid in **all four**. Under a sole agency R is paid in **cases 1, 2 and 3**
but **not case 4**, where the seller was the effective cause.

**Application.** Was Steve the effective cause of the sale?

For Steve:

- Georgina learned the property was for sale from Steve's **marketing** —
  the sign at the front, the window advertisement and the web listing. But
  for that marketing there was nothing to approach Samantha about.
- There is **no break in the causal chain**: the approach, the inspection
  and the agreed price all happened in one continuous sequence, on
  substantially the terms Steve was marketing.
- The tutorial's own extension of the doctrine supports him: even where an
  agent's introduction does **not** ripen into a contract, if the property
  later sells **on substantially the same terms**, the first agent may still
  be the effective cause. Steve's case is stronger than that — the sale
  followed immediately.

For Samantha:

- She personally conducted the inspection and personally negotiated the
  price. She, not Steve, closed it.

The better view is that **Steve was the effective cause** and Samantha's
involvement was in the closing, not in producing the buyer. Under s 23(3)
Steve therefore keeps his commission — this is **case 1**, not case 4.

**Threshold conditions.** Before any of that matters, check the two gates:
Steve must be **licensed**, and his appointment must be **signed and in the
approved form (Form 6)**. The facts say Samantha "signs an authority to
sell at the stage of listing", which is not the same words as a completed
Form 6. **Flag it** — if the appointment is not in the approved form, Steve
cannot sue for commission at all, however clearly he was the effective
cause.

**Conclusion.** Steve is entitled to commission at the rate in the
appointment, subject to being licensed and validly appointed. Daniel is
entitled to nothing: he had **no appointment from the seller**, and
preparing the contract raises a separate question under the *Legal
Profession Act 2007* (Qld) s 24.

> **Do not write "Samantha sold it herself, so no commission".** That is the
> answer to an *exclusive* agency question read backwards, and it skips the
> only thing s 23(3) asks: **who was the effective cause**. Selling from
> your own front garden a property that an agent's sign, window card and web
> listing brought a buyer to is not being the effective cause. Reserve
> "case 4" for facts where the seller genuinely produced the buyer — an old
> friend, an unsolicited approach, a private ad the seller ran.

### Issue 2 — Is Steve entitled to payment for expenses incurred?

```
I  Is Steve entitled to reimbursement for the expenses he incurred?

S  Under the general law an agent has a right to be reimbursed for expenses
   or losses suffered for acts WHOLLY WITHIN the terms of the agency. There
   is no right to reimbursement for activities outside the scope of the
   appointment. The agent also has a right to a lien.

A  POA ss 88–91; general law of agency, not abrogated by the POA.

A  Photography, the window advertisement, the web listing and the sign are
   all squarely within a selling appointment, so all are recoverable — if
   the appointment authorised the marketing spend. Form 6 records the
   agreed marketing expenditure, which is why the form matters twice.

C  Steve may recover his marketing costs. He may draw them from money held
   in his own trust account, with a duty to account for and repay any
   excess — but he holds none, because Daniel took the deposit into the
   Gympie agency's trust account.
```

**The lien.** The speaker note asks what Steve would take a lien over. The
general law right is to **retain property, goods, documents or money held on
behalf of the principal** until remuneration or expenses are paid. Steve
holds the keys, the photographs and whatever documents he was given — but
**not the deposit**. That is the practical sting of Daniel's involvement:
Steve's lien is close to worthless, and he will have to sue.

> **Commission and expenses are separate entitlements with separate tests.**
> Commission depends on the **type of listing** and, for open and sole
> agencies, on **effective cause**. Reimbursement depends only on whether
> the expense was **within the scope of the appointment**. It is perfectly
> possible to lose the commission and still recover the marketing spend, so
> answer them in two paragraphs, not one.

## Week 4 — Frank, Alex and the standard REIQ contract

**The facts.** Frank contracts to buy a house at Wilston from Alex on the
standard REIQ contract on **5 June 2024** (a Wednesday). The next day Alex
decides he does not want to sell — the house has sentimental value — and is
desperate to terminate. Settlement is set for **5 July 2024** (a Friday),
30 days later. Before settlement:

1. On **7 June** Frank engages a building inspector. **Alex refuses access.**
   Frank still wants to proceed but **does not tell Alex's solicitor**.
2. On **10 June** Alex has a party. The BBQ explodes and **destroys a
   significant portion of the back of the house**. Alex is unhurt.
3. Frank has **no insurance** over the property and wants to cut the price
   by **$100,000**. He telephones Alex's solicitor, who is unavailable, and
   speaks to **Sally the receptionist**, who says: *"that should be OK but I
   will have to speak to my boss. If it is not OK we will let you know."*
   Sally forgets to pass it on.

On 5 July Frank arrives at settlement on time with a cheque for the full
amount **less $100,000**. *Advise Alex of his rights.*

> **The deck says "let's look to the standard form contract" and then does
> not reproduce a single clause.** The REIQ contract is in the tutorial
> folder, not in the slides. Everything below is the **effect** of the
> standard conditions; **read the clause numbers off the contract itself
> before you cite any in an assessment.** Citing a clause number you have
> not read is worse than citing none.

### Element 1 — the refused inspection

Two separate defaults, pointing in opposite directions.

**Alex's default.** A building and pest condition is a condition for the
**buyer's benefit**, and it carries a correlative obligation on the seller
to give the inspector **reasonable access**. Refusing access is a **breach
by Alex**, and it is not cured by his motive.

**Frank's default.** The condition requires the buyer to **notify** by the
inspection date — satisfied, waived, or terminated. Frank decided to proceed
and **told no one**. On its face that exposes him to the seller's right to
terminate for want of notice while the notice remains outstanding.

**Why Alex still cannot use it.** A party cannot rely on their own breach to
trigger a right. **Alex's refusal of access is the reason there was nothing
to notify about.** He also cannot terminate under a condition inserted
solely for the buyer's benefit. So element 1 gives Alex nothing, and gives
Frank a complaint he did not press.

### Element 2 — the fire, and who should have insured

**Who should have insurance? The buyer.** Under the REIQ standard contract
the property is at the **buyer's risk from 5pm on the first business day
after the contract date**:

```
Contract date                    Wednesday 5 June 2024
First business day after         Thursday  6 June 2024
Risk passes                      5pm Thursday 6 June 2024
BBQ explosion                    Monday   10 June 2024   ← 4 days after risk passed
Settlement                       Friday    5 July 2024   (30 days after contract)
```

This is the "**TRANSFER OF RISK**" label the Week 4 deck attaches to stage 3
of the transaction process. From that moment Frank should have had the
property insured; he did not, and **an uninsured buyer bearing the risk is a
buyer who bears the loss**.

**Should the price be reduced by $100,000? No.** There is no right to a
unilateral abatement of the price for damage occurring after risk has
passed. Frank must complete at the contract price.

Two qualifications, both worth marks:

- **A statutory right to terminate, not to discount.** Where a dwelling
  house is so damaged before completion as to be **unfit for occupation**,
  the buyer has a statutory right to **rescind by notice before
  completion** — the provision under the old Act was *Property Law Act 1974*
  (Qld) **s 64**, and it has been carried into the *Property Law Act 2023*.
  **Confirm the current section number with Chris before citing it**; the
  decks never mention this provision, and the 2023 renumbering is exactly
  where these notes have already caught the lecturer's slides out (s 59 →
  s 7). Whether "a significant portion of the back of the house" makes the
  house unfit for occupation is a **question of fact**. Note what the right
  is: **terminate the whole contract**, not knock $100,000 off it.
- **Alex caused the damage himself.** He was cooking on the BBQ that
  exploded. A risk clause allocates the risk of **casualty**; it is a
  different question whether it protects a seller against the consequences
  of the seller's **own negligence**, and the seller is separately obliged
  to deliver the property in essentially the condition it was in at the
  contract date. Frank has an arguable claim for the damage — but it is a
  **claim**, to be made and quantified, not a self-help deduction at
  settlement. **Raise it and flag it as arguable; ask Chris how far he wants
  it taken.**

### Element 3 — Sally the receptionist

Three independent reasons the $100,000 variation was never agreed. Any one
is enough; write all three.

1. **No authority.** Sally is a receptionist. She has no **actual**
   authority to vary a contract on behalf of the firm's client, and no
   **ostensible** authority either — ostensible authority must arise from
   **the principal's** conduct or representations, not from the purported
   agent's own words. Nothing Alex or his solicitor did held Sally out as
   able to agree a six-figure price reduction. Nor has Alex **ratified**
   anything; he never learned of it.
2. **No acceptance.** Even taken at face value, *"that should be OK but I
   will have to speak to my boss"* is **conditional**. Acceptance must be
   **unconditional and communicated**. "If it is not OK we will let you
   know" is an attempt to make **silence** operate as acceptance, and
   **silence is not acceptance**.
3. **Not in writing.** A contract for the disposition of land — and a
   variation of it — must be **in writing and signed by the party to be
   charged**: **PLA 2023 s 7**. A telephone call to a receptionist satisfies
   neither limb.

### Conclusion — advise Alex

Frank tendered a cheque **$100,000 short** at settlement. Under the standard
contract **time is of the essence**, and tendering less than the balance
purchase price is a **failure to complete**, which is a breach of an
essential term entitling the innocent party to terminate. Alex may:

- **terminate** the contract;
- **forfeit the deposit**, subject to the deposit not being a penalty;
- **sue for damages** — assessed at the date of breach, with a duty to
  mitigate; or
- seek **specific performance**, which is the usual remedy for land — though
  Alex is the last person who would want it.

The result is the tutorial's joke: Alex spent a month desperate to escape
the contract, and Frank handed him the exit at settlement.

> **Do not let Alex's own bad behaviour dissolve the answer.** His refusal
> of access was a breach and his BBQ caused the damage, and both give Frank
> arguments. Neither of them **caused** Frank to tender a short cheque —
> that was Frank's own choice, made in reliance on a receptionist. A
> defaulting seller can still terminate for a buyer's *unrelated* essential
> breach. Keep the two chains of causation separate.

## Week 5 — Angie, Brad and the beach views

**The facts.** Angie and Brad fly in from the USA to buy a house at Mermaid
Beach, offered by **private treaty**. They inspect and sign an offer on the
spot, which the seller accepts later that day. The sellers make the
disclosures required by the *Property Law Act* — title, survey plan, pool
safety compliance and other certifications.

**Jason** is the real estate agent. The house is one block from the beach
but has excellent beach views from the second level, because the adjoining
allotment with beach frontage is **vacant but well landscaped**. Angie says
to Jason:

> "look at the amazing beach views. This is exactly what I am looking for.
> That must be a Gold Coast City Council park on the beach front. That's
> great news for us because it will never be developed and we will always
> have beach views."

**Jason remains silent** and redirects the conversation to the BBQ area on
the back deck. Angie asks about the **history of the house**; Jason says he
has no further information.

**The reality.** At the pre-settlement conference a neighbour remarks: *"I
am very happy that a family is moving into the house. The previous
neighbours were so quiet. We didn't even realise what was going on in the
house."* Angie googles the address and finds the house was the site of
**horrific murders**. On the council website she also finds a **development
approval** over the "parkland" — the beach views will be lost entirely.

### Issue 1 — Was Jason under a duty to disclose the stigmatised history?

**The lecturer's own answer, from the slide:**

*Caveat emptor* means buyers are responsible for their own investigations.
Certain disclosures are required by the **Property Law Act 2023**, but **the
requirement does not include any requirement to report murders or other
similar events**. The position is different in **NSW**, where disclosure of
these events is required following the ***Hinton*** decision.

**Based on the disclosures required under the PLA in Queensland, Jason was
under no duty to disclose the history. Angie and Brad cannot terminate the
contract on that basis.**

Two further points the notes add: Jason said he "didn't have any further
information", which — if he in fact knew — is not silence but a **false
statement**, and that changes the analysis entirely (see the callout below).
And there is **no requirement in the POA** to disclose a property's history
either; a stigma claim in Queensland must be run through **consumer
protection legislation**, where it is a **question of fact** whether silence
breaches the Act.

### Issue 2 — Does silence about the DA amount to fraudulent misrepresentation or a breach of ACL s 18?

**The law, from the slide.** **Silence, where there is an obligation to
correct a mistruth and the buying party was relying on the information, can
be a breach of s 18 of the ACL.**

**Authority.** ***Demagogue v Ramensky*** — failure to disclose something
that ought to be disclosed can constitute misleading and deceptive conduct.

**Application.** Angie stated, in Jason's hearing, three things: that the
land was **council parkland**, that it **would never be developed**, and
that they would **always have beach views**. Every one was wrong, and Jason
— a local agent — would know the adjoining beachfront block was privately
owned and had a development approval over it. He did not correct her; he
**changed the subject**, which is conduct suggesting he registered the
statement and chose to move past it. Angie made plain she was **relying** on
the point: *"this is exactly what I am looking for."* The conduct was
plainly **in trade or commerce**. On those facts there was a **reasonable
expectation of disclosure** and the silence is **misleading or deceptive
conduct** under **ACL s 18**.

Whether it is also **fraudulent misrepresentation** is harder: that requires
a false statement of fact made knowingly, without belief in its truth, or
recklessly. Jason made no statement about the block at all. The stronger
argument runs through **half-truth by conduct** — allowing a false
impression that he knew to be false to stand and be acted on. The lecturer's
own note says the consumer law is used frequently in claims like this **but
to discuss whether fraudulent misrepresentation could also be made out**, so
argue both and say which is stronger.

**Conclusion — what can Angie and Brad do?**

- **Terminate?** Not for the stigma. Possibly for the s 18 conduct — the
  court's power under the ACL extends to declaring the whole or part of a
  contract void and to varying it.
- **Damages** under the ACL, measured by the difference between what they
  paid and what the property was worth given the DA.
- **Sue the agent personally.** Section 18 binds "a person", and the agency
  as principal is liable for an agent's ACL breach — *Guthrie v Metro Ford*
  (Topic 2, §2.6).
- **Complain to the Office of Fair Trading**, which enforces the ACL in
  Queensland, and to the POA regulator about Jason's conduct.

> **Split the two issues by the source of the duty, or you will merge them
> and lose both.** The murders fail because **no statute requires
> disclosure** and no one asked a question Jason answered falsely on the
> known facts. The DA succeeds because a **buyer's own false statement was
> left uncorrected** — the duty comes from the **circumstances**, not from a
> disclosure list. Same agent, same conversation, opposite outcomes, and the
> reason is which body of law creates the obligation.

> lettered **(b)** there and **(a)** here. The proposition is what to learn:
> **a deposit of more than 10% of the price** converts the contract into an
> **instalment contract** (20% for a **proposed lot**). "More than 5%" is a
> distractor in both decks and is never right.

## Week 6 — leases

The Week 6 tutorial is a document exercise: read a real commercial lease and
answer questions off it. The lease itself is in the tutorial folder and is
not reproduced in the slides, so what follows is the **vocabulary** — which
is what the quiz can test — and **where in a lease to look** for each of the
ten questions.

### Lease key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Lessor** | The entity of the **owner** — the exact legal entity, not the trading name |
| **Lessee** | The entity of the **tenant** — again, the entity that signs |
| **Premises** | The area available for the **exclusive use** of the tenant |
| **Area** | Measured in accordance with the **Property Council Method of Measurement** |
| **Lease commencement** | Start date of the lease |
| **Lease expiry** | **Last day** of the lease |
| **Term and option** | How long the lease runs, and the option period to extend **after expiry** |
| **Rental rate** | Quoted as **$/m²/annum + GST**, or as an annual rental + GST |
| **Types of rent** | **Face**, **net**, **effective** |
| **Rent reviews** | On the **anniversary of lease commencement** |
| **Incentives** | Rent-free periods, fitout contributions, rent abatements |

**Exclusive use is the concept that connects this to Week 2's MCQ 5.** The
premises are defined as the area of **exclusive** use; where there is no
exclusive possession there is a **licence**, not a lease.

**Worked example — face, net and effective rent.** Riverside Centre at 123
Eagle Street was quoted in the tutorial at approximately
**$850–$900/m²/annum**. Take a 500 m² tenancy at the midpoint, on a 5-year
term with 3.5% fixed annual reviews and a 10-month rent-free incentive:

```
Area                                              500 m²
Face rent, year 1              $875/m²/annum  ×  500 m²  =   $437,500 pa
Plus GST                             × 1.1                =   $481,250 pa
Reviews                        3.5% fixed each anniversary
Gross face rent over the 5-year term                      = $2,346,078.82
Incentive: 10 months rent free at the year-1 rate
                               437,500 × 10 ÷ 12          = −  $364,583.33
                                                            --------------
Net rent received over the term                           = $1,981,495.49
Effective rent per annum       1,981,495.49 ÷ 5           =   $396,299.10
Effective rent per m²          396,299.10 ÷ 500 m²        =   $792.60/m²/annum
```

The **face rent** is $875/m². The **effective rent** is $792.60/m². The
incentive is worth a **9.4% discount to the year-1 face rent** — or
**15.5%** against the **average** face rent over the term of $938.43/m².

> **An "effective rent" is meaningless until you name the base, and the two
> bases give answers 6 percentage points apart.** Against year-1 face rent
> the discount is 9.4%; against average face rent over the term it is 15.5%.
> Both are correct arithmetic and only one answers the question asked. State
> the base in the same sentence as the number, every time — and note that
> this example ignores discounting; a true net effective rent discounts the
> cash flows to present value.

### The ten document questions — where to look

| Question | Where in the lease, and what to extract |
|---|---|
| What is the **permitted use**? | Reference schedule / "Permitted Use". Note how narrowly it is drawn — a narrow use limits assignment and resale |
| When are **operating costs** paid? | Outgoings clause: monthly in advance on estimate, reconciled annually? |
| Is a **bank guarantee** required? | Security clause + reference schedule. Note the **amount** (often 3–6 months' gross rent) and the **release** conditions |
| What **insurances** must the lessor take out, noting the lessor's interest? | Insurance clause. Typically building/reinstatement and public liability; check whose interest is noted and who pays the premium through outgoings |
| Key dates to **exercise the first option** | Option clause: the notice window (e.g. not earlier than 12 and not later than 6 months before expiry), and whether the tenant must be **not in breach** |
| When is rent **adjusted to market**? | Rent review clause: which reviews are CPI, which are fixed, which are **market**, and whether there is a **ratchet** |
| What **guarantees** are in place? | Guarantee and indemnity — personal or corporate guarantors, and whether liability survives assignment |
| Is there a **demolition clause**? | A landlord right to terminate on notice for redevelopment. It **devalues the tenant's interest** and must be disclosed |
| Is there a **make good** clause? | End-of-term obligation: base building condition or as-at-commencement, and whether a cash settlement is permitted |
| Is the lease **commercial in confidence**? | Confidentiality clause. Face rents and incentives are the commercially sensitive part — incentives are kept confidential precisely so the **face** rent supports the building's valuation |

> **The last question is the one with a real answer, and it is about
> valuation, not privacy.** A building is valued off its **face** rents. If
> the market learns that a $875/m² face rent was let with 10 months free,
> the effective rent of $792.60/m² is what a purchaser will capitalise. That
> is why lease incentives sit behind a confidentiality clause, and it is why
> due diligence on a commercial purchase (Topic 3, §3.6) demands the
> **lease summary and the incentive deeds**, not just the rent roll.

### Week 6 multiple choice

*No answer key in the deck. Reasoned answers.*

| Q | Question | Answer | Why |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | A buyer will have **all** the information they need supplied under the PLA 2023 | **False** | The s 99 statement carries express **warnings** about what is *not* included — flooding history, structural soundness, building approvals and zoning limits. *Caveat emptor* still governs |
| 2 | s 99 requires **flooding** and **structural integrity** to be disclosed | **False** | Both are expressly on the **not included** list, and the statement warns there is **no warranty as to structural integrity** |
| 3 | A contract for the sale of a commercial building must be in writing by virtue of | **(a) Section 7 of the Property Law Act** | Not the Statute of Frauds (the historical ancestor, not the operative law in Queensland) |
| 4 | Due diligence investigates | **(d) All of the above** — technical, legal and financial | |
| 5 | Technical due diligence generally looks into | **(a)** all aspects of the building and land including structural integrity and access, for compliance and fitness for purpose | (b) describes **legal** due diligence; (c) describes **financial** |

> **Questions 1 and 2 are the same trap twice, and it is the best-value
> trap in the unit.** The PLA 2023 disclosure statement is a **list, not a
> guarantee** — and the three things buyers most want to know (does it
> flood, is it sound, is it approved) are precisely the three the statement
> **warns you it does not cover**. That gap is the entire commercial
> justification for due diligence. Any statement in a quiz that the PLA
> gives the buyer "all" the information is **false** on its face.
