## Why a planner starts here

Financial planning requires specialist knowledge across investments,
taxation, life and disability insurance, estate planning, social security
and retirement planning. But every one of those needs the same two inputs:
what comes in and goes out, and what is owned and owed. So personal
financial statements are prepared in two parts:

```
1. Personal cash flow budget / statement   →  FLOW   (over a period)
2. Personal balance sheet                  →  STOCK  (at a point in time)
```

> **Flow vs stock is the distinction that generates the most marks and the
> most errors in this topic.** A $22,000 *annual loan repayment* is a flow
> and belongs in the cash flow budget. A $250,000 *mortgage balance* is a
> stock and belongs on the balance sheet. Later in this note the case study
> has both a $22,000 flow and a $22,000 balance-sheet stock, and they are
> different things that happen to be the same number.

## The personal cash flow budget

| Side | Contents |
|---|---|
| **Income (inflows)** | Salary, wages, interest, profits, bonuses, fees charged, dividends, distributions, social security pensions or allowances, any other earnings |
| **Expenditure (outflows)** | Food, clothing, gas, electricity, rent, **interest on loans**, rates, any other expenses |

```
Income > Expenditure   →  Net savings
Expenditure > Income   →  Negative savings (deficit)
```

### Worked example — the case-study family

All figures after PAYG tax, annual.

```
INCOME
  Employed partner (after PAYG tax)                    $ 85 000
  Part-time partner (after PAYG tax)                     35 000
  Interest and managed fund distribution                  3 000
                                                       --------
  Total income                                          123 000

EXPENSES
  Food, car, clothing, utilities and insurances           55 000
  School fees, uniforms, other activities etc.            15 000
  Loans and credit card repayments                        22 000
  Personal contribution to superannuation                  6 000
  Entertainment, holidays and miscellaneous expenses      18 000
                                                       --------
  Total expenses                                         116 000

SURPLUS                                                 $  7 000
```

Reconciliation, both sides:

```
85 000 + 35 000 + 3 000                        = 123 000  ✓
55 000 + 15 000 + 22 000 + 6 000 + 18 000      = 116 000  ✓
123 000 − 116 000                              =   7 000  ✓
```

The textbook's own note on this illustration is worth carrying: it focuses
on **cash flow** and does **not** take into account end-of-year taxation
issues arising from the investments, nor **employer superannuation
contributions** that go directly to a fund.

> **Employer super is deliberately absent from this statement, and that is a
> modelling decision, not an oversight.** Employer SG never touches the
> household's bank account, so it is not a cash flow. It *is* an asset that
> shows up on the balance sheet as the super balance grows. Personal
> contributions, by contrast, are cash out — which is why the $6,000
> personal contribution appears as an expense.

> **Income here is already after PAYG tax.** Read the line labels. If a
> question gives you *gross* salary you have to strip tax, Medicare levy and
> any HECS-HELP repayment out before it enters the budget — see note 06, where
> the assignment case study does exactly that.

## Projecting the budget forward

Projections should include assumed rates of growth for income and expenses.
The lecture's five-year projection grows both at **3% p.a.**:

| | Year 1 | Year 2 | Year 3 | Year 4 | Year 5 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Income ($) | 123 000 | 126 690 | 130 490 | 134 405 | 138 438 |
| Expenses ($) | 116 000 | 119 480 | 123 064 | 126 756 | 130 559 |
| **Net cash flow ($)** | **7 000** | **7 210** | **7 426** | **7 649** | **7 879** |

Verified:

```
123 000 × 1.03 = 126 690.00 → 126 690
126 690 × 1.03 = 130 490.70 → 130 490   (truncated, not rounded)
130 490 × 1.03 = 134 405.42 → 134 405
134 405 × 1.03 = 138 437.58 → 138 438

116 000 × 1.03 = 119 480.00
119 480 × 1.03 = 123 064.40 → 123 064
123 064 × 1.03 = 126 756.33 → 126 756
126 756 × 1.03 = 130 559.02 → 130 559
```

> **Year 3 is where you can tell the table truncates rather than rounds.**
> Exact income in year 3 is $130,490.70. Rounded that is $130,491, and the
> net cash flow would be 130,491 − 123,064 = **$7,427**. The table shows
> $130,490 and **$7,426**, which is only consistent with truncation. Either
> convention is defensible; using one for income and the other for expenses
> is not. Pick one, say so in your assumptions, and apply it everywhere.

> **Growing income and expenses at the same rate is a *choice*, and usually
> the wrong one.** If income grows at wage growth and expenses grow at CPI,
> they diverge. Note that here the surplus grows at exactly 3% too —
> `7 000 × 1.03 = 7 210` — which is the giveaway that a single rate was
> applied to both sides. Part A of the assignment explicitly asks for wage
> growth on income and inflation on expenses, with sources.

## The personal balance sheet

Shows the overall financial **position** at a point in time.

```
Total assets = Total liabilities + Net worth

  ⇒ Net worth (equity) = Total assets − Total liabilities
```

| | Definition | Examples from the lecture |
|---|---|---|
| **Assets** | Things of value we own | Bank deposits, property, managed funds, shares, motor vehicles, antiques, superannuation |
| **Liabilities** | Amounts owed to other people or organisations | Credit card debt, loans, mortgage |
| **Net worth** | Assets − liabilities | The residual |

### Worked example — the case-study family

```
ASSETS
  House and contents                                  $ 650 000
  Cash in a savings account                              12 000
  Motor vehicles (2)                                     32 000
  Managed investment fund                                23 000
  Superannuation funds (combined)                       240 000
                                                      ---------
  Total assets                                          957 000

LIABILITIES
  Credit card debt                                       12 000
  Home mortgage                          250 000
  Car loans                               22 000
                                                        272 000
                                                      ---------
  Total liabilities                                     284 000

NET WORTH                                             $ 673 000
```

Reconciliation:

```
650 000 + 12 000 + 32 000 + 23 000 + 240 000  = 957 000  ✓
 12 000 + 250 000 + 22 000                    = 284 000  ✓
957 000 − 284 000                             = 673 000  ✓
```

> **Make the balance sheet actually balance, in that order.** Assets minus
> liabilities equals net worth is a definition, so it cannot fail — but a
> mis-keyed subtotal can. Check both column totals against their components
> *and* the residual, every time. In the textbook's own layout the mortgage
> and car loans are sub-totalled to $272,000 before being added to the
> credit card debt, which is exactly the sort of nested subtotal that hides
> an error.

> **Superannuation is on the personal balance sheet.** $240,000 of the
> $957,000 — a quarter of total assets — is super the family cannot touch
> for decades. It is an asset, and it is *not* a liquid asset. This
> distinction is the entire content of the liquidity ratio below.

## The four planning ratios

Personal financial statements exist to be analysed. The lecture gives four
ratios, and Part A of the assignment requires all four.

| Ratio | Formula | Reads from |
|---|---|---|
| Net worth (equity) | Net worth ÷ Total assets × 100 | Balance sheet only |
| Liquidity | Liquid assets ÷ Current debt × 100 | **Both** statements |
| Savings | Savings ÷ Net income × 100 | Cash flow statement |
| Debt service (monthly) | (Annual debt commitments ÷ 12) ÷ (Annual net income ÷ 12) × 100 | Cash flow statement |

### 1. Net worth ratio

```
Net worth ratio = Net worth / Total assets × 100
                = 673 000 / 957 000 × 100
                = 70.32%  →  70.3%
```

Interpretation, in the lecture's own words: the family owns 70.3% of the
assets they have acquired, and other people or institutions own the other
**29.7%**.

```
Check the complement:  284 000 / 957 000 × 100 = 29.68%  →  29.7%  ✓
70.3 + 29.7 = 100  ✓
```

> **The denominator is total *assets*, not total liabilities and not net
> worth.** Dividing net worth by liabilities gives 673/284 = 237%, which is a
> perfectly meaningful gearing statistic and a completely wrong answer to
> this question. Name your base every time.

### 2. Liquidity ratio

```
Liquidity ratio = Liquid assets / Current debt × 100
```

Two definitions you must get right before you can compute it:

- **Liquid assets** are cash, and other assets that are quickly converted
  into cash.
- **Current debt** is *the total of all debt repayments for the next twelve
  months*.

The lecture identifies **$12,000** — cash in the savings account — as the
liquid asset, and assumes annual repayments on the credit card, home
mortgage and car loan of **$22,000**:

```
Liquidity ratio = 12 000 / 22 000 × 100
                = 54.55%  →  54.5%
```

This shows the percentage of liquid assets available to cover current debt.

> **The $22,000 is a *flow* off the cash flow statement, not the $284,000 of
> balance-sheet debt.** Look at the cash flow budget above: "Loans and
> credit card repayments $22,000". That is the same number. Using total
> liabilities as the denominator gives 12,000 / 284,000 = 4.2%, which is not
> this ratio.

> **And beware the coincidence: "Car loans $22,000" also appears on the
> balance sheet.** Two unrelated $22,000 figures in the same case study —
> one an annual repayment flow across three debts, one the car-loan
> balance. Reading the wrong one gives the right answer by accident here
> and the wrong answer in the exam.

> **Is the $23,000 managed investment fund a liquid asset?** By the
> lecture's own definition — "quickly converted into cash" — a managed fund
> arguably qualifies, and including it would give
> `35 000 / 22 000 = 159.1%` instead of 54.5%, a completely different story.
> The lecture counts only the cash. If you widen the definition, **say so
> and show both**. This is exactly the kind of judgement the assignment's
> Assumptions sheet exists for.

The lecture also flags on the balance-sheet slide that the home mortgage and
car loans "will also have a current component" — i.e. the twelve-month
repayment portion of a long-term debt is current debt even though the
balance is not.

### 3. Savings ratio

```
Savings ratio = Savings / Net income × 100
```

Two definitional traps built into this one, both stated on the slide:

- **Savings is not just the surplus.** Add back any other form of saving —
  investments, extra super contributions.
- **"Net" income means income *net of tax*** — after-tax income.

For the case-study family:

```
Surplus from the cash flow budget                    $  7 000
Add back: personal contribution to superannuation       6 000
                                                     --------
Savings                                                13 000

Savings ratio = 13 000 / 123 000 × 100
              = 10.57%  →  10.6%
```

The $6,000 super contribution was subtracted as an *expense* in the budget,
which is why it has to come back: money put into super is saved, not spent.
And the $123,000 income figure is already after PAYG tax, which is what
makes it "net income".

> **Adding back the wrong lines is the failure mode.** The $22,000 of loan
> repayments also builds net worth — principal repayment is a form of
> saving in the ordinary sense — but the lecture does **not** add it back.
> Neither does it add back "entertainment, holidays". Stick to what the
> slide says: investments and extra super contributions.

The lecture's interpretation: this shows the percentage of net income they
are saving, and **the savings ratio will typically be low or negative for a
young couple with small children, and for an elderly couple**. Life-cycle
theory from Week 1 is doing the work there — consumption is smoothed, so
saving concentrates in the middle-earning years.

### 4. Debt service ratio (monthly)

```
Debt service ratio = (Annual debt commitments / 12)
                     ---------------------------- × 100
                       (Annual net income / 12)

                   = (22 000 / 12) / (123 000 / 12) × 100
                   = 1 833.33 / 10 250 × 100
                   = 17.89%  →  17.9%
```

This indicates the amount of income required to cover debt repayments.

> **The two `/12`s cancel exactly.** `22 000 / 123 000 = 17.89%` gives an
> identical answer. The lecture writes it as a monthly ratio because that is
> how lenders express serviceability, and because if you have monthly
> figures already you do not need to annualise them. It is *not* because
> monthly and annual give different answers — they cannot, as long as you
> divide both sides by 12.

> **Where the `/12` genuinely bites is when only one side is monthly.**
> Monthly repayments over annual income gives 1,833 / 123,000 = 1.49%,
> which looks like a household with almost no debt. Check that the numerator
> and denominator cover the same period before you divide anything.

### Reading the four together

| Profile | Net worth ratio | Savings ratio | Debt service ratio |
|---|---|---|---|
| Low assets and income, high debt as a proportion of income | Low | Low | Potentially **high** |
| High assets and income, low debt as a proportion of income | High | High | **Low** |

Note the direction flip: for the first two ratios higher is better; for the
debt service ratio **lower is better**. The case-study family at 70.3% /
54.5% / 10.6% / 17.9% sits in the second row — substantial equity, modest
debt service — with the one soft spot being liquidity at 54.5%, i.e. cash on
hand covers only about half of a year's debt repayments.

> **A ratio is a sentence, not a number.** Both the lecture and the
> assignment's Financial Ratios sheet require a *comment* on each one. Write
> the comment as: what the number is, what it means for this household, and
> what would improve it. "70.3%" scores nothing; "they own 70.3% of their
> asset base, with the remaining 29.7% financed largely by the mortgage"
> scores.

## Checkpoint

<details><summary>Questions</summary>

1. The family's total liabilities are $284,000 and the annual repayments on
   those debts are $22,000. Which figure is the denominator of the liquidity
   ratio, and why?
2. Compute the net worth ratio if the managed investment fund is sold for
   $23,000 cash and the proceeds are used to pay down the mortgage. Does the
   ratio go up or down?
3. The surplus is $7,000 and the personal super contribution is $6,000. Why
   is the savings ratio numerator $13,000 rather than $7,000?
4. A household has monthly debt repayments of $1,500 and annual after-tax
   income of $96,000. What is the debt service ratio?
5. Both income and expenses in the five-year projection grow at 3%. Without
   computing anything, what must the growth rate of the *surplus* be?
6. Why is employer superannuation excluded from the personal cash flow
   budget but included on the personal balance sheet?

</details>

<details><summary>Answers</summary>

1. **$22,000.** Current debt is defined as the total of all debt repayments
   for the next twelve months — a flow. The $284,000 is the outstanding
   balance, a stock, and belongs to the net worth ratio's calculation, not
   this one.
2. Assets fall to $934,000 and liabilities fall to $261,000. Net worth is
   unchanged at $673,000 — swapping an asset for a liability reduction never
   changes net worth.
   ```
   673 000 / 934 000 × 100 = 72.06%   →  up from 70.32%
   ```
   The ratio **rises**, because the denominator shrank while the numerator
   held. A useful sanity check on the ratio's behaviour: it is not a measure
   of wealth, it is a measure of what proportion of the asset base is
   unencumbered.
3. Because the $6,000 was deducted as an expense in the budget, so the
   $7,000 surplus is already net of it. Money contributed to super is saved,
   not consumed, so it is added back. Otherwise the ratio would penalise a
   household for saving into super rather than into a bank account.
4. Put both on the same footing.
   ```
   Annual repayments = 1 500 × 12 = 18 000
   18 000 / 96 000 × 100 = 18.75%
   ```
   Equivalently `1 500 / 8 000 × 100 = 18.75%` using monthly income of
   $96,000/12 = $8,000. Mixing the two — 1,500 / 96,000 — gives 1.56% and is
   wrong.
5. **3%.** If `I` and `E` both grow at `g`, then
   `(I − E)(1 + g) = I(1+g) − E(1+g)`, so the surplus grows at `g` too.
   Check: `7 000 × 1.03 = 7 210` ✓. This is why growing both sides at the
   same rate makes a projection uninformative — it cannot show a household
   moving into or out of deficit.
6. Employer super never passes through the household's bank account, so it
   is not a cash inflow or outflow — a **flow** concept. But it accumulates
   as a **stock** the household owns, so it sits on the balance sheet. The
   textbook's own note on the case study says the illustration excludes
   employer contributions for exactly this reason.

</details>

## Summary

- Two statements: the **cash flow budget** (a flow, over a period) and the
  **personal balance sheet** (a stock, at a point in time). Total assets =
  total liabilities + net worth.
- The case-study family: income $123,000, expenses $116,000, surplus
  $7,000; assets $957,000, liabilities $284,000, net worth $673,000. All
  four reconcile exactly.
- Projections need explicit growth assumptions. Growing income and expenses
  at the same rate makes the surplus grow at that rate too, which tells you
  nothing.
- Four ratios:

  ```
  Net worth ratio     = 673 000 /  957 000 × 100 = 70.3%   ← higher better
  Liquidity ratio     =  12 000 /   22 000 × 100 = 54.5%   ← higher better
  Savings ratio       =  13 000 /  123 000 × 100 = 10.6%   ← higher better
  Debt service ratio  =  22 000 /  123 000 × 100 = 17.9%   ← LOWER better
  ```

- The three definitional traps: current debt is **twelve months of
  repayments**, savings **adds back** other forms of saving, and net income
  means **after tax**.
- Every ratio needs a written interpretation, both in the lecture and in the
  assignment.
