# Topic 1 — Financial Planning and Regulation

Week 1. Source: `AYB250 Week 1.pptx` / `.pdf`, 41 slides.

This is the vocabulary week. Almost nothing here is arithmetic, and that is
exactly why it is dangerous — the exam questions on this material are
definition questions, and a definition question is either right or it is
zero. The five learning objectives are listed in the overview note; work
through them as five things you must be able to write a paragraph on
without looking.

## What financial planning is

The definition the lecture quotes:

> "A holistic process whereby a client's total position, both financial and
> non-financial, is examined and […] a plan is put in place, which once
> implemented, will assist in meeting the client's ultimate goals and
> objectives."
> — Taylor & Juchau 2018, p.4

Three words in that sentence do the work, and each is a mark:

| Word | Why it is there |
|---|---|
| **holistic** | Not product selection. Not "which fund should I buy". The whole position at once |
| **non-financial** | Goals, health, family structure, risk tolerance, values — all inputs |
| **ultimate goals and objectives** | The plan serves the client's objectives, not the adviser's product list |

> **If you are asked to define financial planning and you write "advice about
> investments", you have written the definition of one of its nine
> components.** The lecture lists nine areas a planner must cover: the legal
> framework and ethical responsibilities, tax planning, investments, credit
> and debt, risk management and insurance, superannuation and retirement
> planning, social security, estate planning, and financial plan
> construction. Investments is one of nine.

Memorise the nine. A cheap way in: **L-T-I-C-R-S-S-E-P** — Legal, Tax,
Investments, Credit, Risk/insurance, Super, Social security, Estate, Plan
construction.

## Where it came from

The origin story matters because it explains why the regulation looks the
way it does.

```
mid-1970s (US) / mid-1980s (Australia)
    Funds management sector: pooling and investing client money
        ↓  accelerated by
    · deregulation of financial services
    · privatisation / demutualisation
    · the superannuation guarantee scheme
        ↓
    Australians sought advice from bankers, brokers, accountants,
    insurance advisers
        ↓
    The first financial advisers came out of LIFE INSURANCE:
    sales-driven, commission-based
        ↓
    Scandals → decades of reform → FOFA (2011)
```

The lecture's own line is that traces of the sales-driven, commission-based
culture **are still present in the industry today**. That sentence is the
link between the history slide and the regulation slides, and it is the
sentence an essay question wants.

### FOFA — the reform you must be able to list

The Future of Financial Advice reforms (2011) are named on the slide as
*the most significant*. Four key elements:

| FOFA element | What it stops or forces |
|---|---|
| Ban on conflicted remuneration | No commissions on investment products |
| Opt-in obligations | Client must actively renew ongoing advice fees |
| Annual fee disclosure statements | Client is told each year what they paid |
| Best interests duty | Adviser must act in the client's best interests |

Drivers, in the lecture's words: **improve consumer protection, increase
professionalism, rebuild trust**. Impact: significantly changed business
models and increased compliance requirements.

> **"Conflicted remuneration" is banned on *investment* products, not on
> everything.** The slide says "such as commissions on investment
> products". Don't write that FOFA banned all commissions in financial
> services — life insurance commissions in particular have their own
> regime. If you are asked what FOFA banned, quote the slide's scope and
> stop there.

## Why financial planning is growing

Four drivers, and they compound on each other:

1. Increasing numbers in older age groups
2. Increase in longevity
3. De-emphasis on the age pension and increased importance of superannuation
4. Low financial literacy and poor financial habits

### 1. An ageing population

Causes: falling birth rates, falling death rates, changing immigration
rates. The baby boomer cohort (born 1945–1960) is the structural feature.

The number to remember:

```
Working people per retiree
  now   : 6.0
  2050  : 2.7   (expected)

Fall = 6.0 − 2.7 = 3.3 workers per retiree
     = 3.3 / 6.0 = 55% fewer workers supporting each retiree
```

> **State the base when you quote that.** "The ratio falls to 2.7" is the
> slide's claim. "A 55% fall" is the same fact expressed against the
> current ratio as the base. Writing "workers fall by 55%" is wrong — the
> *ratio* falls by 55%, not the workforce.

### 2. Longevity

| Period | Men | Women |
|---|---|---|
| 1900s | 55 | 59 |
| Recent times | 79 | 84 |
| 2050 (expected) | 88 | 91 |

Reasons given: medical science, health awareness and exercise, nutrition
and food safety, higher standards of living.

> **The "recent times" figures are textbook figures and they lag.** Current
> ABS life-expectancy-at-birth estimates sit above the 79/84 on the slide.
> The direction of the argument is unaffected, but if the exam asks for a
> current figure rather than the slide's, cite the ABS and say which year.

The planning consequence is the one to write down: a longer retirement
means **the same accumulated balance has to be drawn down over more years**,
which is a longevity-risk problem, not a return problem.

### 3. Age pension de-emphasis, superannuation emphasis

Pension changes on the slide:

- Age of entitlement for women matched to that of men
- Pension age raised to 67
- Incentives to defer: the Work Bonus scheme, the Restart Wage Subsidy

Superannuation guarantee history, which is a favourite short-answer item:

```
1992        introduced at 3%
2003        9%
2013/14     9.25%
2014/15     9.5%
2021 →      +0.5 percentage points each year
now         12%
```

Check the arithmetic yourself — it is the kind of thing that gets asked:

```
9.5% (held from 2014/15) then +0.5 pp per year from 2021:
  2021-22  10.0%
  2022-23  10.5%
  2023-24  11.0%
  2024-25  11.5%
  2025-26  12.0%   ✓ matches the slide's "now 12%"
```

Two more points on the slide: a **tax deduction (15%)** is now offered to
everyone to encourage contributions, and there has been a general move from
**defined benefit** to **accumulation** funds.

> **The consequence of defined benefit → accumulation is a *transfer of
> investment risk from employers to employees*.** That exact phrase is on
> the slide and it is the answer to "why does the shift matter". Under
> defined benefit the employer carries the market risk; under accumulation
> the member's balance is whatever the market delivered. This is the single
> most quotable line in Week 1.

> **The 15% figure is the *tax rate on concessional contributions*, not a
> "15% deduction" in the ordinary sense.** The slide compresses it. If you
> are asked, describe it as concessional treatment — contributions are taxed
> at 15% in the fund rather than at the member's marginal rate — and confirm
> the current wording with your tutor before quoting the slide verbatim.

### 4. Low financial literacy

Drivers listed: greater individual responsibility for funding retirement, a
wider and more complex range of products, frequent changes in taxation and
incentives.

The **Big 3** financial literacy questions (Lusardi & Mitchell, 2011) test
interest/numeracy, inflation, and diversification. The slide's approximate
correct-answer rates:

| Question | US adults | Australian adults | Global average |
|---|---|---|---|
| Interest (numeracy) | ~75–80% | ~75% | ~60–70% |
| Inflation | ~60–65% | ~65% | ~50–60% |
| Diversification | ~50–55% | ~50–55% | ~40–50% |
| **All 3 correct** | ~30–35% | ~35% | ~25–35% |

> **The interesting number is the last row, and the reason is that the rows
> are not independent.** If the three questions were independent, "all 3
> correct" for Australia would be roughly 0.75 × 0.65 × 0.525 ≈ **26%**. The
> observed ~35% is higher, which tells you the same people tend to get all
> three right — financial literacy clusters in individuals rather than being
> spread randomly. That is the finding, and it is why the policy response is
> *education*, not *simpler products*.

Who has lower literacy, per the slide: young people, women, retirees and
First Nations Australians. The slide adds a methodological caveat worth
carrying — research shows **women choose the "do not know" option due to a
lack of confidence**, which means part of the measured gap is a response
effect rather than a knowledge gap.

### The national strategy

- Australia has had a national **financial literacy** strategy since 2005
- In 2018 it moved to a **financial capability** strategy

The vision: Australians have the *knowledge, skills, attitudes and
confidence* to engage in positive financial behaviours and make decisions
appropriate to their individual circumstances throughout their lives.

> **Literacy → capability is a deliberate change of concept, not a rebrand.**
> Literacy is what you know. Capability adds attitudes, confidence and
> actual behaviour. That distinction is exactly the sort of thing a
> definition question hangs on.

## Financial counselling is not financial planning

| | Financial planner | Financial counsellor |
|---|---|---|
| Client | People with assets and goals to plan | People having difficulty managing money |
| Typical work | Investments, super, insurance, estate planning | Financial advocacy, restructuring debt facilities, budgeting |
| Cost | Fee for service | Free services exist in Australia |
| Licensing | AFSL / authorised representative | Not an AFSL activity |

> **A comparison question between the two is very easy to write and very
> easy to half-answer.** The distinguishing feature is not "one is free" —
> it is the *client's situation*. Counselling is remedial and
> debt-focused; planning is forward-looking and goal-focused.

## Key participants

| Role | Who |
|---|---|
| The regulators | ASIC, ATO, APRA, ACCC, AUSTRAC |
| Licence holder | Holder of an Australian Financial Services Licence (AFSL) |
| Authorised representative | The financial adviser acting under someone's licence |
| Industry partners | Insurance companies, stockbrokers, fund managers, banks, software providers, training providers, research houses |
| Professional body | Financial Advice Association Australia (FAAA) |

## The five regulators — the table you must be able to reproduce

| Regulator | Full name | What it does |
|---|---|---|
| **ASIC** | Australian Securities and Investments Commission | **Primary regulator of financial advice and financial planners.** Enforces the Corporations Act 2001. Monitors misleading or deceptive conduct in financial advice. Maintains the financial adviser registers |
| **ATO** | Australian Taxation Office | Administers parts of the super system, **particularly SMSFs**. Oversees taxation aspects of planning (e.g. capital gains) |
| **APRA** | Australian Prudential Regulation Authority | Regulates and supervises banks, insurers and super funds — **not SMSFs**. Ensures financial soundness and stability |
| **ACCC** | Australian Competition and Consumer Commission | Protects competition and fair trading in financial services markets. Acts against anti-competitive behaviour and misleading advertising |
| **AUSTRAC** | Australian Transaction Reports and Analysis Centre | Anti-money-laundering and counter-terrorism financing. Customer identification and reporting of suspicious transactions |

> **The single highest-value distinction in Week 1 is ATO vs APRA on
> superannuation.** APRA prudentially supervises the big funds; the ATO
> regulates **self-managed** super funds. An exam question that says "who
> regulates a client's SMSF" is testing precisely this, and "APRA, because
> APRA regulates super" is the wrong answer.

Second most valuable: **ASIC vs ACCC on misleading conduct.** ASIC covers
misleading or deceptive conduct in *financial advice*; the ACCC covers
misleading *advertising* and competition in the market generally.

## The regulatory framework — nine Acts

The lecture lists these as the principal regulations governing the
profession:

- Corporations Act 2001 (Corps Act)
- Financial Services Reform Act 2001 (FSRA)
- Insurance Contracts Act 1984
- Life Insurance Act 1995
- Superannuation Industry (Supervision) Act 1993 (SIS Act / SISA)
- Future of Financial Advice 2011 (FOFA)
- Income Tax Assessment Act 1997
- Social Security (Administration) Act 1999
- Anti-Money Laundering and Counter-Terrorism Financing Act 2006 (AML/CTF)

Map each Act to the regulator that enforces it and the nine planning areas
it touches — that mapping is a very natural exam question and the slides
never draw it for you.

## The Corporations Act sections — learn the numbers

This is the part of Week 1 with hard, checkable facts. Five section
numbers, and they are worth having cold.

| Section | Concept | One-line test |
|---|---|---|
| **s 911A** | Australian Financial Services Licence | Carrying on a financial services business? You need an AFSL unless exempt |
| **s 766A** | Financial *service* | Are you doing one of the eight listed activities? |
| **s 763A** | Financial *product* | Is the facility used to invest, manage risk, or make non-cash payments? |
| **s 942C** | Financial Services Guide (FSG) | Retail client — must be given the FSG |
| **s 946A** | Statement of Advice (SoA) | Personal advice to a retail client — must be given an SoA |

Plus **Part 7.6 Div 5** for authorised representatives and **s 961B** for
the best interests obligation (with **s 947B** setting out what an SoA must
contain, and **s 947B(6)** requiring it to be "clear, concise and
effective").

### s 911A — the AFSL and its exemptions

A person carrying on a financial services business must hold an AFSL
covering those services, **unless an exemption applies**. Exemptions
generally cover people offering legal advice, tax agents, and incidental
providers such as the media.

> **"The media" being exempt is the point of that slide.** A newspaper
> columnist recommending shares is not operating without a licence — general
> commentary directed at the public is not the same as a financial service
> provided to a client. If a question describes a podcast host giving stock
> tips, the exemption is the answer, not "they need an AFSL".

### s 766A — the eight financial services

You provide a financial service if you:

1. Give advice in relation to financial products
2. Deal in a financial product
3. Make a market for a financial product
4. Operate a registered scheme
5. Provide a custodial or depository service
6. Provide a crowd-funding service
7. Provide a claims handling and settling service
8. Provide a superannuation trustee service

### s 763A — the three limbs of a financial product

A **facility** through which, or through the acquisition of which, a person
does one or more of:

```
· makes a financial investment
· manages financial risk
· makes non-cash payments
```

Examples: shares, insurance, derivatives, superannuation, bank accounts.
A financial product requires a **Product Disclosure Statement (PDS)**.

> **Three limbs, "one or more", and the word *facility*.** A term deposit is
> a financial product because it is a facility for making a financial
> investment. A bank account is one because it is a facility for making
> non-cash payments — not because of the interest. Pick the limb that
> actually applies and name it; naming the wrong limb loses the mark even
> when the conclusion is right.

### Authorised representatives — Part 7.6 Div 5

- A licensee may give a person a **written notice** authorising them to
  provide financial services on behalf of the licensee
- The authorisation may cover some or all of the licence's services
- Authorised representatives may act and transact on behalf of the licensee
- **The licensee is responsible for the conduct of the representative**
  when acting within the scope of the appointment
- The licensee must take reasonable steps to ensure representatives comply
  with financial services laws

> **Liability flows to the licensee, and that is why the licensee table
> matters.** A licensee with 1,082 authorised representatives is legally
> responsible for the conduct of all 1,082 within their scope of
> appointment. That is the direct link between the "largest licensees" slide
> and the regulation slides — and it is a very natural short-essay question.

## The three disclosure documents

| Document | Section | Trigger | Purpose |
|---|---|---|---|
| **FSG** — Financial Services Guide | s 942C | Providing financial services to a **retail client** | So the client can decide whether to acquire the services |
| **PDS** — Product Disclosure Statement | (attaches to a s 763A financial product) | Offering a financial **product** | So the client can decide whether to acquire the product |
| **SoA** — Statement of Advice | s 946A | Giving **personal** advice to a retail client | To communicate the advice and recommendations |

The FSG must contain enough for an informed decision about the *services*:

- Who will provide the service
- The kind of financial services offered
- Who the service provider will be acting for
- How they are remunerated
- Details of any associations or relationships which might be expected to
  influence a service provider

The SoA must disclose sufficient information for an informed decision about
whether the advice is appropriate, with the content requirements in s 947B
and the standard in s 947B(6): **"clear, concise and effective"**.

> **Three separate triggers, and only one of them is about advice.** FSG
> fires on *providing a service*. PDS fires on *offering a product*. SoA
> fires on *personal advice to a retail client*. A scenario where general
> advice is given to a retail client needs an FSG but **not** an SoA — that
> is the trap, and it turns on the word *personal*.

> **Only someone authorised under an AFSL to provide financial advice can
> provide an SoA.** So an unlicensed person giving personal product advice
> commits two breaches at once: no licence (s 911A) and no SoA (s 946A).

## Best interests duty — s 961B

Providers must act in the best interests of the client in relation to the
advice. The six steps on the slide, in order:

1. Identified the (relevant) objectives, financial situation and needs of
   the client
2. Identified the subject matter of the advice sought by the client
3. Where it was reasonably apparent that information disclosed was
   incomplete or inaccurate, made reasonable inquiries to obtain complete
   information
4. Assessed whether the provider has the **expertise** required to provide
   the advice
5. If reasonable to recommend a financial product, conducted a **reasonable
   investigation** into suitable financial products
6. Based all judgement in advising the client on the client's relevant
   circumstances

> **Step 4 is the one people forget and it is the one that is easiest to
> ask about.** "Assessed whether the provider has the expertise required" —
> an adviser who is competent on super but not on estate planning does not
> satisfy s 961B by trying hard on the estate planning part. The correct
> discharge is to refer. Steps 3, 4 and 5 are all about the *adviser's own
> process*, not the client's paperwork.

Note also the phrasing of step 5: *if it is reasonable to recommend a
financial product*. The duty does not assume a product is the answer. "Pay
down the mortgage" and "do nothing yet" are valid outputs of a s 961B
process.

## How the pieces fit

```
Corps Act s 911A ──── AFSL holder (licensee)
                          │
                          │ written authorisation (Pt 7.6 Div 5)
                          ▼
                   Authorised representative  ── licensee stays liable
                          │
                          │ provides a FINANCIAL SERVICE (s 766A)
                          │ in relation to a FINANCIAL PRODUCT (s 763A)
                          ▼
      Retail client ──► FSG (s 942C)  before/at the service
                   ──► PDS            for the product
                   ──► SoA (s 946A)   if the advice is PERSONAL
                          │
                          ▼
              Best interests duty (s 961B), FOFA fee rules
                          │
              ASIC enforces · ATO/APRA/ACCC/AUSTRAC in their lanes
```

## Checkpoint

<details><summary>Questions</summary>

1. Which regulator is responsible for a client's self-managed
   superannuation fund, and which regulates the large public-offer funds?
2. Under s 763A, which limb makes an everyday transaction account a
   financial product?
3. A retail client receives *general* advice. Which of the FSG, PDS and SoA
   are triggered?
4. What is the workers-per-retiree ratio expected to be in 2050, and by
   what percentage has it fallen from the current level?
5. Name the four key FOFA reforms.
6. What single consequence does the move from defined benefit to
   accumulation superannuation have?
7. An adviser recognises they lack expertise in a client's estate planning
   needs but proceeds anyway, doing careful research. Which limb of s 961B
   have they breached?
8. Why did the national strategy change name from "financial literacy" to
   "financial capability" in 2018?

</details>

<details><summary>Answers</summary>

1. **ATO** for SMSFs; **APRA** for banks, insurers and (non-SMSF)
   superannuation funds. APRA's remit is prudential — financial soundness
   and stability.
2. The **non-cash payments** limb. Not the investment limb — the interest is
   incidental.
3. **FSG** yes (s 942C, triggered by providing a financial service to a
   retail client). **PDS** only if a product is offered. **SoA** no — s 946A
   is triggered by *personal* advice.
4. **2.7 workers per retiree**, down from 6. That is a fall of
   `(6 − 2.7) / 6 = 55%` **of the ratio**, not of the workforce.
5. Ban on conflicted remuneration (e.g. commissions on investment
   products); opt-in obligations for ongoing advice fees; annual fee
   disclosure statements; best interests duty.
6. **A transfer of investment risk from employers to employees.**
7. Limb 4 — *assessed whether the provider has the expertise required to
   provide the advice*. Diligence on limbs 3 and 5 does not cure a failure
   on limb 4; the correct response is to refer.
8. Because capability adds **attitudes, confidence and actual behaviour** to
   knowledge and skills. Knowing the right answer and doing it are
   different problems, and the strategy targets both.

</details>

## Summary

- Financial planning is **holistic**, covers financial *and* non-financial
  position, and serves the client's ultimate goals. Nine component areas;
  investments is one of them.
- It grew out of **funds management and life insurance sales**, which is why
  conflicted remuneration needed banning. FOFA (2011) is the landmark
  reform: commissions ban, opt-in, annual fee disclosure, best interests
  duty.
- Four growth drivers: **ageing population** (6 → 2.7 workers per retiree by
  2050), **longevity**, **pension de-emphasis with super emphasis** (3% in
  1992 → 12% now; defined benefit → accumulation moves investment risk to
  employees), and **low financial literacy** (~35% of Australians get all
  Big 3 right).
- Five regulators: **ASIC** (primary, advice, Corps Act), **ATO** (SMSFs,
  tax), **APRA** (banks/insurers/non-SMSF super, prudential), **ACCC**
  (competition, misleading advertising), **AUSTRAC** (AML/CTF).
- Five section numbers: **s 911A** AFSL, **s 766A** financial service,
  **s 763A** financial product, **s 942C** FSG, **s 946A** SoA. Plus
  **Pt 7.6 Div 5** authorised representatives and **s 961B** best interests.
- The licensee is **responsible for the conduct** of its authorised
  representatives within the scope of their appointment.
