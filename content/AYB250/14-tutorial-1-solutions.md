## 1. What is financial planning, and how does it differ from accounting
   and law?

The slides pose the question and leave the answer slide blank, so this is
worked from the Topic 1 lecture.

**Financial planning** is the process of helping a client define personal
financial goals and then designing, implementing and reviewing a strategy
to meet them — across cashflow and budgeting, tax, investment, risk and
insurance, superannuation, retirement and estate planning.

| | Focus | Time orientation | Scope |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Financial planning** | The **client's goals** and whole financial position | **Forward-looking** — strategy over a lifetime | Integrative: draws on tax, law, investment and insurance together |
| **Accounting** | **Recording, reporting and compliance** on transactions that have occurred | **Backward-looking** — historical | Technical and defined: the financial statements and the tax return |
| **Law** | **Rights, obligations and instruments** | Point-in-time, on the document or dispute | Technical and defined: the will, the trust deed, the contract |

> **The distinguishing feature is *integration around a person*, not
> subject matter.** A planner uses tax law, investment theory and
> insurance products, but none of those is the discipline. The
> discipline is turning a household's goals, constraints and risk
> tolerance into a coherent, reviewable strategy — and being accountable
> for whether it is appropriate to *that* client. Accountants and
> lawyers answer a defined technical question; planners answer "what
> should this person do?"

Note the regulatory consequence: that advisory relationship is why
financial planning carries a **best interests duty** and personal advice
obligations that preparing a tax return does not.

## 2. Who are the key participants, and what are their roles?

The slides give the structure as a chain. Learn it as a chain — it is
how the licensing regime actually works:

| Participant | Role |
|---|---|
| **The regulators** — ASIC, ATO, APRA, ACCC, AUSTRAC | Set and enforce the rules |
| **Licence holder** — holds an **Australian Financial Services Licence (AFSL)**, e.g. Professional Investment Services Pty Ltd | Legally responsible for the advice given under its licence |
| **Authorised representative** — the **financial adviser** | Authorised *by* the licensee to provide advice on its behalf |
| **Industry partners** | Insurance companies, stockbrokers, fund managers, banks, software providers, training providers, research houses |
| **Professional body** — the **Financial Advice Association Australia (FAAA)** | Professional standards, membership, ethics |

The regulators, and what each one actually does:

| Regulator | Remit |
|---|---|
| **ASIC** | Corporate, markets and **financial services conduct** — issues the AFSL, polices advice |
| **ATO** | Tax administration, including **superannuation** obligations and SMSFs |
| **APRA** | **Prudential** supervision — banks, insurers, and APRA-regulated super funds |
| **ACCC** | Competition and consumer protection |
| **AUSTRAC** | **Anti-money-laundering** and counter-terrorism financing reporting |

> **The adviser you meet usually does not hold the licence.** They are an
> **authorised representative** acting under someone else's AFSL, and the
> **licensee** carries the legal responsibility for the advice. That is
> why the Financial Services Guide names both, and why "who is
> responsible when advice goes wrong?" has a two-part answer.

> **APRA and ASIC split by *what they protect*, not by industry.** APRA
> is **prudential** — is the institution financially sound enough to keep
> its promises? ASIC is **conduct** — was the customer treated properly?
> A super fund answers to both, for different things. Naming the wrong
> one is a standard lost mark.

## 3. Three reasons appropriate retirement advice matters

From the answer slide:

1. **Longer life expectancy** — a longer period in retirement to fund,
   i.e. **longevity risk**.
2. **Changes to the age pension** — a lower amount and more restricted
   access, so less of the load is carried by the state.
3. **The rising importance of superannuation** — the system now expects
   accumulated super to do the work the pension once did.
4. **Low levels of financial literacy and capability** — people are
   asked to make complex, irreversible decisions without the tools.
5. **Financial planning helps manage uncertain economic conditions.**

Three will do; pick the ones you can explain.

> **The three strongest answers are the ones that compound.** Longevity
> risk is not merely "living longer" — it is that a **fixed pool of
> savings must stretch over an unknown horizon**, so a small error in the
> drawdown rate becomes ruin risk twenty years later. Pension tightening
> and the shift to super convert what was a **defined, government-borne**
> obligation into an **individual, market-exposed** one. The retiree now
> bears longevity risk, investment risk and inflation risk personally,
> and cannot go back to work to fix a mistake. That irreversibility is
> what makes the advice matter.

## 4. Do commissions make financial planning more affordable?

The answer is genuinely two-sided, and the slides give both:

**Yes, in a sense:**

- Commissions **do make it possible to offer advice** at all to some
  clients.
- Particularly valuable for **clients with limited financial resources**,
  who would not pay an up-front fee.

**But:**

- The commission is **built into the price of the insurance product**.
- **The client still pays the fee** — as part of the premium.

> **"Affordable" is doing a lot of work in that question.** Commissions
> change **who writes the cheque and when**, not whether a cost is
> incurred. They remove the up-front barrier and spread the cost across
> the premium — real access benefits — while making the cost **invisible**
> to the client and creating an incentive to recommend the product that
> pays, and to recommend *a* product rather than *no* product. That
> conflict is precisely what the reforms below target.

The current state of the rules, from the slide:

- Commissions are **no longer allowed on investment products**.
- They **are** still allowed on **general insurance** (e.g. home and
  contents), **some life insurance**, and **consumer credit insurance**
  (e.g. credit card insurance).
- **From July 2025**, advisers providing personal financial advice about
  insurance products must **obtain the client's consent** to charge
  commissions for selling the product.

> **Note the pattern of reform: the conflict was removed where the advice
> is most substitutable.** Investment products are comparable and
> switchable, so a commission there distorts a decision the client could
> otherwise make on merit — banned. Insurance is harder to sell and more
> often *not* bought at all, so the commission survives, now behind a
> **consent** requirement. Consent converts a hidden conflict into a
> disclosed one; it does not remove it.

## 5. Case study — Tanya

*Tanya, 19, has worked as a retail assistant for two years and earns
**$33,800 per year after tax**. She shares a flat with two friends.*

*Average weekly payments: rent $150; public transport $50; food $140;
utilities $50; mobile/internet/phone $30; clothing $50; entertainment
$60.*

*She has an automatic debit of **$400 per month** into a balanced managed
fund, running for two years. The fund holds **$10,000** — the original
$2,000 she started it with plus $8,000 of contributions and earnings.
Money left over after expenses and investment stays in her bank account,
currently **$1,000**.*

*Her primary goal is to buy a car in the next six months for about
**$13,000 plus $750 p.a. insurance**. She would use some of the managed
fund if she has to, but would prefer to save.*

> **The slide labels do not match the question sheet.** The sheet asks
> four questions (a) to (d); the slides label them 5a, 5b, 5d and 5e —
> skipping 5c, because the sheet's (b) is split across two slides ("can
> she achieve it?" and "what changes would you suggest?"). Same content,
> shifted labels. Below follows the **question sheet's** lettering.

### (a) State Tanya's short-term objective

> Tanya's short-term objective is to **buy a car costing $13,000 plus
> $750 insurance within the next 6 months**.

A well-formed objective is **specific, measurable and time-bound** — a
dollar amount and a deadline. "Save more money" is not an objective; this
is. That framing is itself the mark.

### (b) Can she achieve it? What changes would you suggest?

Work the monthly position, because the managed fund debit is monthly.
The slides' method: annualise the weekly expenses, then divide by 12.

```
Weekly expenses:
  Rent                              $150
  Public transport                    50
  Food                               140
  Utilities                           50
  Mobile / internet / phone           30
  Clothing                            50
  Entertainment                       60
  ───────────────────────────────────────
  Total per week                    $530
  Per year  ($530 × 52)          $27,560
  Per month ($27,560 ÷ 12)     $2,296.67
```

| Monthly position | |
|---|---|
| Income ($33,800 ÷ 12) | **$2,816.67** |
| Less expenses | ($2,296.67) |
| Less managed fund contribution | ($400.00) |
| **Monthly surplus** | **$120.00** |

```
In six months she saves  6 × $120  =  $720
Plus cash already held                $1,000
                                      ──────
Available at month 6                  $1,720

Target:  $13,000 + $750  =  $13,750
Shortfall (on the $720 of new savings alone)  =  $13,030
```

Every figure verified: weekly $530, monthly expenses $2,296.67 (slide
$2,297), monthly income $2,816.67 (slide $2,817), surplus $120.00, and
$720 over six months.

**No — she cannot achieve it** on current settings. Even drawing the full
$10,000 managed fund *and* the $1,000 in the bank *and* six months of
surplus gives $11,720, still $2,030 short of $13,750.

> **The slides stop at $720 and do not compare it to the target. Do the
> comparison.** The answer to "can she achieve her objective?" is a
> number against a number. $720 of new savings against a $13,750 target
> is not a near miss — it is a factor of nineteen. Note too that the
> $750 is *annual* insurance, so it recurs every year, and running costs
> (fuel, registration, servicing) are absent from the case entirely.

**Changes to suggest**, from the answer slide:

- **Redefine the objective** to make it achievable — a cheaper car and
  more affordable insurance.
- **Cut back on expenses.**
- **Stop contributing to the managed fund** — that frees an additional
  **$2,400** over six months for car savings (verified: 6 × $400).
- **Invest the extra savings** to help them grow.
- A **balanced** managed fund may be **too conservative** for her life
  stage.
- **Review the fund** for higher potential returns and lower fees.
- **Redeem the managed fund** and use the proceeds to buy the car.

> **Two of those suggestions contradict each other, and a good answer
> says so.** "A balanced fund is too conservative — switch to growth" and
> "redeem the fund to buy the car in six months" cannot both be right.
> An asset you will liquidate in six months should be in **cash**, not
> growth assets: the investment horizon governs the asset allocation.
> The conservatism point is only valid for money she is *not* about to
> spend. Resolve it by splitting the goals — car money to cash, long-term
> money to growth. That is the Topic 4 risk-and-return principle applied
> a topic early, and it is the kind of reasoning the exam rewards.

> **The highest-value advice here is the cheapest to give: redefine the
> goal.** $11,720 buys a perfectly serviceable used car. Advice that
> makes a goal achievable by adjusting the goal is not a failure of
> planning — it is planning.

### (c) If her pay falls to $31,000, how are living costs and savings
       affected?

```
Pay cut:  (33,800 − 31,000) / 33,800  =  8.28%

New monthly income   $31,000 ÷ 12       = $2,583.33
Less expenses (unchanged)                ($2,296.67)
─────────────────────────────────────────────────────
Available for saving and investing          $286.67
```

Verified — the slide's **$286** and **8.28%** both reconcile.

**Living costs are unaffected** — they are the same $2,296.67, which is
the point. Income fell 8.3%; expenses fell 0%.

> **The savings capacity does not fall by 8.28% — it collapses by 45%.**
> She previously directed **$520** a month to saving and investing ($400
> to the fund plus $120 surplus). Now she has **$286.67**. An 8.3% income
> cut has produced a **44.9% cut in savings capacity**, because expenses
> are fixed and savings are the residual.
>
> This is the **operating leverage** of a household budget, and it is the
> most important idea in the question. She also **cannot maintain the
> $400 monthly contribution** — it now exceeds her entire surplus, so the
> automatic debit would overdraw the account.

### (d) What advice would you give if her hours are reduced?

From the answer slide:

- **Reduce discretionary spending** — "less money in means less money
  out" — specifically the **$60 weekly entertainment** and **$50 weekly
  clothing**.
- If she wants to maintain her current expenditure, **seek additional
  casual work** to increase income.
- She will likely have to **revise her goals** given the decrease in
  income.

Quantify the first one, because the slides do not:

```
Entertainment + clothing  =  $110 per week
                          =  $110 × 52 ÷ 12  =  $476.67 per month
```

Cutting both entirely restores her to $763.34 a month of capacity —
**more** than the $520 she had before the pay cut. Realistically, halving
them recovers $238 a month and returns her roughly to the original
position.

> **Add the step the slides omit: fix the automatic debit first.** The
> $400 monthly transfer now exceeds her $286.67 surplus. Before any
> lifestyle advice, reduce the debit to something sustainable — otherwise
> the "disciplined savings plan" generates dishonour fees and, if she
> is unlucky, credit card debt at 20%. A savings plan that overdraws an
> account is not a savings plan.

## Checkpoint

1. Tanya's income falls 8.28%. By how much does her savings capacity
   fall, and why is it not 8.28%?
2. Who carries legal responsibility for advice given by an authorised
   representative?
3. Commissions are banned on investment products but permitted on some
   insurance. What is the logic?
4. Name the regulator: (a) prudential soundness of an APRA-regulated
   super fund; (b) whether an adviser met the best interests duty;
   (c) anti-money-laundering reporting.

<details><summary>Answers</summary>

1. **From $520 to $286.67 — a 44.9% fall.** Because **expenses are
   fixed** and savings are the **residual**. A small percentage change
   in income produces a large percentage change in whatever is left
   over. Household operating leverage.
2. **The AFSL holder — the licensee.** The authorised representative is
   authorised to act *on the licensee's behalf*, and the licensee is
   legally responsible for the advice given under its licence.
3. Because **investment products are substitutable and comparable**, so a
   commission distorts a decision the client could otherwise make on
   merit. Insurance is under-purchased and harder to sell, so the
   commission preserves access — now subject, from **July 2025**, to the
   client's **consent**. Consent discloses the conflict; it does not
   remove it.
4. (a) **APRA** — prudential. (b) **ASIC** — conduct and financial
   services licensing. (c) **AUSTRAC**.
</details>

## Summary

- Financial planning is distinguished by **integration around a client's
  goals**, and is forward-looking; accounting is historical reporting and
  law is rights and instruments.
- The chain: **regulators → AFSL holder → authorised representative →
  client**, with industry partners alongside and the **FAAA** as the
  professional body. The **licensee** is responsible for the advice.
- ASIC = conduct; APRA = prudential; ATO = tax and super; ACCC =
  competition and consumer; AUSTRAC = AML/CTF.
- Retirement advice matters because **longevity risk**, a **tightening
  age pension** and the **shift to superannuation** have moved
  retirement risk from the state to the individual, who cannot undo a
  mistake.
- Commissions improve **access** but the client still pays, inside the
  premium. Banned on investments; permitted on general insurance, some
  life and consumer credit insurance; **consent required from July
  2025**.
- Tanya, all verified: weekly expenses **$530**, monthly **$2,296.67**;
  monthly income **$2,816.67**; surplus **$120**; six months of saving
  **$720** against a **$13,750** target. **She cannot achieve the goal.**
- On a cut to $31,000: income falls **8.28%** but savings capacity falls
  **44.9%** (from $520 to $286.67), and the $400 automatic debit becomes
  unaffordable.
- Two slide suggestions conflict — "switch to growth" and "redeem in six
  months". Money to be spent within a year belongs in **cash**.
