# Practice Questions

Exam-style questions across the whole unit. Answers are hidden — write yours
before opening one. Anything numerical should be worked on paper first; the
worked answers show the arithmetic in full so you can find where a wrong
answer diverged.

Question types are labelled the way BSB106 assesses: **[Recall]** for a
definition, **[Apply]** for a framework applied to a scenario, **[Calculate]**
for arithmetic, and **[Extended]** for a structured written answer with a
marking skeleton.

## Section A — The Business Environment Canvas

<details><summary>A1 [Recall] · State the four quadrants of the BEC and the framework that populates each.</summary>

```
Key trends              PESTLE
Macro-economic forces   GDP, inflation, unemployment, consumer spending,
                        monetary policy, fiscal policy
Industry forces         Porter's Five Forces
Market forces           market structure, market share, growth rate,
                        BCG matrix, consumer buying behaviour
```

The BEC analyses the **external** environment only. Every entry needs a stated
impact on the specific organisation plus an opportunity/threat verdict.
</details>

<details><summary>A2 [Apply] · Sort each of the following into a BEC quadrant, a BMC block, or "neither, it is internal to the firm".</summary>

| Item | Where it goes |
|---|---|
| The RBA holds the cash rate steady | BEC — Macro-economic forces |
| Supermarkets take 49.1% of category sales value | BEC — Industry forces (buyer power) |
| The firm's patent portfolio | **BMC — Key resources.** Internal; not a BEC entry |
| Consumers shifting to refillable packaging | BEC — Key trends (Environmental / Socio-cultural) |
| A rival launches a subscription service | BEC — Industry forces (rivalry) |
| The firm's distribution agreement with Woolworths | **BMC — Key partners.** The *existence* of retailer power is a BEC entry; *this firm's contract* is a BMC entry |
| The market is a concentrated oligopoly | BEC — Market forces |
| Loss-leader pricing on handles | **BMC — Revenue streams** |

The dividing line is not the topic, it is the ownership. "Retailers are
powerful" is environment. "We are contracted to these retailers" is business
model.
</details>

<details><summary>A3 [Recall] · PESTLE contains two categories starting with E. Distinguish them and place "a carbon price is introduced on freight".</summary>

**Economic** — the money conditions consumers and firms face: purchasing
power, interest rates, cost of living.
**Environmental** — the physical and ecological world: packaging, waste,
climate, supply-chain resilience.

The test: does it change what money is worth, or does it change the physical
world?

A carbon price on freight is **Political/Legal in origin and Economic in
effect** — it is a government instrument (P/L) that raises the landed cost of
goods (E). The defensible answer names the origin and the transmission
mechanism rather than picking one letter and moving on. What is *not*
defensible is filing it under Environmental because it has the word carbon
in it.
</details>

<details><summary>A4 [Apply] · A group writes: "Unemployment is 4.1%, below the RBA's target of 4.5%, so the RBA will raise rates." Identify every error.</summary>

**The RBA does not have an unemployment target.** Its target is **inflation**
— the 2–3% band on average over time — sitting alongside a full employment
objective.

**4.5% is a NAIRU estimate**, not a target: the non-accelerating-inflation rate
of unemployment, below which wage and price pressure is expected to build.
Calling it a target inverts the meaning — it implies the Bank *wants* people
unemployed.

**The conclusion does not follow directly.** Labour-market tightness is one
input to a rate decision, not the decision.

Correct version: *"unemployment at 4.1% sits below common estimates of the
NAIRU (~4.5%), so labour-market tightness continues to add to inflationary
pressure."*

> **Framework-name errors are cheap to make and expensive to be caught at.**
> A marker who sees "the RBA's unemployment target" now doubts every other
> macro claim in the quadrant. Check the target you are naming actually
> belongs to the institution you are naming.
</details>

<details><summary>A5 [Calculate] · A survey of 1,000 people asks an optional question. 480 answer it; 312 of those choose "sustainable packaging". A student writes "31% of consumers prefer sustainable packaging". A tutor writes "65%". Who is right?</summary>

```
312 / 480   = 65.0%   ← share of those who answered
312 / 1000  = 31.2%   ← share of the whole sample
```

**Both are arithmetically correct and both are incomplete.** They are answers
to different questions, and neither sentence says which.

Defensible wording: *"65% of the 480 respondents who answered the question
nominated sustainable packaging (312 of 480); this is 31% of the full sample
of 1,000."*

This is the exact structure of the error in Team A's A1 summary, which quoted
"64% of millennials" from a table whose base was the 261 respondents who
answered an open-text question, not the 385 surveyed — and whose population
was Metro Manila, not Australia.

> **Every percentage needs a base and a population.** Name both, or a marker
> has to assume you did not check either.
</details>

## Section B — Industry and market forces

<details><summary>B1 [Recall] · Name Porter's five forces and state, for each, one condition that makes it strong.</summary>

| Force | Strong when |
|---|---|
| Threat of new entrants | Low capital requirements, weak brands, light regulation, **a growing market** |
| Bargaining power of suppliers | Few suppliers, no substitutes for the input, suppliers not dependent on you |
| Bargaining power of buyers | Few large buyers, low switching costs, buyers can backward-integrate |
| Threat of substitutes | Cheap, convenient, comparable alternatives from **outside** the industry |
| Rivalry among existing firms | Many similar players, slow growth, low differentiation, high exit costs |

Market growth appears in two forces with **opposite** signs: it *raises* the
threat of new entrants (the prize is growing) and *lowers* rivalry (everyone
can grow without taking share). MarketLine's male toiletries assessment uses
it both ways for exactly that reason.
</details>

<details><summary>B2 [Apply] · Before assessing buyer power in a packaged-goods industry, what must you define, and how does getting it wrong change the answer?</summary>

You must define **players, buyers and suppliers**. MarketLine states them
explicitly for male toiletries:

```
Players    manufacturers of male toiletry products
Buyers     retailers of male toiletry products
Suppliers  packaging, plastics, steel-strip and chemical manufacturers
```

The buyers are **Coles and Woolworths, not the man buying a razor**.

Get it wrong and the answer inverts. Individual shoppers are numerous,
unorganised and cannot negotiate — buyer power looks *weak*. Supermarkets are
few, take roughly half the category's value, face low switching costs and run
private label — buyer power is **strong**. Every downstream conclusion about
margin and channel strategy flips with it.
</details>

<details><summary>B3 [Apply] · Classify each as a rival or a substitute for a disposable-razor manufacturer, and justify the hard one.</summary>

| | Classification |
|---|---|
| Schick disposable razors | **Rival** — same industry |
| A subscription blade service | **Rival** — same industry, different business model |
| Growing a beard | **Substitute** — the need is met with no purchase |
| Laser hair removal | **Substitute** — different industry, same job |
| An electric shaver | **Depends on the market definition.** MarketLine defines male toiletries as covering *disposable* razors and blades, so an electric shaver sits outside it and is a substitute. Under a broader "shaving devices" definition it would be a rival |

The hard one is the electric shaver, and the mark is for naming the market
definition you are using rather than for the label itself.

> **A different business model is not a substitute.** Substitutes come from
> outside the industry. A subscription blade seller is competing in the same
> industry with a different channel and pricing architecture — that is
> rivalry, and putting it under substitutes weakens both forces at once.
</details>

<details><summary>B4 [Calculate] · Four firms hold 38.4%, 21.7%, 12.9% and 9.6% of a market. State the concentration, the market structure, and the second firm's relative market share.</summary>

```
Top four   38.4 + 21.7 + 12.9 + 9.6 = 82.6%
Other                        100 − 82.6 = 17.4%

Firm 2 relative market share = 21.7 / 38.4 = 0.57
```

**A concentrated oligopoly with a dominant firm.** Four firms hold 82.6% of
value, products are differentiated, entry is hard, and pricing must anticipate
rivals' reactions.

Firm 2's RMS of 0.57 is **low** — it holds a bit over half the leader's scale,
so on the BCG horizontal axis it sits on the right-hand side despite a
substantial-sounding 21.7%.

Note that the leader at 38.4% is not a monopolist. A monopoly means **one**
seller with no close substitute.
</details>

<details><summary>B5 [Calculate] · A market is worth $420m in 2020 and $512m in 2025. Give the total growth and the CAGR. A student divides total growth by five — what do they get, and why is it wrong?</summary>

```
Total growth   512 / 420 − 1                 = 21.90%
CAGR           (512 / 420)^(1/5) − 1         =  4.04%

Student's method   21.90 / 5                 =  4.38%   ✗
```

The student's 4.38% is too high by 0.34 percentage points because dividing by
five is a **simple** average that ignores compounding. Growing at 4.38% for
five years gives 420 × 1.0438⁵ = $520.2m, overshooting the actual endpoint.

Always use the root form:

```
CAGR = (End / Start)^(1 / number of years) − 1
```

Note the exponent uses the number of **years elapsed**, which for 2020→2025 is
5 — not the six year-labels in the range.
</details>

<details><summary>B6 [Calculate] · Brand X holds 22% of a market; the leader holds 31%; the market grows 6% a year. Place X on the BCG matrix. Then the leader divests and X's nearest rival now holds 9.5%. Re-place it.</summary>

```
Before   RMS = 22 / 31  = 0.71   low relative share
         growth 6%              low growth (below a 10% cut-off)
                               → DOG

After    RMS = 22 / 9.5 = 2.32   high relative share
         growth 6%              low growth
                               → CASH COW
```

**X's own share never changed.** It went from Dog to Cash Cow because the
denominator changed. That is the whole point of the horizontal axis being
*relative* share: it measures scale advantage against the strongest
competitor, not size in the abstract.

State the growth cut-off you are using. The conventional split is 10%, but many
texts drop it for mature consumer markets — whichever you pick, name it, or the
vertical placement is unauditable.

> **If your BCG working never divides by anything, you have not used the
> framework.** Absolute share is not an axis. 12% share against a 40% leader
> (RMS 0.30) and 12% against a 10% leader (RMS 1.20) are opposite cells with
> opposite strategic advice.
</details>

<details><summary>B7 [Recall] · Distinguish disposable income from discretionary income, and explain why the distinction predicts which product categories suffer in a cost-of-living squeeze.</summary>

```
Disposable income     = gross income − tax and compulsory deductions
Discretionary income  = disposable income − the cost of basic necessities
```

Shopping goods, durables and luxuries are bought out of **discretionary**
income.

Inflation raises the cost of necessities, so discretionary income falls
*faster* than disposable income does — the necessity floor rises underneath a
roughly flat ceiling. Categories close to necessity (toothpaste, razor blades)
hold up; premium discretionary categories (anti-ageing skincare, fragrance) are
hit first and hardest.

"Consumers have less money" describes both categories identically and
therefore predicts nothing.
</details>

## Section C — The Business Model Canvas

<details><summary>C1 [Recall] · List the nine BMC blocks grouped under desirability, feasibility and viability.</summary>

```
Desirability   Customer segments · Customer relationships · Channels
Feasibility    Key partners · Key activities · Key resources
Viability      Cost structure · Revenue streams
Centre         Value propositions
```

Desirability asks whether customers want it and can be reached; feasibility
whether you can deliver it; viability whether the money works durably. The
value proposition is the hinge all three test.
</details>

<details><summary>C2 [Apply] · Rewrite "the company should invest more in advertising" as a business-model recommendation.</summary>

As written it changes no block — advertising is already a Key activity, so this
is "do the existing thing more". It is a budget decision, not a business-model
change.

A business-model version: *"Shift from retailer-mediated promotion to owned,
data-generating customer relationships by launching a direct account and
subscription platform. This adds **first-party customer data** as a Key
resource, converts **Channels** from wholly retail-mediated to omnichannel, and
adds recurring income to **Revenue streams** — strengthening desirability
through direct engagement and viability through revenue predictability."*

The test: name the blocks that change, and name the lens. If nothing structural
moves, it is not a business-model recommendation.
</details>

<details><summary>C3 [Apply] · A team labels a nationwide direct-to-consumer subscription as "Porter's differentiation focus strategy". Correct them.</summary>

Porter's generic strategies have **two** axes: source of advantage (low cost
vs differentiation) and **competitive scope** (broad vs narrow).

The source is right — a subscription differentiates on convenience,
personalisation and brand. The **scope is wrong**: selling nationwide to an
entire product market is the broadest scope available. The correct label is
**broad differentiation**.

It would be differentiation *focus* only if the offer targeted a deliberately
narrow segment — sensitive-skin sufferers, a single metropolitan delivery zone,
one professional trade.

Both Team A's A2 deck and Cal's A3 report carry this error. It is a one-word
fix that inverts the strategy's logic if left in.
</details>

<details><summary>C4 [Apply] · A firm sells hardware near cost and makes its margin on consumable refills. Which BMC blocks does this involve, and what is the model's central risk?</summary>

**Revenue streams** — the pricing architecture: loss leader plus a recurring
consumables annuity. **Key resources** — the installed base of hardware owners,
which is what makes the annuity possible. **Cost structure** — hardware is
deliberately produced at little or no margin.

The risk is **retention, not volume**. Anything that pushes a customer onto a
competing hardware system — a stockout, a cheaper rival subscription, private
label — does not cost one sale. It costs the entire future refill stream from
that customer, and winning them back requires re-acquisition spend.

This is the razor-and-blades model. It is also why a supply disruption is more
dangerous to this model than to a conventional one.
</details>

<details><summary>C5 [Apply] · A recommendation proposes a subscription service with auto-renewal and an AI app that photographs the customer's skin. Name the specific legal and ethical exposures.</summary>

| | Legal | Ethical |
|---|---|---|
| Subscription | Australian Consumer Law on refunds and **automatic renewals**; clear disclosure of renewal terms | Genuinely easy opt-out; honest shipping timelines; not designing cancellation to be difficult |
| AI skin app | **Privacy Act 1988** obligations on collection, storage and use of image and biometric data; informed consent | Not using personalisation manipulatively; being explicit about what images are used for and how long they are kept |
| Any sustainability claim attached to either | ACL prohibition on misleading environmental claims | **Greenwashing** |

The mark is for tying the law to the mechanism. "The company must comply with
relevant laws and act ethically" names nothing and earns nothing.
</details>

## Section D — Disruption and response

<details><summary>D1 [Recall] · Name the four stages of the Double Diamond in order, with the mode of thinking each uses.</summary>

```
PROBLEM DIAMOND    Problem articulation   DIVERGENT
                   Problem selection      CONVERGENT

SOLUTION DIAMOND   Solution generation    DIVERGENT
                   Solution selection     CONVERGENT
```

A disruption enters at t0; a market offering exits at t4. The UK Design
Council's original names for the four phases are **Discover, Define, Develop,
Deliver** — use the unit's labels in an assignment, recognise the originals in
a question.

Divergence and convergence should be separated in time. The tutorial's advice
is to brainstorm without disregarding ideas and to leave *"a day or so"* before
converging.
</details>

<details><summary>D2 [Recall] · Give the five-step convergence procedure for selecting the most significant impact, and say which step does the real work.</summary>

```
1  Remove impacts that are non-consequential or not very substantial.
2  Remove impacts that could be accommodated within the current
   business model.
3  Consolidate inter-related impacts into one general description.
4  Rank by significance — how challenging each is to the current model.
5  Identify the most significant impact(s) from that ranking.
```

**Step 2.** It is the filter that decides whether you have written a
business-model report or an operations memo. "Freight costs rise 8%" can be
real, large and still absorbable — if the current model survives it, it is not
a business-model impact.

The test is not "how bad is it". It is "does the current model still work".
</details>

<details><summary>D3 [Apply] · A disruption causes (i) a temporary 30% cost increase, (ii) two weeks of empty shelves, and (iii) 4% of customers switching to a rival's competing hardware system. Rank them and justify the ranking.</summary>

Ranking: **(iii), then (i), then (ii)** — by **persistence**, not by size.

(i) and (ii) are recoverable. When the disruption ends, freight costs
normalise and shelves refill; both impacts are bounded by the disruption's
duration.

(iii) does not revert. Under a razor-and-blades model a customer who switches
hardware systems takes the entire future consumables annuity with them, and
recovering them costs acquisition spend the incumbent did not previously need.
A 4% share loss that locks in outranks a 30% cost spike that reverses.

This is precisely what step 4 of the convergence procedure — "how challenging
to the current business model" — is asking you to weigh, and framing it as
persistence turns an assertion into an argument.
</details>

<details><summary>D4 [Apply] · A report cites Christensen's disruptive innovation to explain a local competitor winning shelf space during an import shortage. What is wrong, and what would a correct application look like?</summary>

Christensen's theory describes entrants attacking from a **low-end** or
**new-market** foothold with a different value network and business model —
typically a cheaper, simpler, initially inferior offer that incumbents
rationally ignore — then moving upmarket over time. It is a theory about
**business-model asymmetry**, not about capacity to supply.

A domestic rival filling a temporary gap is ordinary competitive rivalry. It is
better analysed with Porter's Five Forces (rivalry, threat of substitutes) or
as a supply-chain resilience argument.

A correct application in this case is **Dollar Shave Club**: low-end entry with
subscription pricing and direct delivery, a model the incumbent's
retail-and-margin structure made awkward to copy without cannibalising itself,
then moving upmarket.

> **Citing a framework that does not fit reads as the opposite of critical
> thinking.** A rubric that rewards "use of theory" is rewarding *appropriate*
> use. One well-fitted framework beats three name-dropped ones.
</details>

<details><summary>D5 [Apply] · Why must a disruption report state its assumptions, and which three assumptions change the answer most?</summary>

A hypothetical disruption has no facts, so the assumptions **are** the facts.
Every later argument is only checkable against them.

The three that decide the recommendation:

- **Duration.** A three-week event calls for inventory buffering. A
  three-month-plus event, or a recurring one, is what licenses a structural
  change like a channel restructure.
- **Breadth.** One port versus many; one supplier versus a category.
- **Severity.** Degraded capacity versus no capacity, and whether a workaround
  (air freight, alternative routing) exists at a cost.

State them in one sentence, early. Doing so is what makes a structural
recommendation proportionate rather than an over-reaction.
</details>

<details><summary>D6 [Apply] · A student situates a disruption in one BEC quadrant only. Why is that a warning sign?</summary>

Because the tutorial states explicitly that **more than one quadrant may be
affected**, and because a disruption confined to one quadrant is usually one
that has been read shallowly.

A severe-weather port disruption, for example, lands in all four:

| Quadrant | How it lands |
|---|---|
| Key trends | Environmental — rising frequency of severe weather makes supply-chain fragility a structural trend, not an event |
| Macro-economic | Freight and commodity cost pressure; air-freight substitution raises landed cost economy-wide |
| Industry forces | Rivals with domestic supply chains stay stocked; local substitutes strengthen |
| Market forces | Buying behaviour shifts — panic buying, then forced brand switching |

**Key trends is the one most often missed, and it is the load-bearing one.** If
severe weather is a trend rather than an event, a temporary fix does not
address it — which is the argument that justifies a structural response.
</details>

## Section E — Teamwork and collaborative learning

<details><summary>E1 [Recall] · Define individual accountability and positive interdependence, and name the source of each.</summary>

**Individual accountability** — the degree to which individual students are
held accountable for their contribution to the collaborative learning
(Slavin 1980, as cited in Meijer et al., 2020).

**Positive interdependence** — the extent to which the performance of a single
group member depends on the performance of the other group members
(Johnson 1981, as cited in Meijer et al., 2020).

They trade against each other: a design that maximises one tends to weaken the
other.
</details>

<details><summary>E2 [Apply] · Match each scenario to free-riding, social loafing, the sucker effect, or performance-orientation.</summary>

| Scenario | Behaviour |
|---|---|
| A member stops attending meetings and submits nothing | **Free-riding** |
| A member attends and contributes, but produces noticeably weaker work than they would have alone | **Social loafing** |
| One member rewrites three sections the night before submission to cover for others | **Sucker effect** — the condition of the person carrying the load |
| The team gives every task to whoever is already best at it, to protect the mark | **Performance-orientation** — the mark is optimised, the learning is not |

The last one is the subtle one: it looks like good management, and Meijer et
al. classify it as misaligned with the objectives of collaborative learning.
</details>

<details><summary>E3 [Calculate] · On a four-person team you receive 4, 5 and 3 from teammates and rate yourself 7. What average is recorded, and what would it be if self-scores counted?</summary>

```
As QUT computes it (self excluded):  (4 + 5 + 3) / 3     = 4.00
If the self-score counted:           (4 + 5 + 3 + 7) / 4 = 4.75
```

**4.00.** Your own score is excluded from your own average, so inflating it
changes nothing about your mark — it only widens the gap between your
self-assessment and your peers', which is one of the biases Meijer et al. note
corrections have had to be written for.

Also note the volatility: on a four-person team the average rests on **three**
ratings, so one rating moving by a point shifts the average by a third of a
point. Getting to the 1–2 band, where the whole assignment can be voided,
takes fewer hostile ratings on a small team.
</details>

<details><summary>E4 [Recall] · What is the reciprocity effect, and why does it matter more in a summative peer evaluation than a formative one?</summary>

The **reciprocity effect** (Magin 2001) states that the relationship between
peers influences how they assess each other. Diprose, Judd and Morris (1997)
found students who rated a peer's contribution poorly feared their own rating
would be marked down in retribution — so they inflated.

It matters more summatively because Sridharan, Tai and Boud (2019) found
students assess peers validly in a **formative** context but not a
**summative** one: when the rating carries marks, students are reluctant to
penalise peers honestly.

BSB106's peer evaluation is summative and runs twice with the same team, which
is the worst case — high stakes plus an ongoing relationship.
</details>

<details><summary>E5 [Apply] · A team contract's preamble cites research showing cognitive and personality diversity improves the quality of written group work. The formation section then ticks "similar personalities". What is the problem and how do you fix it in one clause?</summary>

The contract argues against its own selection and never acknowledges it. Both
positions are defensible — homogeneous teams coordinate faster, heterogeneous
teams produce better analysis — but citing the diversity finding and then
selecting against it, silently, reads as a contract that did not read itself.

Fix: *"The team formed on similar personalities to reduce coordination cost,
and mitigates the resulting loss of cognitive diversity by rotating who
critiques each section."*

That one clause converts an unnoticed contradiction into a stated trade-off
with a management response — which is what "research-informed" is supposed to
produce.
</details>

## Section F — Extended response

These are the shape of a longer written question. Each answer gives a skeleton
rather than prose — build the paragraphs yourself, then check the skeleton for
anything you missed.

<details><summary>F1 [Extended] · A consumer-goods manufacturer holds 60% of an Australian market. Assess the attractiveness of that industry using Porter's Five Forces, and explain what its share does and does not entitle it to conclude.</summary>

**Skeleton**

1. **Define the unit of analysis.** Name the market, and state who the players,
   buyers and suppliers are. In packaged goods the buyers are retailers, not
   shoppers. One sentence; it makes everything after it auditable.

2. **Work the five forces, each with evidence and a strength verdict.** Note
   the two that commonly run against intuition in a growing consumer market:
   new entrants can be **strong** (growth attracts entry despite scale and
   advertising barriers), and substitutes can be **weak** (no out-of-industry
   way to meet the need).

3. **Reconcile the forces into an attractiveness judgement.** Strong buyer
   power plus strong rivalry means margin is competed away at the retail
   interface even for a dominant firm.

4. **Address the share.** 60% is **not a monopoly** — a monopoly is one seller
   with no close substitute. It is a **concentrated oligopoly with a dominant
   firm**. The strategic difference is decisive: a monopolist prices against
   demand alone; a dominant firm prices anticipating a live second-place rival
   and remains exposed to retailer private label.

5. **Conclude with the implication**, not a summary. Where is profit leaking,
   and which force is the one to manage?

**Marks are lost for**: analysing shopper power instead of retailer power;
calling 60% a monopoly; naming rivals from an adjacent market; asserting force
strengths with no evidence; running Five Forces on the *company* rather than on
a named *market*.
</details>

<details><summary>F2 [Extended] · A major disruption threatens a brand's supply of imported product for at least six months. Analyse the impact on its business model and recommend a response.</summary>

**Skeleton**

1. **State assumptions** — duration, breadth, severity. One sentence, first.
   Six months and multiple entry points is what licenses a structural answer.

2. **Situate the disruption in the BEC.** All affected quadrants, and do not
   omit Key trends — that is what makes the response structural rather than
   temporary.

3. **Problem articulation (divergent).** Walk all nine BMC blocks and name a
   candidate impact for each. Nine blocks, nine impacts, one pass.

4. **Problem selection (convergent).** Run the five steps explicitly and say
   you ran them. Rank by **persistence**: recoverable impacts below locking-in
   ones. Name the single most significant impact and why.

5. **Solution generation (divergent).** At least three responses.

6. **Solution selection (convergent).** Choose comparatively — the winner
   should solve both the immediate and the structural problem where the others
   solve only one. State each rejected option's limitation.

7. **Revise the business model.** Rewrite each affected block, mark the changes
   on the canvas, and say which lens — desirability, feasibility, viability —
   each change serves. Check whether **Key resources** changed: new capability
   and new data are resources, and this block is the one most often left
   unrevised.

8. **Use theory precisely.** Five Forces and PESTLE for the environment, BMC
   for the impact, and only cite Christensen if the case genuinely involves a
   low-end or new-market entrant with an asymmetric business model.

**Marks are lost for**: no assumptions; one BEC quadrant; a single recommended
response with nothing rejected; a revised canvas that does not show what
changed; framework name-dropping.
</details>

<details><summary>F3 [Extended] · Evaluate group assessment, individual assessment, and group assessment combined with intra-group peer assessment as methods for assessing collaborative learning.</summary>

**Skeleton**

1. **Set up the two criteria.** Construct validity — does the measure measure
   what it intends to, which here is the *individual's* domain-specific
   ability. Behavioural alignment — does it provoke the behaviour intended.

2. **Set up the two principles.** Individual accountability (Slavin 1980);
   positive interdependence (Johnson 1981). Say that they trade off.

3. **Group assessment.** Fosters interdependence, no accountability. Validity
   problem: a group score appears on the transcript as though it were an
   individual one. Provokes free-riding, social loafing, the sucker effect and
   performance-orientation.

4. **Individual assessment.** Fosters accountability, no interdependence.
   Validity problem: performance is still shaped by the group, so it is at best
   a *collaboration-moderated* individual assessment. Provokes superficial
   coordination — divide, staple, submit — and can create rivalry that impairs
   information sharing.

5. **Group plus intra-group peer assessment.** Restores some accountability
   while keeping interdependence. Three challenges: the collaborative *process*
   does not necessarily tell you about the *product*; peer ratings are biased
   (friendship, peer pressure, harsh marking after conflict, strategic grading,
   the reciprocity effect); and there is no agreed formula for converting a
   group score into individual scores.

6. **Conclude with the formative/summative split.** Peer assessment appears
   valid formatively and not summatively (Sridharan, Tai & Boud, 2019). So the
   third method is strongest as feedback and weakest as a grade — which is
   exactly where most units, including this one, apply it.

**Marks are lost for**: treating the four misaligned behaviours as synonyms;
arguing "peer assessment is unfair" instead of naming the validity problem;
omitting that individual assessment has a validity problem too.
</details>

## How to use these

Work Section B on paper with a calculator and check every digit — the
arithmetic questions are where a confident answer is most often wrong. For
Sections A, C, D and E, write two or three sentences before opening the answer;
recognising a correct answer is much easier than producing one, and the exam
asks for the second.
