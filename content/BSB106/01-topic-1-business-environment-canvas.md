# TOPIC 1 — The Business Environment Canvas

## 1.1 What the BEC is for

The **Business Environment Canvas (BEC)** is a one-page tool for analysing
how the *external* environment presses on an organisation. The Assignment 1
brief defines it exactly that way: *"a tool used to analyse how the external
environment impacts an organisation."*

Two words in that sentence do the work.

**External.** The BEC is not about the firm. Its capabilities, its culture,
its cost base, its brand equity — none of that belongs on the canvas. Those
are internal, and they belong on the Business Model Canvas (Topic 3). If a
sentence in your BEC could only be written by someone with inside knowledge
of the company, it is in the wrong document.

**Impacts.** A quadrant is not a list of facts about the world. It is a list
of forces *with a stated consequence for this organisation*. "Inflation is
high" is an observation. "Inflation raises commodity, material and transport
costs across P&G's supply chain, compressing margins on discretionary
products" is a BEC entry.

The canvas derives from Osterwalder and Pigneur's *Business Model
Environment* map in *Business Model Generation* (2010), which surrounds the
Business Model Canvas with four contextual areas. The QUT template supplies
only those four headings and a 60-word box under each:

```
┌──────────────────────────┬──────────────────────────┐
│      KEY TRENDS          │   MACRO-ECONOMIC FORCES  │
│  where the world is      │   the money conditions   │
│  heading                 │   everyone operates in   │
├──────────────────────────┼──────────────────────────┤
│    INDUSTRY FORCES       │      MARKET FORCES       │
│  who you compete with,   │   the shape of demand    │
│  buy from, sell to       │   and of the market      │
└──────────────────────────┴──────────────────────────┘
```

Osterwalder's original names sub-blocks under each area — technology,
regulatory, societal and socioeconomic trends under Key Trends; global market
conditions, capital markets, commodities and economic infrastructure under
Macro-economic Forces. The QUT template does not print these, but they are a
useful checklist for making sure a quadrant is complete rather than four
sentences about the same thing.

> **The four quadrants are not four topics — they are four lenses on one
> organisation.** A marker reading a strong BEC can tell it is about *this*
> company from any single quadrant. A weak BEC reads like four Wikipedia
> summaries of Australia. If you could paste the quadrant into a competitor's
> canvas unchanged, it is not analysis.

## 1.2 Which framework goes in which quadrant

This mapping is what the assignments actually reward. It is inferred from
Assignment 1's instruction to explain *"the models and theoretical frameworks
that informed"* the canvas, and from what Team A was credited for using.

| Quadrant | Framework that populates it | Output you write |
|---|---|---|
| Key trends | **PESTLE** | Direction of travel — technology, regulation, society, environment |
| Macro-economic forces | Macro indicators: GDP, inflation, unemployment, consumer spending, monetary policy, fiscal policy | Conditions of demand and cost |
| Industry forces | **Porter's Five Forces** | Where profit is being competed away (Topic 2) |
| Market forces | Market structure, market share, growth rate, **BCG matrix**, consumer buying behaviour | The shape of demand and the firm's position in it (Topic 2) |

Topic 2 covers the bottom two quadrants in detail. This topic covers the top
two.

## 1.3 Key trends — PESTLE

PESTLE splits the macro-environment into six categories. Every entry gets
three things: the issue, its impact on *this* organisation, and a verdict of
**opportunity or threat**.

```
P  Political       trade regulation, tariffs, geopolitical exposure
E  Economic        purchasing power, interest rates, cost of living
S  Socio-cultural  values, demographics, media and influence
T  Technological   channels, automation, data, e-commerce
L  Legal           consumer law, privacy, advertising standards
E  Environmental   packaging, waste, climate, supply-chain resilience
```

### Worked example — P&G Australia

Team A's PESTLE (Appendix B of the A1 summary), tidied and with the verdicts
stated:

| | Issue | Impact on P&G | Verdict |
|---|---|---|---|
| **P** | Operates in over 180 countries under different trade regimes and tariff structures | Russia–Ukraine conflict forced suspension of Russian operations and a financial loss | Threat |
| **E** | Australian inflation reduces disposable income | Demand shifts away from premium/discretionary lines | Threat |
| **S** | Social media and influencer marketing have reshaped brand engagement | Changes how consumers interact with brands and decide | Opportunity |
| **T** | Online sales channels now dominate parts of the category | Rising mobile use, growth of e-commerce | Opportunity |
| **E** | Tightening packaging and waste regulation; climate events disrupting supply chains | Sustainable packaging raises unit cost, compressing margins, but raises brand standing | Threat (with an embedded opportunity) |
| **L** | January 2025 class action alleging Charmin's environmental claims were misleading | Reputational damage, financial penalties, possible forced product and marketing change | Threat |

Note what makes the Legal row good: it is a **dated, specific, attributable
event**, not "P&G faces legal risk". Note what makes the Political row good:
it names a *consequence that already happened*.

> **PESTLE's E appears twice and they are different letters.** Economic = the
> money conditions consumers and firms face. Environmental = the physical and
> ecological world, including packaging, waste and climate. Putting
> "rising energy costs" under Environmental instead of Economic is a common
> and avoidable slip. If in doubt: does it change what money is worth, or
> does it change the physical world? Economic, or Environmental.

> **An issue can be a threat and an opportunity at once, and saying so is
> worth marks.** Tightening packaging regulation raises P&G's costs *and*
> hands it a differentiation lever that lower-margin private-label competitors
> cannot easily copy. A BEC that only ever writes one verdict per row is
> flattening the analysis. Write the dominant verdict, then name the other
> side in the same breath.

## 1.4 A trap sitting in the source material — evidence provenance

Team A's A1 summary and BEC both lean on this claim:

> *"64% of millennials prefer sustainable, environment-friendly packaging
> solutions from brands (Market-Line, 2023)"*

Three things are wrong with it, and each is the kind of thing a marker checks.

**The attribution is wrong.** The 64% is not from MarketLine. It is Table 4
of **Bara, Dela Cruz and Mendoza (2021)**, the sustainable packaging reading
supplied in the A1 folder. No MarketLine profile in the folder contains the
word "millennial".

**The base is not what it looks like.** The study surveyed 385 respondents,
but only **261 answered the open-text question** the table reports:

```
Sustainable packaging, environment-friendly   168    64%
Informative packaging                          72    28%
Secure packaging - tight and protective        21     8%
                                              ───   ────
Total                                         261    100%

168 / 261 = 64.4%   ← the quoted figure
168 / 385 = 43.6%   ← the share of the whole sample
```

Both are true statements about the same 168 people. "64% of millennials" is
only defensible if you say *of those who answered the question*.

**The population is not Australian.** The 385 respondents were millennials
**residing in Metro Manila, the Philippines**, surveyed online in 2021. The
paper itself flags this as a limitation. Generalising it to P&G's Australian
market landscape without saying so is exactly the kind of unsupported leap
the rubric penalises.

> **Always name the base of a percentage, and always name the population it
> was measured on.** The correct citation is: *64% of the 261 Metro Manila
> millennials who answered the question nominated sustainable, environmentally
> friendly packaging (Bara et al., 2021)*. That sentence is longer, and it is
> the one that survives a marker's scrutiny.

## 1.5 Macro-economic forces

Six standard indicators. For each: what it is, which direction is good for
the firm, and the specific consequence.

| Indicator | What it measures | Why the firm cares |
|---|---|---|
| **GDP growth** | Total output; the size of the economy | Rising GDP signals room to invest and grow revenue |
| **Inflation** | Rate of general price increase | Raises input, commodity and transport costs; erodes real purchasing power |
| **Unemployment** | Share of the labour force out of work and looking | Low unemployment → more household income and spending, but can add to inflation |
| **Consumer spending** | Where households actually put money | Shows the essentials-versus-discretionary shift directly |
| **Monetary policy** | Central bank interest rate settings | Sets the cost of capital and the pace of demand |
| **Fiscal policy** | Government spending and taxation | Budget deficits and transfers lift disposable income |

### Worked example — Team A's macro quadrant

The submitted quadrant, with each claim assessed:

| Claim | Verdict |
|---|---|
| "Australia's 0.3% GDP growth per quarter presents further investment opportunities" | Plausible for 2024–25 but **unsourced in the folder**, and quarterly figures date fast |
| "Inflationary impacts cause unnecessary commodities and material price increases, as well as transportation" | Sound, and correctly attributed to P&G's own 2024 annual report |
| "Unemployment at 4.1%, below the Reserve Bank of Australia's target ... the RBA believes the target should be 4.5%" | **Wrong on the framework.** See below |
| "P&G derives a significant amount of its revenue (65%) from developed markets like NA and Europe, which are experiencing slow growth" | Cited to Kramer (2017) — an eight-year-old Investopedia piece. Directionally fine, dated as evidence |
| "Fiscal policy is government spending to influence economic activity ... boost to the economy" | Correct definition, correctly applied |

> **The RBA does not have an unemployment target.** Its inflation target is
> the 2–3% band on average over time; alongside it sits a *full employment*
> objective. The ~4.5% figure floating around is an estimate of the **NAIRU**
> — the non-accelerating-inflation rate of unemployment, the level below which
> wage and price pressure is expected to build. Calling it "the RBA's target"
> in an assignment inverts the meaning: it implies the Bank *wants* more
> people unemployed. Write it as: *unemployment at 4.1% sits below common
> estimates of the NAIRU (~4.5%), so labour-market tightness continues to add
> to inflationary pressure.* Confirm the current framing with your tutor —
> the RBA's stated objectives were restated in the 2023–24 reforms.

## 1.6 Reading the macro data you are given

The four MarketLine profiles each carry an Australian country-data appendix.
They **contradict each other**, because they were published in different years
and the older ones carry pre-COVID projections that were never updated.

| Indicator, year 2020 | Male Toiletries (pub. Feb 2022) | Skincare (pub. 2023) | Haircare / Oral Hygiene (pub. 2024) |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Population (m) | 24.5 | 24.5 | 25.6 |
| Inflation | 2.6% | 2.6% | 0.8% |
| Real GDP growth | +3.1% | +3.1% | −0.3% |

The 2020 rows in the two older profiles are not observations. Australia's
economy contracted in 2020 and CPI inflation was well under 1%; a report
claiming +3.1% real growth and 2.6% inflation for the COVID year is showing
you a forecast that was never revised. The newer profiles' figures (−0.3%,
0.8%) are the ones consistent with what actually happened.

The Skincare profile is internally broken as well: its country-data tables are
headed **"2018–22"** but contain only three rows, ending at 2020.

> **Cite the newest profile for macro data, and say which one you used.** If
> two supplied sources disagree, a marker wants to see that you noticed. One
> clause — *"the more recent 2024 profiles report 2020 inflation at 0.8%,
> which is used here in preference to the 2.6% carried in the 2022
> profile"* — converts a contradiction from a liability into evidence of
> critical reading.

### The currency column that carries no information

MarketLine reports values in US dollars with A$ and € columns beside them.
The Male Toiletries profile states that *"all currency conversions ... have
been calculated using constant annual average 2020 exchange rates."*

Check what that does to the A$ column:

```
Year   US$m     A$m     implied A$/US$
2016   259.3   376.6       1.4524
2017   271.3   394.0       1.4523
2018   282.1   409.7       1.4523
2019   296.2   430.1       1.4521
2020   280.0   406.5       1.4518

Growth 2019→2020:   US$  280.0/296.2 − 1 = −5.47%
                    A$   406.5/430.1 − 1 = −5.49%
```

The rate never moves. The A$ column is the US$ column multiplied by a constant,
so it contains **zero** exchange-rate information and the growth rates are
identical to two decimal places.

> **You cannot read Australian-dollar market growth off a MarketLine A$
> column.** It is a fixed-rate restatement, not a currency-adjusted series. If
> an assignment asks what an Australian market is worth in Australian dollars
> at today's exchange rate, that number is not in the report.

## 1.7 Writing a 60-word quadrant

Sixty words is roughly four sentences. The structure that survives the limit:

```
1. The dominant force, with a number.              (~18 words)
2. The second force, with its consequence.         (~16 words)
3. The third force.                                (~14 words)
4. The strategic implication for the organisation. (~12 words)
```

What to cut, in order:

- **Hedging.** "It could potentially be argued that" is six words of nothing.
- **Framework names.** The canvas does not need to say "using PESTLE" — the
  written summary is where you name the framework. The A1 brief asks for that
  explanation in the *summary*, not the canvas.
- **Definitions.** The CEO knows what inflation is.
- **References.** The brief says explicitly they are not needed on the canvas.

What never to cut: the **numbers** and the **verdict**. A quadrant with no
figures reads as opinion; a quadrant with no opportunity/threat call has not
finished the job.

### Team A's key trends quadrant — 59 words, and why it works

> *"Consumers are seeking eco-friendly, sustainable, and ethically sourced
> products, prompting P&G to adapt packaging and formulations. The prominent
> rise of social media and influencer marketing reshapes brand engagement.
> E-commerce growth and digital innovation offers new opportunities to
> strengthen brand identity, while the rise of health-conscious trends drives
> a newfound demand for natural ingredients in personal care and hygiene
> products."*

Four forces, each with a named consequence for P&G, inside the limit. What it
lacks is a single number — adding one would have cost about eight words and
strengthened it considerably.

## Checkpoint

<details><summary>1. A student writes under Industry forces: "P&G has strong R&D capability and a portfolio of trusted global brands." What is wrong?</summary>

It is an **internal** statement. R&D capability and brand equity are Key
Resources — they belong on the **Business Model Canvas**, not the Business
Environment Canvas. The BEC is external only.

The external version of the same idea would be: *"Incumbent brand strength
and scale economies across the category raise barriers to entry, limiting the
threat of new entrants"* — now it is a statement about the industry, and it
belongs under Industry forces.
</details>

<details><summary>2. Your macro quadrant runs to 71 words. The brief says 60 with a ±10% margin. Are you safe?</summary>

No. 60 + 10% = 66. At 71 words you are five words past even the tolerance,
and the brief says markers "will otherwise not consider content beyond stated
limits" — so the tail of the box is ignored anyway.

Cut hedging and definitions first, then the least consequential force. Never
cut a number to save words; cut the words around it.
</details>

<details><summary>3. Two supplied MarketLine profiles report 2020 Australian inflation as 2.6% and 0.8%. Which do you use, and what do you write?</summary>

Use **0.8%**, from the more recently published profile. The 2.6% figure sits
in a profile published in February 2022 whose country tables were evidently
not refreshed for COVID — the same tables claim +3.1% real GDP growth for
2020, which did not happen.

Then say so in one clause: *"2020 inflation is taken from the 2024 profiles
(0.8%); the 2022 profile's 2.6% appears to be an unrevised pre-pandemic
projection."* You get credit for the figure and for the judgement.
</details>

<details><summary>4. Which quadrant does "a competitor launches a subscription razor service" belong in, and which does "consumers increasingly buy grooming products online" belong in?</summary>

The competitor launch is **Industry forces** — it is a named rival changing
its offer, which is a competitive-rivalry event.

The shift to online buying is **Key trends** (technological / socio-cultural)
if you are describing the direction of travel, or **Market forces** if you are
quantifying it as a change in the shape of demand for your specific market.
Either is defensible; what is not defensible is putting it in both without
saying anything different in each.
</details>

## Summary

- The BEC analyses the **external** environment only, and every entry needs a
  stated **impact on this organisation** plus an opportunity/threat verdict.
- Four quadrants, four frameworks: PESTLE → Key trends; macro indicators →
  Macro-economic forces; Porter's Five Forces → Industry forces; market
  structure, share, growth and BCG → Market forces.
- PESTLE's two E's are different: Economic is money, Environmental is the
  physical world.
- The RBA targets **inflation** (2–3%), not unemployment. ~4.5% is a NAIRU
  estimate, not a target.
- Supplied sources contradict each other on 2020 macro data. Use the newest
  and say that you did.
- MarketLine's A$ columns use a constant 2020 exchange rate and carry no
  currency information.
- Always name a percentage's base and the population it was measured on.
- 60 words per quadrant is a hard limit with a 66-word tolerance. Cut hedging,
  keep numbers.
