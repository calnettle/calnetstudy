# TOPIC 2 — Industry and Market Forces

The bottom half of the Business Environment Canvas. Industry forces is
Porter's Five Forces. Market forces is market structure, share, growth, the
BCG matrix, and what actually drives a consumer to buy.

This is where most of the unit's arithmetic lives, and where Team A's
submitted A1 made most of its errors — so the worked examples double as a
list of things not to repeat.

## 2.1 Porter's Five Forces

Porter's (2008) framework asks a single question: **where is the profit in
this industry going, and who is taking it?** Five forces compete it away.

```
                    THREAT OF NEW ENTRANTS
                            │
                            ▼
 BARGAINING POWER  →  RIVALRY AMONG   ←  BARGAINING POWER
   OF SUPPLIERS       EXISTING FIRMS      OF BUYERS
                            ▲
                            │
                  THREAT OF SUBSTITUTES
```

| Force | The question | Strong when |
|---|---|---|
| **New entrants** | How easily can someone else start competing? | Low capital needs, weak brands, light regulation, growing market |
| **Supplier power** | Can suppliers raise prices or cut quality? | Few suppliers, no substitutes for inputs, suppliers not dependent on you |
| **Buyer power** | Can buyers force prices down? | Few large buyers, low switching costs, buyers can backward-integrate |
| **Substitutes** | Can the need be met a different way? | Cheap, convenient, comparable alternatives from *outside* the industry |
| **Rivalry** | How hard do incumbents fight each other? | Many similar players, slow growth, low differentiation, high exit costs |

### Defining the players is half the analysis

Before any force can be assessed, you have to say who is who. MarketLine
states this explicitly for male toiletries:

```
Players    = manufacturers of male toiletry products
Buyers     = retailers of male toiletry products
Suppliers  = packaging, plastics, steel-strip and chemical manufacturers
```

That definition drives everything. **Buyers are Coles and Woolworths, not the
man buying a razor.** If you analyse buyer power as "consumers can shop
around", you have analysed the wrong buyer and every conclusion downstream is
wrong.

> **Get the unit of analysis right before you assess a single force.** Change
> "buyer" from retailer to shopper and buyer power flips from strong to weak.
> Change "player" from manufacturer to retailer and the whole industry changes.
> State your definitions in one sentence at the top — it is cheap, and it makes
> the rest of the analysis auditable.

## 2.2 Worked example — Australian male toiletries, 2020

Straight from the supplied MarketLine profile. This is the industry Gillette
competes in.

| Force | MarketLine's assessment | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Buyer power | **Strong** | Hypermarkets and supermarkets take 49.1% of sales value; large buyers, low switching costs, and many run private-label lines (backward integration). Offset only by end-user brand loyalty, which motivates retailers to stock the popular brands |
| Supplier power | **Strong** | Ingredient and packaging suppliers are small relative to manufacturers, which *weakens* them; but some inputs have no substitutes and suppliers serve diverse client bases, which dominates |
| New entrants | **Strong** | The market is growing, which attracts entrants — despite scale economies, heavy advertising requirements, and Department of Health / Product Safety Australia regulation |
| Substitutes | **Weak** | Homemade alternatives are time-consuming and ineffective; aerosol foams and gels cannot be made domestically; razors and blades are very hard to substitute |
| Rivalry | **Strong** | Top four players hold 88.6% of value. Offset partly by strong forecast growth, which lets everyone grow without taking share |

Check the concentration figure:

```
Procter & Gamble        60.1%
Edgewell Personal Care  18.3%
Societe Bic              5.4%
Beiersdorf AG            4.8%
                        ─────
Top four                88.6%   ✓ matches MarketLine's stated 88.6%
Other                   11.4%
                        ─────
Total                  100.0%   ✓
```

### The razor-and-blades model as a barrier to entry

MarketLine's new-entrants section explains a mechanism worth knowing by name:

> *"razors are typically sold at little-to-no profit, or even at a loss, in the
> hope that recurring purchases of consumable products, in this case razor
> blades, by the same customers will make the business profitable."*

This is a **captive-consumables (razor-and-blades) model**. It matters for two
different parts of the unit:

- **Five Forces** — an entrant must fund years of loss-leading handle sales
  before blade revenue arrives, so the capital requirement is far higher than
  the manufacturing cost suggests.
- **Business Model Canvas** (Topic 3) — it is a *Revenue Streams* design
  choice, and it is what makes Gillette's installed base a Key Resource.

> **Two forces in this market run opposite to intuition, and both are stated
> in the supplied source.** New entrants: **strong**, not weak — market growth
> outweighs the barriers. Substitutes: **weak**, not strong — there is no
> non-razor way to get a close shave. Team A's submitted BEC asserted the
> reverse of both ("low entrant threat ... strong substitute threats"). If you
> are handed an industry profile and your Five Forces contradicts it, you need
> to either follow it or explain in writing why you departed from it.

### Substitutes versus rivals — the distinction markers test

A **substitute** meets the same need from *outside* the industry. A **rival**
meets it from inside.

| For Gillette | Classification |
|---|---|
| Schick disposable razor (Edgewell) | Rival — same industry |
| BIC disposable razor | Rival |
| Dollar Shave Club subscription blades | Rival — different business model, same industry |
| Growing a beard | Substitute — the need is met with no purchase at all |
| Laser hair removal | Substitute — different industry, same job |
| Electric shaver | Arguable. MarketLine's market definition covers *disposable razors and blades*, so an electric shaver sits outside it — a substitute. State which definition you are using |

> **Team A's BEC named "L'Oréal and Colgate-Palmolive" as the intense rivalry
> facing the case organisation.** They are real rivals of P&G, but in
> **haircare/skincare** and **oral hygiene** respectively. In male toiletries
> they hold no share at all — MarketLine's competitor set is Edgewell, Societe
> BIC and Beiersdorf. Naming rivals from the wrong market is one of the fastest
> ways to show a marker you did not read the industry profile you were given.

### The same firm, five different Five Forces

P&G competes in four Australian markets and the forces differ in every one:

| Force | Male toiletries (2020) | Haircare (2023) | Oral hygiene (2023) | Skincare (2022) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Rivalry | Strong | Boosted by global incumbents | Significant | **Weak** |
| Buyer power | Strong | Considerable | Significantly increased | **Moderate** |
| Supplier power | Strong | Raw-material quality critical | Restrains manufacturers | **Moderate** |
| New entrants | Strong | Niche entry possible | Extremely challenging | **Limited** |
| Substitutes | Weak | Weak | Weak (no direct substitutes) | Weak |

> **Five Forces is an *industry* framework, not a *company* framework.** You
> cannot run it once on "P&G" and be finished — P&G faces weak rivalry in
> skincare and strong rivalry in male toiletries at the same moment. Pick the
> market, name it, and analyse that. If the assignment is about the parent
> organisation across several markets, run the framework on the market that
> matters most and say why you chose it.

## 2.3 Market structures

Market forces starts with a structural read of the market.

| Structure | Sellers | Product | Entry | Price power |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Perfect competition** | Very many | Identical | Free | None — price taker |
| **Monopolistic competition** | Many | Differentiated | Easy | Some, from branding |
| **Oligopoly** | Few, each large | Either | Hard | Substantial; rivals react |
| **Monopoly** | **One** | Unique, no close substitute | Blocked | Full, subject to regulation |

### Applying it to the case brands

```
Male toiletries   P&G 60.1 | Edgewell 18.3 | Bic 5.4 | Beiersdorf 4.8 | Other 11.4
Haircare          L'Oréal 20.1 | Unilever 12.8 | P&G 9.4 | Henkel 5.8 | Other 51.9
Oral hygiene      Colgate-Palmolive 46.8 | Haleon 14.2 | J&J 10.4 | P&G 8.8 | Other 19.9
Skincare          L'Oréal 17.3 | Natura&Co 13.5 | P&G 10.1 | Estée Lauder 8.4 | Other 50.6
```

All four are **oligopolies**: a handful of large firms, differentiated
products, high entry barriers, and pricing that has to anticipate rivals'
reactions. Male toiletries and oral hygiene are *concentrated* oligopolies
with a dominant firm; haircare and skincare are looser, with more than half
of value outside the top four.

> **A 60% share is not a monopoly.** Team A's A1 stated that *"Gillette has a
> monopoly in the male skincare and grooming market (60% market share)"* and
> repeated it on the canvas. A monopoly means **one** seller and no close
> substitute. Gillette faces Schick at 17.5% brand share, plus BIC, plus
> Beiersdorf, plus private label. The correct description is a **concentrated
> oligopoly with a dominant firm** — or, if you want the economics term, a
> *dominant-firm* market. The strategic implication is completely different: a
> monopolist sets price; a dominant firm has to price against a live 18% rival.

Two smaller errors ride along with it, both worth avoiding:

- The market is **male toiletries** (aftershaves and colognes, men's
  disposable razors and blades, men's pre-shave and post-shave cosmetics), not
  "male skincare and grooming". Use the source's market definition verbatim.
- The 60.1% is **P&G's company share** of Australian male toiletries.
  MarketLine's own brand section does attribute 60.1% to the Gillette brand,
  but in the same sentence it names *"Fancy Feast"* as co-holder of that share
  — Fancy Feast is a Nestlé cat-food brand, and its appearance is a copy-paste
  error inside the MarketLine profile. Quote the company-level table (§7.1),
  which is clean, rather than the brand paragraph (§7.5), which is not.

## 2.4 Market growth — get the period right

Every MarketLine profile reports two growth rates and they are not
interchangeable:

- a **historical CAGR** over the review period, and
- a **forecast CAGR** over the forecast period.

```
CAGR = (End value / Start value)^(1 / number of years) − 1
```

Verified against the supplied profiles:

| Market | Review period | Historical CAGR | Forecast period | Forecast CAGR |
|---|---|---:|---|---:|
| Male toiletries | 2016–20 | **1.9%** | 2020–25 | **4.3%** |
| Skincare | 2017–22 | **2.6%** | 2022–27 | **4.6%** |
| Haircare | 2018–23 | **2.1%** | 2023–28 | **3.2%** |
| Oral hygiene | 2018–23 | **1.5%** | 2023–28 | **2.9%** |

Recomputed from the endpoints:

```
Male toiletries  (345.2 / 280.0)^(1/5) − 1 = 4.28%  ✓
Skincare       (1619.9 / 1293.7)^(1/5) − 1 = 4.60%  ✓
Haircare       (1256.5 / 1072.2)^(1/5) − 1 = 3.22%  ✓
Oral hygiene    (717.6 /  623.5)^(1/5) − 1 = 2.85%  ✓
```

> **Team A's haircare growth figure of "3.8% (2022–2024)" does not exist in
> the supplied profile.** The Haircare profile gives 2.1% for 2018–23 and 3.2%
> for 2023–28, and reports single-year growth of 2.0% in 2023. Neither the
> number nor the period matches. When you quote a growth rate, quote the
> period with it — *"3.2% CAGR, 2023–28 forecast"* — because a bare
> percentage cannot be checked, and a marker who cannot check it assumes it is
> invented.

## 2.5 The BCG growth–share matrix

Boston Consulting Group (1970). Two axes, four cells, one persistent mistake.

```
              HIGH market growth
                     │
      STAR           │       QUESTION MARK
  invest to hold     │      fix, sell or kill
──────────────────────┼──────────────────────
    CASH COW         │           DOG
  milk, fund others  │      divest or reposition
                     │
              LOW market growth

  HIGH relative ←─────────────→ LOW relative
   market share                 market share
```

### The axis almost everyone gets wrong

The horizontal axis is **relative market share**, not market share.

```
Relative market share = your share ÷ largest competitor's share

RMS > 1.0  →  you are the market leader        → "high"
RMS < 1.0  →  someone else leads               → "low"
```

The vertical axis is **market growth rate**, conventionally split at 10% a
year — though many texts use a lower threshold for mature consumer markets.
Whatever cut-off you use, **state it**.

### Recomputing the four case brands

Using the supplied MarketLine share tables:

| Brand | Market | Brand share | Largest rival brand | RMS | Growth | Correct cell |
|---|---|---:|---|---:|---:|---|
| Gillette | Male toiletries | 60.1% | Schick 17.5% | **3.43** | 4.3% | **Cash Cow** |
| Oral-B | Oral hygiene | 8.77% | Colgate 36.51% | **0.24** | 1.5–2.9% | **Dog** |
| Olay | Skincare | 7.12% | Aesop 9.96% | **0.71** | 4.6% | **Dog** |
| Pantene | Haircare | 5.22% | L'Oréal Excellence Crème 5.02% | **1.04** | 2.1–3.2% | **Cash Cow**, marginally |

```
Gillette  60.10 / 17.50 = 3.43   high share, low growth  → Cash Cow
Oral-B     8.77 / 36.51 = 0.24   low share,  low growth  → Dog
Olay       7.12 /  9.96 = 0.71   low share,  low growth  → Dog
Pantene    5.22 /  5.02 = 1.04   high share, low growth  → Cash Cow (just)
```

**Team A classified Olay as a Cash Cow. On the numbers it is a Dog.** Olay
does not lead the Australian skincare market on any reading — the brand sits
behind Aesop (9.96%), and P&G at company level (10.10%) sits behind L'Oréal
(17.33%), giving RMS 0.58. Low relative share plus 4.6% growth is the
bottom-right cell.

**Pantene is the interesting one**, because the answer flips with the level of
analysis:

```
Brand level    Pantene 5.22  vs  L'Oréal Excellence Crème 5.02  → RMS 1.04 → Cash Cow
Company level  P&G     9.36  vs  L'Oréal S.A.            20.06  → RMS 0.47 → Dog
```

Both computations are correct. They answer different questions. Say which
level you are working at and the analysis is defensible; leave it implicit and
it is not.

> **Team A's A1 recorded Pantene at "20% market share of the hair care
> market". That 20% is L'Oréal's.** MarketLine's 2023 haircare table reads
> L'Oréal 20.1%, Unilever 12.8%, P&G 9.4%, Henkel 5.8%. Pantene the brand is
> **5.22%**. The market leader's share was read off the top of the table and
> attributed to the case organisation's brand — which quadruples the position
> and turns a marginal Cash Cow into an apparently dominant one. Read down to
> the row with your company's name on it.

> **Absolute share is not a BCG axis.** A brand with 12% share in a market
> where the leader has 40% is a *low* relative share (0.30). A brand with 12%
> where the leader has 10% is a *high* relative share (1.20). Same 12%,
> opposite cells, opposite strategic advice. If your BCG working never divides
> by anything, you have not used the framework.

### What each cell means for strategy

| Cell | Cash position | Strategy |
|---|---|---|
| **Star** | Generates and consumes cash | Invest to defend leadership; it becomes the future Cash Cow |
| **Cash Cow** | Generates cash surplus | Milk it; fund Stars and Question Marks; defend share cheaply |
| **Question Mark** | Consumes cash | Decide: invest heavily to build share, or exit. Do not drip-feed |
| **Dog** | Roughly cash-neutral, low return | Divest, harvest, or reposition into a niche where RMS is high |

## 2.6 Consumer buying behaviour

Market forces also asks *why* people buy. Ramya and Mohamed Ali (2016),
supplied as an A1 reading, structure it as a stimulus–response model: external
stimuli and the marketing mix enter the buyer's **"black box"**, interact with
buyer characteristics and decision processes, and produce a purchase decision.
The marketer's job is to understand the box.

Seven questions frame any consumer analysis:

```
Who is the market, and how much power do they have?
What do they buy?      Why do they buy?    Who is involved in buying?
How do they buy?       When do they buy?   Where do they buy?
```

Five groups of factors sit inside the box:

| Factor group | Components | Marketing implication |
|---|---|---|
| **Psychological** | Motivation; perception | Needs become motives when intense enough; perception is a constructed cognitive map, not a photograph |
| **Social** | Family; reference groups; roles and status | People buy products that signal their role and status |
| **Cultural** | Culture; sub-culture; social class | Culture is learned, shared and slow to change — work within it, don't try to shift it |
| **Economic** | Personal and family income; income expectations; savings; liquid assets; consumer credit | Discretionary income, not gross income, drives non-essential purchases |
| **Personal** | Age; occupation; income; lifestyle | Needs change across the life cycle |

Three mechanisms from the reading that are worth knowing by name:

- **Biogenic vs psychogenic needs.** Biogenic arise from physiological tension
  (thirst, hunger). Psychogenic arise from psychological tension (recognition,
  esteem). Grooming products sell almost entirely on psychogenic needs.
- **Chunking and categorisation.** The brain pigeonholes information into
  related chunks, so a sight and a sound experienced together are stored as one
  item. This is why consistent brand assets work.
- **The law of primacy.** Past experience shapes the interpretation of later
  experience — first impressions are disproportionately durable.

### Disposable versus discretionary income

The reading separates two things students routinely merge:

```
Disposable income     = gross income − tax and compulsory deductions
Discretionary income  = disposable income − the cost of basic necessities
```

Shopping goods, durables and luxuries are bought out of **discretionary**
income. That is exactly why Team A's macro argument works: inflation raises
the cost of necessities, so discretionary income falls faster than disposable
income does, and premium personal-care lines are hit before staples are.

> **"Consumers have less money" is not an analysis; "discretionary income
> compresses faster than disposable income" is.** In a cost-of-living squeeze,
> a category's exposure depends on whether it is bought from the disposable
> pool or the discretionary one. Razor blades and toothpaste sit close to
> necessity; premium anti-ageing skincare does not. Same inflation, very
> different demand response — and saying which is which is the mark.

## Checkpoint

<details><summary>1. Gillette holds 60.1% of Australian male toiletries. Is that a monopoly? What is it, and why does the label matter?</summary>

No. It is a **concentrated oligopoly with a dominant firm**. Edgewell holds
18.3%, Societe BIC 5.4%, Beiersdorf 4.8%, others 11.4%; the top four total
88.6%.

The label matters because it changes the strategy. A monopolist sets price
against demand alone. A dominant firm in an oligopoly must price and promote
anticipating a live 18% rival's response, and remains exposed to retailer
private label. Team A's canvas called it a monopoly, and then had to argue that
disruption threatened "Gillette's immutable market share" — the two claims
undercut each other.
</details>

<details><summary>2. Brand A has 15% of a market. The leader has 45%. The market grows 3% a year. Which BCG cell, and what do you do?</summary>

```
RMS = 15 / 45 = 0.33  → low relative share
Growth = 3%           → low growth
```

**Dog.** Divest, harvest for cash, or reposition into a defensible niche where
relative share can exceed 1.0.

The trap: 15% *sounds* substantial. The BCG axis is relative share, and at
one third of the leader's scale Brand A has neither cost advantage nor pricing
power.
</details>

<details><summary>3. The supplied industry profile says the threat of substitutes in male toiletries is weak. Your group wants to argue it is strong because Dollar Shave Club is taking share. Is the group right?</summary>

No — they have confused a **substitute** with a **rival**. Dollar Shave Club
sells razors and blades: same industry, same product category, different
business model and channel. That is competitive rivalry, and it belongs in the
rivalry force.

A genuine substitute for male toiletries is something that meets the grooming
need without buying the product: growing a beard, laser hair removal, or (on
MarketLine's definition, which covers disposable razors and blades) an electric
shaver.

Where the group has a real point is that a subscription entrant *does* raise
the rivalry assessment and weaken the razor-and-blades barrier to entry. Put it
in the right force and the argument is strong.
</details>

<details><summary>4. Why does Team A's "intense rivalry from L'Oréal and Colgate-Palmolive" fail as an Industry forces entry for Gillette?</summary>

Because neither competes in male toiletries. L'Oréal leads Australian haircare
(20.1%) and skincare (17.3%); Colgate-Palmolive leads oral hygiene (46.8%).
Neither appears in the male toiletries share table at all.

The rivals that belong there are **Edgewell Personal Care** (Schick, 18.3%
company / 17.5% brand), **Societe BIC** (5.4%) and **Beiersdorf** (4.8%), plus
retailer private label — which MarketLine names explicitly as a source of
buyer power.

If the analysis is at parent level across all four markets, then naming
L'Oréal and Colgate-Palmolive is fine — but then you must say which market
each one contests, because a marker cannot otherwise tell whether you knew.
</details>

<details><summary>5. A profile reports a market grew "by 2%" and also gives a "CAGR of 2.1%" and a "forecast CAGR of 3.2%". What are these three numbers?</summary>

- **2%** — single-year growth in the most recent year (2023 for haircare).
- **2.1%** — compound annual growth over the **review** (historical) period,
  2018–23.
- **3.2%** — compound annual growth over the **forecast** period, 2023–28.

They are not alternatives. Quote whichever answers the question you are
asking, and always state the period. For BCG's growth axis you generally want
the forecast rate, because the matrix is about where to put money next.
</details>

## Summary

- Define **players, buyers and suppliers** before assessing any force. In male
  toiletries the buyers are supermarkets, not shoppers.
- Australian male toiletries 2020: buyer power **strong**, supplier power
  **strong**, new entrants **strong**, substitutes **weak**, rivalry
  **strong**. Two of those run against intuition.
- Substitutes come from **outside** the industry; competing business models
  inside it are rivalry.
- Five Forces is per-market. P&G faces weak rivalry in skincare and strong
  rivalry in male toiletries simultaneously.
- All four case markets are **oligopolies**. 60.1% is a dominant firm, not a
  monopoly.
- The BCG horizontal axis is **relative** market share (yours ÷ leader's),
  crossing at 1.0. Verified: Gillette 3.43 Cash Cow, Oral-B 0.24 Dog, Olay 0.71
  **Dog**, Pantene 1.04 Cash Cow at brand level and 0.47 Dog at company level.
- Quote every growth rate **with its period**, and say whether it is historical
  or forecast.
- Consumer behaviour: five factor groups feeding a black box. Discretionary
  income, not disposable income, drives non-essential purchases.
