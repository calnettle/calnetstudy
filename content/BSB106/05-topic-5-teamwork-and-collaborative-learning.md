# TOPIC 5 — Teamwork and Collaborative Learning

Assignments 1 and 2 are group tasks worth 50% of the unit between them, and
both are *"assessed using the team contract and peer evaluation"* as well as
the analysis itself. The teamwork component is not administrative overhead
wrapped around the real assessment. It is part of the real assessment, it has
a research literature behind it, and the A1 brief requires the contract to be
**research-informed** — meaning cited.

This topic covers the theory that makes a contract research-informed, the
mechanics of QUT's peer evaluation and how the arithmetic actually works, and
the critical reflection that closes Assignment 3.

## 5.1 The two principles everything else hangs off

Meijer, Hoekstra, Brouwer and Strijbos (2020) — supplied as a reading in the
A1 folder — open by naming two principles as *"crucial to the design of
collaborative learning"*. Learn both definitions precisely, because the entire
argument about assessment methods runs off them.

```
Individual accountability   the degree to which individual students are held
                            accountable for their contribution to the
                            collaborative learning              (Slavin 1980)

Positive interdependence    the extent to which the performance of a single
                            group member depends on the performance of the
                            other group members                (Johnson 1981)
```

They pull in opposite directions, and that tension is the whole problem.

- Push **individual accountability** up (mark everyone separately) and you
  remove the reason to collaborate — students divide subtasks, staple them
  together, and never genuinely interact.
- Push **positive interdependence** up (one mark for the group) and you remove
  the reason to contribute — free-riding becomes rational.

> **These are not two words for "everyone should pull their weight".**
> Individual accountability is about *consequences attaching to the
> individual*. Positive interdependence is about *outcomes being causally
> linked between members*. A contract clause that says "we will each do our
> fair share" is accountability. A clause that says "each member teaches the
> others their assigned reading so no one can pass without the others" is
> interdependence. A strong contract has clauses of both kinds and says which
> is which.

## 5.2 The three assessment methods and their trade-offs

Meijer et al. assess three commonly used methods against two challenges:
**construct validity** (does the assessment measure what it claims to — here,
individual domain-specific ability) and **behavioural alignment** (does it
provoke the behaviour the teacher wanted).

| Method | Individual accountability | Positive interdependence | Main construct-validity problem | Behaviour it provokes |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Group assessment** — one score for the group | Absent | Fostered | The group score "will appear as if it were an" individual score on the transcript, when it is not | Free-riding, social loafing, the sucker effect, performance- rather than learning-orientation |
| **Individual assessment** — each member marked on their own subtask | Fostered | Not fostered | Never truly individual — performance is still shaped by the group, so it is at best a *"collaboration-moderated individual assessment"* | Superficial coordination only: divide subtasks, decide who staples it together. Can create rivalry and impaired information sharing |
| **Group + intra-group peer assessment** — group score moderated by peer ratings | Partly restored | Fostered | Peer ratings measure the *process*, which does not necessarily tell you about the *product*; ratings are biased; no agreed conversion formula exists | Reduces free-riding, but introduces strategic rating behaviour |

QUT uses the third method. That is why the traps below matter to your mark
rather than being abstract.

> **"Construct validity" has a definition and questions can ask for it.** A
> measure accurately measuring what it intends to measure (Messick 1995, as
> cited in Meijer et al., 2020). In this context the construct of interest is
> the *individual student's* domain-specific ability, because that is what a
> degree certifies. A group grade is invalid to the extent that it fails to
> discriminate between members of the group.

## 5.3 The four misaligned behaviours — get the definitions apart

Students routinely use these as synonyms. They are not, and a question that
gives you a scenario is testing whether you can tell them apart.

| Behaviour | Definition (Meijer et al., 2020, drawing on Salomon & Globerson 1989) | Tell-tale sign |
|---|---|---|
| **Free-riding** | A member does not perform a fair share of the work — either because they are unmotivated, or because they assume the group is better off without their contribution | Does little, and the work still gets done |
| **Social loafing** | A member invests *less effort than they would working individually* | Would have produced better work alone |
| **Sucker effect** | Members are taken for a free-ride by others, so one member ends up doing all the work and is thereby the "sucker" | One member's output balloons to cover the gap |
| **Performance-orientation** | When stakes are high, the group optimises for the *grade* rather than for learning — typically by giving each task to whoever is already best at it | Nobody learns anything new; the mark is fine |

The fourth is the subtle one, and it is the one that quietly happens in almost
every good group. Allocating tasks "based on individual strengths" — which is
exactly what Team A's contract commits to, twice — maximises the mark and
minimises the learning. It is not cheating and it is not laziness. It is a
rational response to a high-stakes group assessment, and Meijer et al. name it
as a misalignment for that reason.

> **The sucker effect is the *victim's* condition, not the free-rider's.** If a
> scenario describes one member doing everyone's work, the answer is the sucker
> effect. If it describes the member who stopped turning up, the answer is
> free-riding. The same incident produces both at once, from opposite ends —
> say so, and name who is experiencing which.

## 5.4 Why peer assessment is biased, and which biases have names

Meijer et al. list the ways intra-group peer assessment goes wrong. Three are
worth knowing by name.

**The reciprocity effect** (Magin 2001). The relationship between peers
influences how they rate each other. Diprose, Judd and Morris (1997) found
students who rated a peer's contribution poorly **feared their own rating
would be marked down in retribution** — so they inflated.

**Formative validity without summative validity.** Sridharan, Tai and Boud
(2019) found students *can* assess peers validly in a formative context but
not in a summative one. When the rating counts towards a grade, students are
reluctant to penalise peers they have to keep working with. QUT's peer
evaluation is summative and it happens twice, with the same team — the second
one is exactly the high-stakes, ongoing-relationship case the finding warns
about.

**No agreed moderation formula.** The usual basis is Goldfinch's (1994)
PA-factor, used in systems such as SPARKPLUS, but Meijer et al. state plainly
that *"to date there are no agreed upon practices or formulas to convert a
group score or grade into individual scores or grades with the help of
intra-group peer assessment."* Corrections have been proposed for dishonesty,
for self-rating inflation and for the size of the swing the formula produces.

Alongside these, they list friendship, peer pressure, harsh marking after a
conflict, and deliberate up- or down-grading to improve one's own relative
position.

> **"Peer evaluation is unfair" is not the point a good answer makes.** The
> literature's finding is sharper and more useful: peer ratings are a
> reasonably valid measure of the collaborative *process*, and a poor measure
> of the collaborative *product*, and their validity **collapses when they
> become summative**. That sentence answers a question about peer assessment's
> limitations properly. Complaining about fairness does not.

## 5.5 How QUT's peer evaluation actually computes

Both briefs describe the mechanism in one sentence, and the detail in it
decides how you should behave.

> *"The mark received will be based on the average of the peer evaluation
> scores awarded (excluding scores members give themselves)."*

The scale runs 1 to 7, with only the endpoints defined:

```
1  did not make any contribution to the team and its performance
7  consistently enabled the team to fulfil its goals; demonstrated
   leadership; excellent communication and engagement; completed all
   work to a high standard within agreed timeframes
```

You are required to rate **all** members *including yourself* — but your own
score is stripped out before your average is computed. Worked on a four-person
team, where each member receives three usable ratings:

```
Member    ratings received from the other three     average
Callum          7   7   6                            6.67
Aidan           6   6   7                            6.33
Izaan           5   6   5                            5.33
Josh            2   3   2                            2.33

Josh, if his own 7 were counted:  (2 + 3 + 2 + 7) / 4 = 3.50
Josh, as actually computed:       (2 + 3 + 2)     / 3 = 2.33
```

Two hard consequences sit in the briefs:

- **Not submitting the peer evaluation = zero for the peer evaluation
  component.** It is not a courtesy; it is a mark you forfeit by inaction.
- **Receiving an average of 1–2 = you may receive zero for the whole
  assignment**, not just the peer component, unless HiQ has granted special
  consideration.

Josh's 2.33 above clears that band by a third of a point. A single rating
moving from 3 to 2 would have taken the average to 2.00 and put the entire
assignment at risk.

> **Rating yourself 7 is worth exactly nothing to you, and it can cost you.**
> Self-scores are excluded from your own average, so inflating yours changes
> no mark of yours. What it does change is the *ratio* between your self-rating
> and the rating your teammates gave you — which is precisely the "assessing
> oneself higher than fellow group members" bias Meijer et al. say corrections
> have had to be written for. Rate yourself honestly and spend the effort on
> rating others accurately.

> **The 1–2 cliff is a cliff, not a slope.** Between 7 and 3 the peer score
> moves your mark. Below 2 it can void the assignment. If a teammate is heading
> for that band, the contract's escalation clause exists so the problem is
> documented *before* the rating, not discovered by them afterwards. An
> undocumented dispute settled by everyone quietly rating someone a 1 is the
> worst version of every bias in section 5.4.

## 5.6 Writing a research-informed team contract

The A1 brief: create a team contract using the Canvas template, **4 pages
maximum plus an activity log appendix**, research-informed, signed by every
member, submitted first as a draft for tutor feedback. Assignment 2 requires a
**revised** version of the same contract — revised, not resubmitted.

The template's sections, and what each is actually asking for:

| Section | What earns credit |
|---|---|
| Preamble | The research. Cited studies justifying the choices made in the sections below |
| Purpose and goals | What this specific team is trying to produce, not teamwork in general |
| Leadership and decision-making | Which model, why that model, and how decisions get made when the team disagrees |
| Collaborative learning (positive interdependence) | A mechanism that makes members' outcomes depend on each other |
| Individual accountability | A mechanism with a consequence attached |
| Conduct and communication | Channels, response expectations, standard of behaviour |
| Meetings | A time and a day, stated |
| Task allocation and deadlines | Internal deadlines ahead of the real one |
| Conflict resolution | An escalation ladder, ending somewhere outside the team |
| Signatures | Every contributing member |

### Worked example — Team A's revised contract

The evidence base and what each source was used to support:

| Source | Claim it supports in the contract |
|---|---|
| Markulis, Jassawalla & Sashittal (2006) | Emergent (unchosen) leadership should be avoided as inefficient; assigned leadership promotes accountability and coordination by allocating roles from the outset |
| Mohammed & Angell (2003) | Differences in analytical thinking, cognitive diversity and problem solving strengthen written work by adding perspectives |
| Behfar, Peterson, Mannix & Trochim (2008) | **Task conflict** improves performance through critical evaluation; **relationship conflict** disrupts collaboration. Teams that handle conflict directly reach solutions |
| Johnson & Johnson (1999) | Positive interdependence raises motivation; perceiving your success as linked to teammates' raises productivity |
| Meijer et al. (2020) | Peer and self-evaluation as tools to promote accountability and limit free-riding |

Five sources, each doing a distinct job, each tied to a section of the
contract rather than floated in the preamble and abandoned. That structure is
what "research-informed" means.

The interdependence clause is the strongest thing in the document, because it
describes a mechanism rather than an intention:

> *"having mutual learning goals such as learn an assigned material each and
> ensure all members learn it from the effort of one another. This creates a
> sink or swim together mindset."*

That is Johnson's definition operationalised — if one member does not learn
their material, the others cannot cover it.

> **Team A's contract ticks "similar personalities" for team formation, and
> then argues the opposite in its own preamble.** The Mohammed and Angell
> paragraph is a case for *cognitive and personality diversity* improving the
> quality of written work; the tick box selects a similar-personality team.
> Both positions are defensible in the literature — homogeneous teams
> coordinate faster, heterogeneous teams produce better analysis — but you
> cannot cite the diversity finding and then select against it without a
> sentence explaining the trade-off. A marker reading the preamble and then the
> tick box sees a contract that did not read itself. One clause fixes it:
> *"the team formed on similar personalities to reduce coordination cost, and
> mitigates the resulting loss of cognitive diversity by rotating who
> critiques each section."*

> **Team A's contract also lists four members. The brief says teams are
> "typically 5 members".** Not an error — teams shrink — but it changes the
> peer-evaluation arithmetic: with four members each person's average rests on
> **three** ratings, so one hostile rating moves it by a third of a point
> rather than a quarter. Smaller team, more volatile peer score.

### The activity log

Required as an appendix to the contract in both A1 and A2: *"a record of"* what
the team did. Team A's log records date, members present, activity
description, progress achieved, hours spent and notes — five entries across
four weeks for Assignment 2, with hours ranging from one to 2.5 per person per
session.

Its real function is evidentiary. If a peer-evaluation dispute reaches the
tutor, the log is the only contemporaneous record of who was in the room.

> **Write the log at the time, not at the end.** A log reconstructed the night
> before submission is easy to spot — the entries are uniform, the hours are
> round, and nothing is ever recorded as going wrong. It is also useless as
> evidence, which is the one thing it exists for.

## 5.7 The critical reflection (Assignment 3, 500 words)

The second half of the individual assessment: *"Provide a critical reflection
on your learning in the unit"*, 500 words ±10%. The submitted example is
structured as direct answers to five set questions:

```
1. How did you find using research evidence and theory to guide your
   understanding of the unit's topics?
2. How did you find integrating that evidence and theory into your
   assessment items to support your arguments?
3. Why is it important to use research evidence and theory to support
   understanding and strengthen arguments?
4. How might you use this approach in future?
5. In what ways might this be beneficial personally and professionally?
```

Five questions, 500 words: about **100 words each**, which is one short
paragraph. There is no room for a preamble and no room for a conclusion.

The reflection is the one place in the unit written in **first person** —
everything else is explicitly third person. It is also the one place where
"at first I found this overwhelming" is the right register: a reflection that
reports no difficulty reports no learning.

> **"Critical" reflection means naming what was hard and what changed, with a
> specific instance.** *"Frameworks helped me structure my thinking"* is a
> statement anyone could write without doing the unit. *"I initially applied
> Porter's generic strategies to the subscription recommendation as
> differentiation focus, and only recognised on revision that a nationwide
> channel is broad scope"* is a reflection — it names a specific error, the
> correction, and the concept learned. Same word count, entirely different
> mark.

> **Confirm the reflection questions on Canvas before writing.** The five above
> are taken from a Semester 1 2025 submission, not from a brief — the
> Assignment 3 instruction document is not in the source folder. The word
> limit (500, ±10%) is confirmed by the Tutorial 10 deck; the questions are
> not.

## Checkpoint

<details><summary>1. Define individual accountability and positive interdependence, and explain why an assessment cannot maximise both.</summary>

**Individual accountability** — the degree to which individual students are
held accountable for their contribution to the collaborative learning
(Slavin 1980). **Positive interdependence** — the extent to which one group
member's performance depends on the performance of the others (Johnson 1981).

They trade off. A pure group mark maximises interdependence (everyone sinks or
swims together) but removes accountability, so free-riding pays. A pure
individual mark maximises accountability but removes interdependence, so the
rational strategy is to split the task into isolated subtasks and never
collaborate — Meijer et al. call this turning a collaborative assignment into
"an individual assignment in disguise".

Group assessment moderated by intra-group peer assessment is the attempt to
have both, and QUT uses it. It works partially, at the cost of importing peer
rating bias.
</details>

<details><summary>2. A four-person team: one member stops responding, and another rewrites their section the night before submission. Name the behaviours precisely.</summary>

The absent member is **free-riding** — not performing a fair share of the work.

The member who rewrote their section is experiencing the **sucker effect** —
taken for a free-ride, ending up doing the work of others.

If the team then assigns every remaining task to whoever is already strongest
at it in order to protect the mark, that is a **performance-oriented** rather
than learning-oriented approach — also a misalignment, even though it looks
like good management.

**Social loafing** is a different thing again: investing less effort than you
would working alone. It applies to a member who is present and producing, but
producing below their solo standard.
</details>

<details><summary>3. On a five-person team you receive 6, 6, 5, 7 from your teammates and rate yourself 7. What is your peer evaluation average, and what would it be if self-scores counted?</summary>

```
Excluding self (as QUT computes it):  (6 + 6 + 5 + 7) / 4 = 6.00
If self-scores were counted:          (6 + 6 + 5 + 7 + 7) / 5 = 6.20
```

**6.00.** The self-rating is excluded, so it changes nothing about your own
mark. On a five-member team you receive four usable ratings; on a four-member
team, three — which makes a small team's average more sensitive to a single
outlying rating.
</details>

<details><summary>4. Why does Sridharan, Tai and Boud's (2019) finding matter specifically to BSB106's peer evaluation?</summary>

They found students assess peers validly in a **formative** context but not a
**summative** one — when the rating counts, students are reluctant to penalise
peers honestly.

BSB106's peer evaluation is summative (it carries marks, and an average of 1–2
can void the assignment) and it runs **twice with the same team**, in
Assignment 1 and again in Assignment 2. That is the worst case for the finding:
high stakes plus an ongoing relationship, which is also the setup for the
reciprocity effect — rating a peer down and fearing retribution in the next
round.

The practical response is the one the contract is for: document contribution
problems in the activity log as they happen, and raise them through the
conflict-resolution ladder, so the rating reflects a recorded history rather
than a private judgement.
</details>

<details><summary>5. A contract's individual accountability section reads: "All members agree to contribute equally and communicate respectfully." What is missing?</summary>

A **mechanism** and a **consequence**. As written it is a statement of
intention, which is what every team already intends.

Accountability needs something that attaches an outcome to an individual:
named task ownership with internal deadlines 2–3 days before submission, a
progress review at each weekly meeting, contribution recorded in the activity
log, and a stated escalation path if the deadline is missed — structured
discussion, then mediation, then referral to the tutor.

It is also missing the research. The A1 brief requires the contract to be
research-informed, so each mechanism should point at the study that justifies
it — Meijer et al. (2020) for peer and self-evaluation as an accountability
tool, Behfar et al. (2008) for handling conflict directly rather than
avoiding it.
</details>

## Summary

- Teamwork is assessed content. Both A1 and A2 are marked *"using the team
  contract and peer evaluation"* as well as the analysis.
- Two principles, in tension: **individual accountability** (Slavin 1980) and
  **positive interdependence** (Johnson 1981).
- Three assessment methods. Group assessment kills accountability; individual
  assessment kills interdependence; group plus intra-group peer assessment
  splits the difference and imports rating bias.
- **Construct validity** = the measure measures what it intends to. Here the
  construct is individual domain-specific ability.
- Four distinct misalignments: free-riding, social loafing, the sucker effect,
  and performance-orientation. Do not use them interchangeably.
- Peer rating biases: friendship, peer pressure, harsh marking after conflict,
  strategic grading, and the **reciprocity effect** (Magin 2001). Peer ratings
  are valid formatively, not summatively (Sridharan, Tai & Boud, 2019). No
  agreed moderation formula exists.
- QUT: 1–7 scale, average of ratings received **excluding your own**. No
  submission = zero for the component. An average of 1–2 can void the whole
  assignment.
- The contract is research-informed: five or so sources, each tied to a
  specific clause. Keep the leadership and formation choices consistent with
  the studies cited for them.
- Keep the activity log contemporaneously — it is the only evidence in a
  dispute.
- The A3 reflection is 500 words across five questions, first person, and
  earns its marks by naming a specific difficulty and what changed.
