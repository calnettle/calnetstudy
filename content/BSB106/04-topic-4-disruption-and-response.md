# TOPIC 4 — Disruption and Response

Module 3, and Assignment 3 — the 50% individual assessment. Everything built
in Topics 1 to 3 gets stress-tested here.

The task, from the Tutorial 10 deck: *"Analyse the impact of a major
disruption on the business model from Assignment 2 and produce a written
report"*, plus a critical reflection. You choose one of six hypothetical
disruptions described in the Assignment 3 instruction document, and each member
of your original team must choose a different one.

> **The Assignment 3 brief is not in the source folder.** The six disruptions
> are described there and nowhere else in the material available for these
> notes, so only the one Cal analysed is known: *a series of severe weather
> events damages many of Australia's ports, drastically limiting shipping
> container access*. Get the current list from Canvas — the framework below
> works on any of them.

## 4.1 The three-step shape of the task

Tutorial 10 sets it out as a sequence, and the report follows it:

```
1. SITUATE   Where does this disruption sit in the Business Environment Canvas?
                  ↓
2. IMPACT    Which parts of the Business Model Canvas does it break?
                  ↓   (Problem Diamond)
3. RESPOND   What is the best response, and how does the BMC change?
                      (Solution Diamond)
```

Skip step 1 and the report reads as a list of operational problems with no
environmental cause. Skip step 3's second half — the revised canvas — and you
have written a recommendation, not a business-model analysis.

## 4.2 Situating the disruption in the BEC

The tutorial's instruction is precise: *"identify where within the Business
Environment Canvas the disruption would be denoted"*, with the tip that
**more than one quadrant may be affected**.

Worked on the port disruption:

| Quadrant | How the disruption lands |
|---|---|
| **Key trends** | Environmental — increasing frequency of severe weather events driving supply-chain fragility up the corporate risk agenda |
| **Macro-economic forces** | Commodity and freight cost pressure; air-freight substitution raises landed cost per unit economy-wide |
| **Industry forces** | Rivals with domestic supply chains keep shelves stocked; the threat from local substitutes rises sharply while imports are stalled |
| **Market forces** | Consumer buying behaviour shifts — panic buying, then forced brand switching when the preferred brand is absent |

Cal's report named three of these four (macro-economic, market, industry). The
**Key trends** link is the one most often left out, and it is the one that
justifies a *structural* response rather than a temporary one. If severe
weather is a trend rather than an event, buying more warehouse space this
quarter does not solve anything.

> **A disruption that lands in only one quadrant is usually a disruption you
> have under-analysed.** The tutorial says so explicitly. Naming all the
> quadrants it touches costs three sentences and directly earns the "situates
> the disruption in the market landscape" credit.

## 4.3 The Double Diamond

From the UK Design Council, and the organising framework for the whole
assignment. Two diamonds, four stages, alternating divergent and convergent
thinking.

```
              PROBLEM DIAMOND              SOLUTION DIAMOND

                 ◆                              ◆
               ╱   ╲                          ╱   ╲
Disruption ──▶╱     ╲────────────────────────╱     ╲──▶ Market
              ╲     ╱                        ╲     ╱     offering
               ╲   ╱                          ╲   ╱
                 ◆                              ◆

           Problem      Problem          Solution     Solution
         articulation  selection        generation   selection

    t0 ─────────── t1 ────────── t2 ─────────── t3 ────────── t4

         DIVERGENT   CONVERGENT       DIVERGENT    CONVERGENT
```

| Stage | Thinking | What you produce |
|---|---|---|
| **Problem articulation** | Divergent | A long, unfiltered brainstorm of possible impacts |
| **Problem selection** | Convergent | The most significant impact(s), ranked |
| **Solution generation** | Divergent | A range of possible responses |
| **Solution selection** | Convergent | The single preferred response, justified |

The diamond shape *is* the argument: each stage widens, then narrows. A
report that goes straight from "here is the disruption" to "here is my
recommendation" has skipped both wide phases, and it shows — the recommendation
arrives with no alternatives to be preferred over.

> **Divergent and convergent thinking are not the same skill, and doing them
> at the same time destroys both.** The tutorial's own advice: brainstorm
> impacts *"without disregarding ideas"*, spread it over a couple of days, and
> **"allow a day or so"** before converging. Judging an idea as you generate it
> is the single most common way to end up with three obvious impacts and no
> insight.

### The Double Diamond's other names

The UK Design Council's original four phases are **Discover, Define, Develop,
Deliver**. BSB106 relabels them problem articulation, problem selection,
solution generation, solution selection. Use the unit's labels in the
assignment; recognise the originals if they appear in a question.

## 4.4 Problem Diamond — articulation (divergent)

The prompt from the tutorial: *"How might the value proposition, and other
segments of the BMC that make up the desirability, feasibility and viability
portions of the business model be impacted by the disruption?"*

So the brainstorm is **structured by the BMC**, not free-floating. Walk the
nine blocks and ask what breaks. For the port disruption:

| Block | Possible impact |
|---|---|
| Key partners | Retailers deprioritise an unreliable supplier; freight partners cannot deliver |
| Key activities | Manufacturing continues offshore but cannot be landed; distribution planning collapses |
| Key resources | The multinational distribution network — a stated strength — becomes the point of failure |
| Value proposition | "Accessibility" is half the proposition, and accessibility is exactly what fails |
| Customer relationships | Loyalty erodes when the product is simply absent |
| Channels | Retail shelf space is lost and refilled by rivals |
| Customer segments | B2B customers (hotels, airlines) with contracted supply cannot be served |
| Cost structure | Air freight replaces sea freight; cost per unit rises sharply |
| Revenue streams | Volume falls; the blade annuity is interrupted at the point of handle switching |

Nine blocks, nine candidate impacts, in one pass. That is why the tutorial
tells you to brainstorm *through* the BMC.

## 4.5 Problem Diamond — selection (convergent)

The tutorial gives an explicit five-step procedure. Use it, and say you used
it.

```
Step 1  Remove impacts that are non-consequential or not very substantial.
Step 2  Remove any impacts that could be accommodated within the current
        business model.
Step 3  Consolidate inter-related impacts into one general description.
Step 4  Rank the list by significance — how challenging each is to the
        current business model.
Step 5  Identify the most significant impact(s) based on that ranking.
```

Step 2 is the one that does the real work and the one most often skipped. If
Gillette can absorb an impact by rerouting freight without changing anything
structural, it is not a business-model impact and it does not belong in a
business-model report. **The test is not "how bad is it" — it is "does the
current model still work".**

### Worked example — the port disruption

Cal's report converged on three impacts:

1. Product shortages and panic buying — empty shelves in supermarkets and
   pharmacies.
2. Increased operating costs — air freight replacing sea freight, raising cost
   per unit and compressing margin.
3. **Competitor advantage for local brands** — domestic-supply-chain rivals
   stay in stock, take shelf space and win customers.

And selected the third as most significant, on the grounds that it is *"both
an immediate threat to sales and places Gillette at a strategic disadvantage
in the long-term"*.

That selection is right, and the reason it is right is worth stating more
sharply than the report does. Impacts 1 and 2 are **recoverable**: when the
ports reopen, shelves refill and freight costs normalise. Impact 3 is
**not** — a customer who switches razor handle systems takes the entire future
blade annuity with them. Under a razor-and-blades model, share lost during a
stockout does not automatically come back when supply resumes.

> **Rank impacts by persistence, not by size.** A large impact that reverses
> when the disruption ends is less significant than a smaller one that locks
> in. That is exactly step 4's "how challenging to the current business model"
> — and framing it that way turns a ranking assertion into an argument.

## 4.6 Solution Diamond

Same structure, applied to responses. **Generate** widely, then **select** one
and justify the choice against the alternatives.

Cal's report evaluated three responses:

| Response | BMC blocks strengthened | Benefit | Limitation |
|---|---|---|---|
| Hold larger local inventory buffers of core products | Key resources, Channels | Immediate availability and shelf presence | Warehousing and inventory cost; no competitive differentiation; short-term only |
| Local assembly / packaging partnerships (import components in bulk, finish in Australia) | Key activities, Key partners | Shortens time to shelf; enables "locally assembled" positioning | Partner screening, cost, quality-control risk; short-to-mid term |
| **Accelerate direct-to-consumer** — e-commerce platform, subscriptions, online-exclusive bundles | Channels, Customer relationships, Revenue streams | Bypasses disrupted retail logistics; direct stock control; first-party data | Requires new capability and partners |

The DTC response was selected. The justification structure is the part to
copy: it is preferred **because it solves both the immediate availability
problem and the long-term strategic problem**, where the other two solve only
one. That is a comparative argument, not a preference.

> **Never present one response.** The Solution Diamond requires generation
> before selection, and a marker cannot award "solution selection" credit if
> nothing was rejected. Three responses with stated limitations, then a
> comparative choice, is the minimum shape.

## 4.7 Revising the business model

The final section. The Assignment 3 report closes by rewriting the affected
BMC blocks and marking the changes on the canvas itself (Cal's revised BMC
marks all changes in red, with the revised BEC in Appendix A and the revised
BMC in Appendix B).

For the DTC response, four blocks changed:

| Block | Revision | Lens |
|---|---|---|
| **Key partners** | Add local warehousing providers, third-party fulfilment, e-commerce platform, payment processors, customer-support systems | Feasibility |
| **Customer relationships** | Move from broad transactional engagement to personalised relationships built on first-party subscription data; loyalty programs, automated communication, recommendations | Desirability |
| **Channels** | Add owned digital channels — Gillette Australia website and mobile app — alongside retail; digital marketing, SEO and paid social to redirect demand away from empty shelves | Desirability + feasibility |
| **Revenue streams** | Add subscription income, online-exclusive bundles, and higher-margin direct transactions from control over pricing and delivery | Viability |

All three lenses are touched, which is what the assignment asks for. Note that
**Key resources** arguably should have changed too — first-party customer data
and e-commerce capability are new assets Gillette's existing canvas does not
list. Adding that block would have strengthened the feasibility argument at no
cost to the word count.

## 4.8 Theory — use it, but use it correctly

The report cites four frameworks. Three are applied well; one is not.

| Framework | Use in the report | Assessment |
|---|---|---|
| **Porter's Five Forces (2008)** | Threat of substitutes rises as local brands stay in stock; buyer power rises as availability falls | Sound |
| **PESTLE** | Environmental and Economic categories both activated — climate events causing infrastructure disruption, which raises logistics cost | Sound |
| **Business Model Canvas** | Impact traced to Channels, Customer segments, Customer relationships | Sound |
| **Christensen's disruption theory (1997)** | *"explains how smaller competitors can take advantage of industry shocks ... to gain market share"* | **Misapplied** |

> **Christensen's disruptive innovation is not a theory about supply shocks.**
> *The Innovator's Dilemma* describes entrants attacking from a **low-end** or
> **new-market** foothold with a different value network — typically a
> cheaper, simpler, initially inferior offer that incumbents rationally ignore
> — then moving upmarket over time. A local rival that stays in stock during a
> port closure is not a disruptive innovator; it is a conventional competitor
> exploiting a temporary capability gap. If you want a genuine Christensen
> application in this case, **Dollar Shave Club is it**: it entered at the low
> end with a subscription model the incumbent's retail-and-margin structure
> made awkward to copy, and moved up. Citing Christensen for the port event
> reads as framework name-dropping, which the rubric punishes precisely because
> it looks like the opposite of critical thinking.

Two smaller things in the same report worth not repeating:

- **"Gillette's immutable market share"**, used twice. Immutable means
  unchangeable — and the entire report argues the share is under threat. The
  word contradicts the thesis. Say *"entrenched"* or *"long-held"*.
- **"Porter's Differentiation Focus Strategy"** for a nationwide DTC channel.
  See Topic 3: focus means **narrow scope**. Nationwide is broad
  differentiation.

## 4.9 State your assumptions

Cal's report does this, and it is a genuinely good move worth copying:

> *"The assumptions made are that the disruption effects last more than three
> months, multiple ports are impacted and Gillette cannot fulfil orders via
> regular shipping routes, resorting to air freight as the new primary import
> method."*

Three assumptions, stated in one sentence, and each one is load-bearing. If the
disruption lasted three weeks, the correct answer would be inventory buffers,
not a channel restructure. Stating the duration assumption is what makes the
structural recommendation defensible instead of disproportionate.

> **A hypothetical disruption has no facts, so your assumptions are the
> facts.** Duration, breadth and severity change which response wins. Set them
> explicitly and early — one sentence — and every later argument becomes
> checkable against them.

<details><summary>3. Why is "local competitors gain share" more significant than "operating costs rise", when the cost impact is larger in dollars this quarter?</summary>

Because of **persistence**. Air-freight costs revert when ports reopen; the
cost impact is bounded by the disruption's duration.

Share lost under a razor-and-blades model does not revert. A customer who
switches handle systems during a stockout takes the future blade annuity with
them, and winning them back requires re-acquisition spend. A smaller impact
that locks in outranks a larger one that reverses — which is exactly what step
4 of the convergence procedure is asking you to weigh.

<details><summary>4. A student cites Christensen to argue that a local rival taking shelf space during a port closure is "disruptive innovation". What is wrong, and what would a correct use look like?</summary>

Christensen's theory describes entrants attacking from a **low-end or
new-market foothold** with a different value network and business model, which
incumbents find structurally awkward to answer, then moving upmarket. It is
about business-model asymmetry, not about capacity to supply.

A rival with a domestic supply chain filling a temporary gap is ordinary
competitive rivalry — better analysed with Porter's Five Forces (rivalry, or
threat of substitutes) or as a resilience argument.

A correct Christensen application in this case: **Dollar Shave Club**. It
entered at the low end with subscription pricing and DTC delivery, a model
Gillette's retail-margin structure made difficult to match without
cannibalising itself, and then moved upmarket.
</details>

Your **assumptions about the disruption** — above all its expected duration,
plus its breadth and severity.

A channel restructure is only proportionate to a long or recurring disruption.
For a three-week event the right answer is inventory buffering. Cal's report
assumes effects lasting more than three months across multiple ports with air
freight as the new primary import route, which is exactly what licenses a

</details>

## Summary

- Three steps: **situate** the disruption in the BEC, **analyse impacts** on
  the BMC, **respond** and revise the model.
- More than one BEC quadrant will be affected. Name all of them, and don't
  forget Key trends — it is what justifies a structural response.
- **Double Diamond**: problem articulation (divergent) → problem selection
  (convergent) → solution generation (divergent) → solution selection
  (convergent).
- Brainstorm impacts **through the nine BMC blocks**. Separate divergence from
  convergence in time.
- Five-step convergence; step 2 — remove what the current model can absorb —
  is the filter that matters.
- Rank impacts by **persistence**, not size.
- Generate at least three responses with stated limitations, then choose
  comparatively.
- Close by rewriting the affected blocks and showing the revised canvases.
- Use theory precisely. Christensen is about low-end and new-market footholds,
  not supply shocks. Focus strategies are narrow-scope.
- State your assumptions about duration, breadth and severity in one sentence,
  early.
