## 1.1 The four functions of management — PLOC

Adapted in the deck from Schermerhorn et al. (2020), *Management*, 7th
Asia-Pacific edition.

| Function | One-line job | Lecture 6's shorthand |
|---|---|---|
| **P**lanning | Decide objectives and how to reach them | set direction |
| **L**eading | Influence, motivate and direct people | inspire effort |
| **O**rganising | Divide tasks and deploy resources | create structures |
| **C**ontrolling | Regulate activity against the plan | ensure results |

> **The acronym and the diagram disagree, and neither is wrong.** Lecture 2's
> circular diagram runs Planning → Organizing → Leading → Controlling. Lecture
> 6 spells the acronym **PLOC** — Planning, Leading, Organising, Controlling.
> Same four functions, different display order. If a question asks you to
> "explain PLOC", use the acronym's order; if it asks about the management
> *process*, the plan-organise-lead-control sequence is the more natural
> narrative. Don't waste a sentence defending the ordering — no marks live
> there.

### Planning

A plan is a statement of intended means for accomplishing objectives. The
systematic planning process has five sequential action steps:

```
1. Define your objectives.
2. Determine where you stand in relation to objectives.
3. Develop premises regarding future conditions.
4. Analyse and choose among alternative actions.
5. Implement the plan and evaluate the results.
```

The deck adds a caveat on the same slide: in practice this is **more
iterative, with steps happening concurrently**. That caveat is free marks —
it shows you know the model is a teaching device, not a description of a
Tuesday.

### Organising

Organising creates an organisational structure, defined in three parts:

- the set of formal tasks assigned to individuals and departments
- formal reporting relationships — lines of authority, decision
  responsibility, number of hierarchical levels, and span of control
- the design of systems ensuring coordination of employees across
  departments

Tasks plus reporting relationships give the framework for **vertical
control**, and the **organisation chart** is the visual representation of
that vertical structure.

**Tall versus flat.** The deck asks "is one structure better than the
other?" and answers "it depends":

| Flat works better for | Tall works better for |
|---|---|
| Small or start-up businesses | Large or complex organisations |
| Creative or tech industries | Highly regulated industries |
| Workplaces wanting an informal culture (e.g. an NGO) | Organisations with specialised roles |

> **"It depends" only earns marks if you name the dependency.** The examinable
> move is not "it depends on context" — it is "flat suits a start-up because
> speed of decision matters more than control; tall suits a bank because
> regulatory accountability requires traceable lines of authority." Name the
> contingency and the mechanism. Two clauses, full marks.

### Leading

Leading is the process of influencing, motivating and directing people to
achieve organisational objectives. It has four core elements: **leadership,
communication, motivation, and organisational culture.** Leadership is
central — the ability to influence people towards organisational goals.

| Style | Mechanism |
|---|---|
| **Transformational** | Inspires and empowers employees towards higher performance and innovation |
| **Transactional** | Structured rewards and penalties maintain performance standards |
| **Servant** | Employee wellbeing and development first; a people-first culture |

### Controlling

Organisational control is the systematic process of regulating activities so
they stay consistent with plans, targets and performance standards. Three
control models, and the deck attaches a timing word to each:

| Control type | What it does | Timing |
|---|---|---|
| **Budgetary** | Sets expenditure targets, monitors results against budget, makes changes | **Proactive** |
| **Financial** | Monitors the balance sheet and income statement — income, risk, expenses, investments | **Ongoing** |
| **Feedback** | Monitors and regulates activity, using feedback to test whether performance met the standard | **Reactive** |

Which one you choose depends on the organisation's goals, the nature of
operations, and the decision-making time-frame.

> **The timing words are the exam question.** Budgetary/proactive,
> financial/ongoing, feedback/reactive is the one triple in Lecture 2 that
> maps cleanly to a matching or multiple-choice item, and the deck states it
> twice on consecutive slides — a strong signal. The common error is calling
> budgetary control "reactive" because budgets get reviewed after the fact.
> The budget is *set* before spending; that is what makes it proactive.

<summary>Checkpoint 1 — PLOC</summary>

**Q.** A logistics firm installs route-optimisation software. Assign one
concrete action to each of the four functions.

**A.** One defensible answer:

- **Planning** — define the objective (cut fuel cost 12% in 12 months),
  establish the current baseline, develop premises about fuel prices, choose
  between vendors, then implement and evaluate.
- **Leading** — communicate to drivers why routes are changing; address the
  motivation problem created by the software overriding their judgement.
- **Organising** — decide who owns the system (a new coordinator role?),
  redraw reporting lines, redesign the dispatcher job.
- **Controlling** — budgetary control on the implementation spend; feedback
  control comparing actual fuel use against the 12% target.

**Q.** Which control type would flag a cost overrun *before* it happened?

**A.** Budgetary — it is the proactive one, setting the limit in advance.

## 1.2 What strategy is

Strategy is a focused approach to achieving long-term goals by aligning
resources and actions with a clear vision. Lecture 2 unpacks that into five
claims:

- **Strategic decisions** involve long-term impact, top-management
  involvement, and resource allocation.
- **Planned versus emergent strategy** — structured plans versus adapting to
  unforeseen changes.
- **Levels of strategy** — corporate (overall direction), business
  (competitive positioning), operational (day-to-day execution).
- **Competitive advantage** comes from positioning, resources and
  capabilities.

**Strategic planning** is the structured process of defining direction and
resource allocation across those three levels, via goal setting,
environmental analysis, strategy formulation, implementation and evaluation.
The deck (adapted from Fleisher and Bensoussan, 2015) gives it six
characteristics:

| Characteristic |
|---|
| Medium to long-term |
| Top-management level |
| Significant inputs and resources needed |
| Impacts many or all of the firm's activities — hence major changes in the company |
| Impacts tactical and operational decisions |
| Impacts, and is impacted by, competitive dynamics |
| Infrequent process |

> **"Infrequent" is doing real work in that list.** It is the characteristic
> that separates strategic planning from operational planning, and it is the
> one students drop. If you are asked to distinguish the three levels of
> strategy, frequency is your cleanest discriminator: corporate strategy is
> revisited every few years, operational strategy every week.

## 1.3 The five-step strategic decision process

Lecture 2's own numbered process — and the spine of any strategy question in
this unit:

```
1. Conduct an internal AND external analysis.
2. Select a course of action that aligns with goals.
3. Competitively position the firm in the market.
4. Assess opportunities and threats before making decisions.
5. *NEW* Consider using AI in decision-making.
```

The deck marks step 5 "NEW" — it is the lecturer's addition to the textbook
process, and it is the step most likely to be probed in this unit
specifically.

The running example for the whole strategy block is the **electric vehicle
industry**.

## 1.4 External analysis — Porter's Five Forces

Porter (1979, 1980), presented via Robbins and Coulter (2020). The framework
asks: *what's changing in the market?* Each force is stated in the deck as
both a definition and a question — answer the question, don't recite the
definition.

| Force | Definition | The question to answer |
|---|---|---|
| **Threat of new entrants** | Risk of new competitors entering and reducing profitability | How likely is it that new competitors will come into the industry? |
| **Bargaining power of suppliers** | Supplier influence over pricing and availability of key inputs | How much bargaining power do suppliers have? |
| **Bargaining power of buyers** | Customers' ability to demand lower prices or higher quality | How much bargaining power do buyers have? |
| **Threat of substitutes** | Alternative products or services that replace existing offerings | How likely is it that other industries' products substitute for ours? |
| **Industry rivalry** | Intensity of competition among existing players | How intense is the rivalry among current competitors? |

**Applied to EVs, using only what the deck supports.** New entrants: low —
capital intensity and battery supply chains are barriers, but Chinese
manufacturers entering Western markets shows the barrier is falling.
Suppliers: high — battery cell and lithium suppliers hold pricing power over
every manufacturer. Buyers: rising — as models proliferate, switching costs
fall. Substitutes: hybrids, public transport, and continued ICE ownership.
Rivalry: intense, and the deck's own three-firm comparison (BYD, Tesla,
Rivian in §1.9) is evidence of it.

> **Five Forces measures industry attractiveness, not company strength.** The
> single most common misuse is writing "Tesla has strong brand power" under
> *bargaining power of buyers*. Brand is a firm-level resource — that belongs
> in VRIO. Five Forces answers "how profitable is it to be *anyone* in this
> industry?" If your sentence names one company, you are probably in the
> wrong framework.

## 1.5 External analysis — PESTEL

Six dimensions, from Johnson et al. (2013), *Exploring Strategy*.

| Dimension | Covers | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| **P**olitical | Government policies, taxation, trade regulations, political stability, lobbying groups | Determines how businesses operate and expand in different regions |
| **E**conomic | Growth, inflation, exchange rates, employment, consumer purchasing power | Affects market demand, pricing strategy, investment decisions |
| **S**ocial | Demographic trends, cultural norms, lifestyle changes, consumer behaviour shifts | Influences product demand, marketing, workforce expectations |
| **T**echnological | Innovation, automation, R&D, adoption of new technologies | Can restructure competition, enable new entrants, eliminate others |
| **E**cological | Sustainability, climate change, resource availability | Forces adaptation to ecological pressure and consumer sustainability expectations |
| **L**egal | Labour law, competition regulation, IP rights, industry-specific requirements | Non-compliance brings financial penalties and reputational damage |

> **This deck says Ecological, not Environmental.** Both letters E in PESTEL
> are live — Economic and Ecological — and the deck labels the second one
> **Ecological**. Plenty of textbooks say "Environmental". Use the deck's word
> in this unit, and never let "Environmental" quietly swallow the Economic
> dimension, which is the actual error this causes: students write five
> dimensions and lose one.

Lecture 6's Australian vehicle-emissions example is a PESTEL item you can
lift straight into an answer. The Federal Chamber of Automotive Industries —
the peak body for 39 auto brands — campaigned to have the industry's
existing *voluntary* emissions scheme adopted as the national standard.
Leaked documents showed that would leave new passenger cars sold in 2030
emitting an average of at least 98 g CO₂/km (Cubby, 2022).

```
Australian voluntary target, 2030    ≥ 98 g CO2/km
European standard, already in force    95 g CO2/km
Gap                                     3 g/km  =  3.2% higher
```

Britain, per the same article, plans to ban the sale of most new petrol and
hybrid cars from 2030.

> **Three grams is not the point — the direction is.** A 3.2% gap sounds
> trivial until you notice Australia's *2030* target is weaker than Europe's
> *current* standard, so the gap is really the better part of a decade. This
> is Political and Legal acting on the same firms at once, which is exactly
> what a good PESTEL answer shows: dimensions interact, they don't sit in
> separate boxes.

## 1.6 Internal analysis — VRIO

A tool to assess whether a resource, tangible or intangible, provides a
**sustainable** competitive advantage.

| Letter | The deck's question |
|---|---|
| **V**alue | Does it create value or help exploit opportunities? |
| **R**arity | Is it unique or widely available? |
| **I**mitability | Can competitors easily copy it? |
| **O**rganization | Is the company structured to leverage it? |

Only resources meeting all four provide a sustained advantage.

> **The "I" is a trick question and the slide is loosely worded.** Value,
> Rarity and Organization all want a **yes**. Imitability wants a **no** — the
> resource must be *hard* to copy, which is why the letter is often written
> as *Inimitability*. Reading "meet all four" as "answer yes four times"
> inverts the framework and will cost you the whole question. Write it out
> as: valuable ✓, rare ✓, costly to imitate ✓, organised to exploit ✓ — and
> notice the third one is a negative dressed as a positive.

Worked on the deck's EV example: Tesla's Supercharger network is valuable
(removes range anxiety), rare (no rival matched its density for years),
costly to imitate (land, grid connections and years of capital), and Tesla
is organised to exploit it (vehicles, software and network are one product).
Four out of four — a sustained advantage. Compare a lithium supply contract:
valuable and arguably rare, but any competitor with cash can sign one, so it
fails on imitability and yields only a temporary advantage.

## 1.7 Internal *and* external — SWOT

The deck frames SWOT as the alignment question: how can a company align
internal strengths and weaknesses with external opportunities and threats?

| | Prompts from the deck |
|---|---|
| **Strengths** | What resources can we deploy? What are our advantages? What's working well? |
| **Weaknesses** | What abilities are we lacking? Where are we starting to struggle? How can we overcome these? |
| **Opportunities** | Who might most value our strengths? What trends work in our favour? What prizes are within reach? |
| **Threats** | What headwinds do we face? Who might challenge us? What could go wrong? |

> **SWOT is a container, not an analysis.** Its two rows are literally the
> outputs of the other frameworks — Strengths and Weaknesses come from VRIO,
> Opportunities and Threats come from PESTEL and Five Forces. A SWOT that
> isn't fed by those is just a list of opinions. That is also why the deck's
> summary table (§1.10) lists "SWOT (Updated)" at step 4: you fill it in
> once, then revise it after the external analysis moves.

## 1.8 Dynamic capabilities

How does the company **adapt** its resources to external changes? The
ability to adapt, build and reconfigure resources to maintain a competitive
edge. Three core capabilities:

| Capability | What it is |
|---|---|
| **Sensing** | Identifying opportunities and threats |
| **Seizing** | Acting on opportunities through resource allocation |
| **Reconfiguring** | Continually adapting to market changes |

The deck's line: the best EV companies continuously bridge internal
resources with external changes, so they stay competitive in a fast-moving
industry.

> **VRIO is a photograph; dynamic capabilities is the film.** VRIO tells you
> whether what you have today is defensible. Dynamic capabilities tells you
> whether you can still be defensible in five years. Any question about a
> firm facing rapid technological change wants both — and the "sensing"
> failure is exactly what Lecture 6 accuses the record labels of (§1.15).

## 1.9 Three paths to competitive advantage

| Path | Mechanism | The deck's EV example |
|---|---|---|
| **Operational excellence** (cost leadership) | Low-cost provider via operational systems that continually reduce cost and price, while offering quality that adds more value than competitors | **BYD** — affordable, mass market |
| **Product leadership** (differentiation) | Innovation in product and/or services creates the advantage | **Tesla** — premium technology, high performance, innovation |
| **Customer intimacy** (differentiation) | Unique solutions customised at the lowest level of customer interface | **Rivian** — niche focus, personalised experiences, direct engagement |

**Stuck in the middle.** A firm that pursues both cost leadership and
differentiation without committing to either suffers three named problems:

1. **No clear competitive advantage** — fails to attract either
   cost-conscious or premium-seeking customers.
2. **Inefficient resource allocation** — resources spread too thin, so
   operating costs rise without achieving brand differentiation or price
   competitiveness.
3. **Brand and market position confusion** — mixed signals weaken brand
   identity, making loyalty and pricing power harder to establish.

> **Two frameworks are wearing one costume here, and it is worth ten seconds
> of care.** "Operational excellence / product leadership / customer intimacy"
> are Treacy and Wiersema's **value disciplines**. "Stuck in the middle" is
> Porter's phrase, and Porter's own generic strategies are cost leadership,
> differentiation and focus. The deck's summary table lists *"Porter's Generic
> Strategies, Product Leadership, Customer Intimacy"* side by side in one
> cell, which reads as though they are one framework. They are two. The
> mapping the deck implies (operational excellence ≈ cost leadership, the
> other two ≈ differentiation) is defensible, but **ask your tutor which
> naming the assessment expects** before you commit an answer to one label
> set. If in doubt, name both: "operational excellence, or in Porter's terms
> cost leadership."

## 1.10 How it all fits together

Lecture 2's own summary table — memorise this shape, not just the individual
tools:

| Step | Key strategic models | Purpose |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Conduct an internal and external analysis | SWOT, VRIO, PESTEL, Porter's Five Forces | Understand company strengths and weaknesses, industry competition, and external factors |
| 2. Select a course of action that aligns with goals | VRIO, Dynamic Capabilities, Feedback Control | Choose a strategy matching resources and long-term goals |
| 3. Competitively position the firm in the market | Porter's Generic Strategies, Product Leadership, Customer Intimacy | Define cost, differentiation, or customer-focused strategies |
| 4. Assess opportunities and threats before deciding | SWOT (updated), PESTEL (updated), Porter's Five Forces, Dynamic Capabilities | Evaluate risks, refine strategy, ensure flexibility before execution |

Note **Feedback Control** appearing at step 2 — that is PLOC's controlling
function reappearing inside the strategy process. The deck is telling you
the two halves of Lecture 2 are one system.

## 1.11 Execution and performance measurement

Five key elements of execution:

- **Aligning resources** — financial, human and technological resources
  support the strategic objectives
- **Clear communication** — everyone understands their role in executing
- **Leadership and accountability** — clear responsibility keeps execution
  on track
- **Organisational agility** — flexibility to adapt to market changes and
  feedback
- **Monitoring and adjustments** — ongoing tracking to refine tactics

**How do we know the strategy worked?** Key Performance Indicators. The deck
stresses that important KPIs vary by company, industry and level of
analysis, then gives five typical categories:

| Category | Example measures |
|---|---|
| Financial performance | Revenue, revenue growth, profit margins, ROI |
| Market performance | Market share, brand positioning |
| Operational efficiency | Production output, supply chain efficiency |
| Customer satisfaction | Loyalty, repeat business |
| Innovation | Speed of new product launches |

> **"KPIs vary by company, industry and level of analysis" is the marked
> sentence, not the list.** A question asking you to propose KPIs for a
> specific organisation is testing whether you *select*, not whether you can
> recite five categories. Proposing "revenue growth" for a public library is
> the failure mode. Pick two or three, and justify each against that
> organisation's actual objective.

## 1.12 Strategy and AI

The deck's five uses:

- **AI for analysis** — enhances PESTEL, Five Forces and SWOT assessments
- **AI for decision-making** — predictive analytics, risk assessment,
  optimisation
- **AI for execution** — automates processes, improves efficiency,
  personalises experiences
- **AI for competitive advantage** — supports cost leadership (efficiency),
  differentiation (innovation), and focus (customer insights)
- **Future potential** — AI-human collaboration may redefine strategic
  decision-making

**Two case examples.** Netflix uses AI for content creation,
personalisation, user experience, forecasting and analysing feedback.
**NatWest** (National Westminster Bank) runs five named applications:

| Application | What it does |
|---|---|
| AI-powered customer service | *Cora*, the chatbot, gives 24/7 instant banking support |
| Fraud detection and cybersecurity | Real-time transaction monitoring |
| AI-driven credit scoring and loan approvals | Alternative data for faster lending decisions |
| Compliance and risk management | Automated monitoring for financial misconduct |
| Personalised banking and financial advice | Spending-habit analysis drives budgeting and savings advice |

> **Notice which competitive path each application serves.** Cora and
> compliance automation are *operational excellence* — they cut cost per
> interaction. Alternative-data credit scoring is *product leadership* — it
> does something rivals cannot. Personalised advice is *customer intimacy*.
> An answer that maps AI applications back onto the three paths is doing the
> synthesis the unit is actually asking for; an answer that lists five AI
> uses is not.

**Lecture 2's stated key takeaways**, verbatim in substance:

1. Strategic success requires alignment between internal resources and
   external market conditions.
2. Choosing the right competitive strategy is crucial to avoid being "stuck
   in the middle".
3. AI is transforming strategy execution and decision-making.

## 1.13 Digital transformation

Lecture 6. The definition the deck quotes:

> "…a process that aims to improve an entity by triggering significant
> changes to its properties through combinations of information, computing,
> communication, and connectivity technologies" (Vial, 2021, p. 118).

Three clusters sit under that definition on the same slide:

| What drives it | What it delivers | What it demands organisationally |
|---|---|---|
| Digitisation | Deliver new value | Management versus IT |
| Digitalisation | Touches all aspects of the organisation *or* the customer | Project versus program |
| New affordances | | Strategy |
| | | Specialist teams |

> **The deck names digitisation, digitalisation and affordances but never
> defines them.** They are listed as bullet points with no explanation on the
> slide, and no reading in the folder fills the gap. The distinction is
> standard — digitisation converts analogue to digital, digitalisation
> changes the *process* using digital data, transformation changes the
> *organisation* — but that is not sourced from this unit's materials, so
> don't cite it as though it were. Get the lecturer's own definitions from
> the recording before you use them in A3.

> **Vial is cited as both 2019 and 2021 in the same deck.** The definition
> slide says "(Vial, 2021, p. 118)"; every slide showing the framework
> diagram says "(Vial, 2019) — paper available on Canvas". These are the same
> author and the same work; the deck is inconsistent about the year. Use
> whichever year matches the PDF actually posted on Canvas, and use it
> consistently — a reference list with both entries for one source is a
> visible error.

The Vial framework diagram is reproduced four separate times across Lecture
6, each time with a different portion highlighted: the disruption inputs,
the barriers, the outcomes. The deck flags that the **outcomes** section is
the focus of Lectures 7 and 8 — which are not in the folder.

**Lecture 6's headline claim: digital transformation has no end point.** It
is a project or program of work that is ongoing in any type of
organisation.

## 1.14 Disruption — two definitions that do not match

The deck puts two definitions of disruptive innovation on screen, from
different sources, and then explicitly says they cover different cases.

| Source | Definition |
|---|---|
| Christensen Institute (2022) | Innovations that make products and services more **accessible and affordable**, thereby making them available to a larger population |
| Samson et al. (2020, p. 346) | Innovation in products, services or processes that **radically changes an industry's rules of the game** for producers and consumers |

The slide comparing Uber and AirBnB to incumbents is headed *"a little
different from Clayton's OG theory of disruptive innovation"*, and Lecture 6
has a slide titled "Disruption: OG vs. new meaning".

> **This is the single most likely short-answer question in Lecture 6, and it
> has a right answer.** Christensen's original theory is about *low-end* entry
> — cheaper, worse products that improve until they take the mainstream.
> Uber and AirBnB did not enter at the low end of hotels and taxis; they
> rewrote the rules. The deck's position is that both definitions are in
> circulation and the newer, looser one covers cases the original does not.
> The mark is for **naming the tension**, not for picking a winner. An answer
> that quotes only one definition has answered half the question.

**Incumbent versus new entrant**, per the deck:

| Incumbent (e.g. hotels, taxis, limos) | New entrant (e.g. AirBnB, Uber/Lyft) |
|---|---|
| Exploitative innovation | Explorative innovation |
| Improvements at the fringes | Entirely new ways of generating revenue |
| Cutting costs through efficiencies | Low fixed costs, low barriers to entry |
| **But high fixed-cost structures** | Variable cost structure |
| | Speaks to new "jobs to be done", which triggers new competitors |

## 1.15 The Napster case — a lived disruption

The deck's extended worked example, and the one with numbers you can check.

**Setting the scene.** Music consumption in the late 1990s ran through CDs,
radio, MTV and *rage*. Four trends converged:

- increasing desire for personalisation, e.g. ripping CDs (consumer trend)
- emerging digital libraries of music (business trend)
- increasing internet connectivity (tech trend)
- MP3 technology advancements (tech trend)

**The new entrant.** Napster launched in 1999, created by Shawn Fanning, who
described the motivation as connecting with people online over shared music
taste — sharing opinions, recommendations and building social connections.
The deck adds a pointed footnote that "disruptor" is over-used and
incorrectly used, and that the lecturer is leaning into it deliberately.

**The incumbent's choice.** The deck states it as a binary:

```
1. Respond with new business models and novel products and services
2. Self-preservation through protectionist lobbying and/or litigation
```

The industry chose (2). Carrier (2012) calls the result "a wasteland of
innovation".

**The numbers.** RIAA members spent eight years pursuing compensation for
revenue lost to Napster. By 2007, when Bertelsmann paid the final
settlement, US album sales had fallen to a 20-year low. Globally:

```
Global recorded music revenue, 1999   $37 billion USD
Global recorded music revenue, 2007   $25 billion USD
                                      ───────────────
Fall                                  $12 billion  =  −32.4%
```

*(Hiatt and Serpick, 2007, in Rolling Stone.)* A later slide cites Ingham
(2020) reporting that global recorded music revenues topped **$20 billion**
in 2019 — a floor figure, so the industry did not recover to 1999 levels
within twenty years.

> **The word "attributes" is carrying the argument, and you should say so.**
> The source says the decline is one "that many attribute primarily to file
> sharing" — an attribution, not a finding. The deck then cites Liebowitz
> (2006), whose title is literally *"File sharing: creative destruction or
> just plain destruction?"*, i.e. the causal question was live in the
> literature. Quote the 32.4% fall by all means, but write "attributed
> largely to file sharing" rather than "caused by". Causal overclaim is the
> cheapest mark to lose in a case analysis.

**The counterfactual the deck endorses.** Steve Knopper's argument, quoted at
length in the slides, is that the labels dealt with file-sharing almost
exclusively through lawsuits and copyright protection; that it took ten to
fifteen years to work out how to profit from it; and that they could have
avoided much of the damage by licensing to Napster or building a
competitive, cost-effective service instead of stonewalling. Jay-Z's line on
the same slide: the industry rejected what was happening instead of
accepting it as change.

**Why Napster still matters.** In 2020, UK immersive-music startup MelodyVR
bought Napster for **$70 million** in a reverse takeover (Lunden, 2020).
Napster's CEO from 2022 — previously VP and global head of music at Roblox,
where he ran virtual concerts with Lil Nas X, David Guetta, Twenty One
Pilots and Zara Larsson — plans virtual artist spaces with digital goods
(Olson, 2024).

**The new Napster?** The deck asks whether NFTs are (del Castillo, 2021),
and quotes Warner Music's chief digital officer on the industry's structural
weakness: labels compete heavily against each other, tech arrives and takes
the value, and the labels still won't talk to each other.

> **The exam-friendly reading of Napster is not "piracy bad".** It is that the
> incumbents had a *sensing* capability failure and a *reconfiguring* failure
> — the two dynamic capabilities from §1.8. They saw the signal (four
> converging trends, in the deck's own framing) and responded with litigation
> instead of resource reallocation. If you can tie Lecture 6's case back to
> Lecture 2's framework, you are writing a distinction answer.

## 1.16 Kimai — disruption from the other side

The deck's second case: **Kimai**, founded by Jessica and Sidney, daughters
of diamond jewellers in Antwerp and Brussels. They grow diamonds chemically
and physically identical to mined diamonds, without the social and
environmental impacts, and use recycled gold — cutting out mines and
middlemen and controlling the process from design to delivery.

Three trends the deck names as making this possible:

- greater call for **trust and transparency in supply chains** — ways to
  track authenticity and provenance of materials
- **social considerations** of labour, slavery, and harm or risk in
  traditional mining
- **environmental impact** of mining diamonds

The incumbent outcome on the following slide: losing market share to
lab-grown diamonds.

> **Kimai is the Christensen definition; Uber is the Samson definition.**
> Lab-grown diamonds make a product more accessible and affordable to a
> larger population — that is textbook Christensen. Uber changed the rules of
> the game without being cheaper-and-worse — that is Samson. Using the right
> case for the right definition is the fastest way to show you understood
> §1.14 rather than memorised it.

## 1.17 Responses to disruption — four actors

The deck works through what each party should do.

**Consumers (the deck says: you).** Increasing consumer power — vote with
your wallet and your actions. **Social licence to operate** — signal what is
acceptable. Give power to new entrants — grant legitimacy to innovation
(Labrecque et al., 2013).

**New entrants should:**

- harness support from consumers early
- avoid exploiting regulatory loopholes, which damages social licence to
  operate
- be prepared for conflict
- test the strategy against future scenarios — increased regulation,
  copycats entering, changing customer preferences

**Incumbents should consider:**

| Prompt | The question |
|---|---|
| (Un)predictable futures | How was this signal missed? Were you watching for trends? |
| Value | What is the "job to be done" drawing consumers to the new entrant? |
| Scenario readiness | How do your current strategy and business models meet that job? |
| Culture of acceptance | Question the internal resistance to change |
| Action | **Invest in innovation, not litigation** |

**Policymakers** get the **Policy Oversight Lifecycle** (Grady, 2023) and
the vehicle-emissions case from §1.5, plus Dootson and Ross (2019).

> **"Social licence to operate" appears in two lectures and returns in
> Lecture 12.** It is the deck's link between consumer behaviour and firm
> strategy, and Lecture 1 flags it as a Week 12 topic alongside business
> decision-making. It is a strong candidate for a synthesis question, and it
> is the concept that makes the consumer an *actor* in disruption rather than
> an audience.

## 1.18 Barriers to, and facilitators of, digital transformation

The deck's named barrier is **resistance**, illustrated twice.

**Case: plant-based meat labelling.** A Senate inquiry into how plant-based
products are labelled heard from the competition regulator that it had no
evidence of deceptive conduct, and that the complaints received showed
people knew what they were buying. The meat and dairy industry wants the
terms protected, arguing plant-based products undermine the industry
(Evans, 2021).

**The weak signal model.** The deck's diagram: a **weak signal** becomes an
**emerging trend**, which resolves over time into either an **emerging
opportunity** or an **emerging threat** — the same signal, two destinations,
depending on the organisation's position and response.

Lecture 6's stated key takeaways:

1. Digital transformation is a project or program of work that is ongoing in
   any type of organisation — there is **no end point**.
2. Organisations can choose to evolve in the face of disruption, or try to
   litigate and regulate their way out of it.
3. There are many barriers to digital transformation, and overcoming them
   has a lot to do with **staff capability and culture**.

> **Takeaway 3 is the bridge to Topic 2 and to A3.** "Staff capability and
> culture" is why the unit spends a whole week on the JISC framework and why
> A3 asks for an *evidence-informed* case rather than a technology
> recommendation. A business case that names a technology but not the
> capability gap it creates has skipped the barrier the lecturer explicitly
> identified.

## Summary

- **PLOC** — Planning (set direction), Leading (inspire effort), Organising
  (create structures), Controlling (ensure results). Planning has five
  sequential steps and the deck says real practice is iterative. Control is
  budgetary/**proactive**, financial/**ongoing**, feedback/**reactive**.
- **Strategy** is long-term, top-management, resource-allocating, and runs
  at corporate, business and operational levels. Strategic planning is
  **infrequent** — that is what makes it strategic.
- **The five-step process**: internal and external analysis → select a course
  of action → competitively position → reassess opportunities and threats →
  consider AI.
- **The toolkit** — Five Forces and PESTEL look outward, VRIO looks inward,
  SWOT is the container that holds both, dynamic capabilities (sensing,
  seizing, reconfiguring) explains how the picture changes over time.
- **Three competitive paths** — operational excellence (BYD), product
  leadership (Tesla), customer intimacy (Rivian). Committing to none leaves
  you **stuck in the middle**. Check the labelling convention with your tutor.
- **Execution** needs aligned resources, clear communication, accountability,
  agility and monitoring; success is measured by KPIs chosen for the specific
  organisation.
- **Digital transformation** (Vial) improves an entity by triggering
  significant changes through combinations of information, computing,
  communication and connectivity technologies. It has **no end point**.
- **Disruption** has two competing definitions in this unit and you need
  both. Napster is the worked case: the incumbents chose litigation over
  innovation, and global recorded music revenue fell 32.4% between 1999 and
  2007.
- **Responses** differ by actor — consumers signal social licence, new
  entrants build consumer support and prepare for conflict, incumbents
  invest in innovation not litigation, policymakers manage the oversight
  lifecycle.
- **The main barrier is resistance**, and overcoming it comes down to staff
  capability and culture.
