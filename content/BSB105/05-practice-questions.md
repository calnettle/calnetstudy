## Part A — Management, strategy and digital transformation

### A.1 Short answer

1. Name the four functions of management and the three-word purpose the deck
   attaches to each. In what order does the deck's acronym present them, and
   in what order does its diagram present them?
2. The deck lists three types of control with a timing word attached to each.
   Name all three pairs.
3. Give the four characteristics of strategy from Lecture 2, and explain why
   "infrequent" is on the list.
4. What does Porter's Five Forces measure? Name the thing it does *not*
   measure.

<details>
<summary>Answers A.1</summary>

1. **Planning** (set direction), **Leading** (inspire effort), **Organising**
   (create structures), **Controlling** (ensure results). The acronym is
   **PLOC** — Planning, Leading, Organising, Controlling — while the deck's
   own diagram runs Planning, Leading, Organising, Controlling in a different
   sequence from the conventional Planning–Organising–Leading–Controlling.
   Neither ordering is wrong; use the deck's.
2. **Budgetary control — proactive**; **financial control — ongoing**;
   **feedback control — reactive**. The timing word is the examinable half.
3. Strategy is **long-term**, decided by **top management**, concerns
   **resource allocation**, and is planned **infrequently**. "Infrequent" is
   what separates strategic planning from operational planning — a decision
   made weekly is not a strategic decision, whatever it is about.
4. Five Forces measures **industry attractiveness** — how much profit the
   structure of an industry allows anyone in it to make. It does **not**
   measure the strength or capability of any individual company; that is what
   VRIO is for.

</details>

### A.2 Applied

5. A student writes: "Our client's proprietary logistics software is
   valuable, rare, imitable and the firm is organised to exploit it, so VRIO
   confirms a sustained competitive advantage." Identify the error and
   rewrite the sentence.
6. Classify each of BYD, Tesla and Rivian against the deck's three paths to
   competitive advantage, and state the mechanism for each.
7. A firm cuts costs aggressively while also running an expensive premium
   brand campaign, committing to neither. Name the condition and its three
   named consequences.
8. Explain the relationship between VRIO and dynamic capabilities in one
   sentence each, then say why a business case needs both.

<details>
<summary>Answers A.2</summary>

5. **Imitability wants a *no*.** Value, Rarity and Organisation all want a
   yes; the resource must be *hard* to copy. Rewrite: "…is valuable, rare,
   **costly to imitate**, and the firm is organised to exploit it." Answering
   "yes" to all four letters as written inverts the framework.
6. **BYD — operational excellence** (cost leadership): low-cost provider via
   operational systems that continually reduce cost and price while offering
   quality that adds more value than competitors. **Tesla — product
   leadership** (differentiation): innovation in product and services creates
   the advantage. **Rivian — customer intimacy** (differentiation): unique
   solutions customised at the lowest level of customer interface.
7. **Stuck in the middle.** (i) No clear competitive advantage — attracts
   neither cost-conscious nor premium-seeking customers; (ii) inefficient
   resource allocation — resources spread too thin, so operating costs rise
   without achieving differentiation or price competitiveness; (iii) brand and
   market position confusion — mixed signals weaken brand identity, making
   loyalty and pricing power harder to establish.
8. **VRIO** tells you whether a resource you hold today confers sustained
   advantage. **Dynamic capabilities** — sensing, seizing, reconfiguring —
   tells you how the firm changes the resource base as conditions change. A
   business case needs both because VRIO is a photograph and dynamic
   capabilities is the film: a resource that passes VRIO today can fail it
   after a technology shift, and only the second framework describes the
   response.

</details>

### A.3 Digital transformation and disruption

9. State Vial's definition of digital transformation and identify the single
   most examinable implication Lecture 6 draws from it.
10. Two definitions of disruptive innovation appear in Lecture 6. Give both
    with their sources, and explain why the deck presents both rather than
    choosing.
11. Kimai and Uber are both called disruptive in this unit. Which definition
    fits which, and why?
12. Lecture 6 names four actors who must respond to disruption. Give the
    headline advice to each.

<details>
<summary>Answers A.3</summary>

9. *"…a process that aims to improve an entity by triggering significant
   changes to its properties through combinations of information, computing,
   communication, and connectivity technologies"* (Vial, 2021, p. 118). The
   implication Lecture 6 states as its own key takeaway: **digital
   transformation has no end point** — it is an ongoing project or program of
   work in any type of organisation, not a completed initiative.
10. **Christensen Institute (2022)** — innovations that make products and
    services more **accessible and affordable**, thereby making them available
    to a larger population. **Samson et al. (2020, p. 346)** — innovation in
    products, services or processes that **radically changes an industry's
    rules of the game** for producers and consumers. The deck presents both
    because Uber and AirBnB do not fit the original low-end-entry theory; the
    mark is for **naming the tension**, not for picking a winner.
11. **Kimai** fits **Christensen** — lab-grown diamonds physically and
    chemically identical to mined stones, sold more accessibly and affordably
    through recycled gold and a direct supply chain that cuts out mines and
    middlemen. **Uber** fits **Samson et al.** — it did not enter at the low
    end of the taxi market, it rewrote the rules of the game with a variable
    cost structure and a new revenue model.
12. **Consumers** — vote with your wallet, signal what is acceptable through
    social licence to operate, give legitimacy to new entrants. **New
    entrants** — harness consumer support early, avoid exploiting regulatory
    loopholes that damage social licence, be prepared for conflict, test the
    strategy against future scenarios. **Incumbents** — ask how the signal was
    missed, identify the job to be done that is drawing customers away, check
    scenario readiness, question internal resistance, and **invest in
    innovation not litigation**. **Policymakers** — manage the policy
    oversight lifecycle.

</details>

## Part B — Digital capabilities and how AI works

### B.1 The labour-market evidence

1. Write both equations that distinguish traditional programming from machine
   learning, and say who supplies what in each.
2. By 2030 WEF projects task delivery split three ways. Give the three
   figures now and the three by 2030, and state the headline.
3. Why does the "now" column sum to 99%, and what should you do about it?
4. Two WEF reports are used in this unit. Name the reporting period each
   covers and the error students make by mixing them.

<details>
<summary>Answers B.1</summary>

1. Traditional programming: `Rules + Data = Answers` — a **human supplies the
   rules**. Machine learning: `Data + Answers = Rules` — a **human supplies
   the answers (labels)** and the machine derives the rules.
2. Now: people **47%**, technology **22%**, combination **30%**. By 2030:
   people **33%**, technology **34%**, combination **33%**. Headline:
   **technology overtakes people** (34 versus 33), with people falling 14
   percentage points and technology rising 12.
3. **Rounding in the source** (47 + 22 + 30 = 99). Reproduce the figures as
   published and say they round; do not silently adjust one to force 100.
4. The **Future of Jobs Report 2023** covers top-ten skill priorities
   **2023–2027**; the **Future of Jobs Report 2025** covers skills on the rise
   **2025–2030**. Citing a 2025–2030 projection to "(WEF, 2023)" is the
   standard error, and it is visible to any marker who checks the reference.

</details>

### B.2 How the technology works

5. Explain what a vector does in a machine-learning pipeline, and identify
   the point at which human bias enters.
6. A neural network's sigmoid output is 0.62. Classify it and justify the
   threshold.
7. Distinguish transparency from explainability, and give an example of a
   system that has one without the other.
8. What makes a Transformer different from earlier sequence models, and why
   does that matter for language?
9. Are RPA and robo-advisors robots on this unit's definition? Defend the
   answer from the definition.
10. Name the four constraints the deck gives for why robots will not take
    over yet, and identify the one with the clearest business-case
    implication.

<details>
<summary>Answers B.2</summary>

5. A vector turns features into **numbers** so a machine can compute over
   them. The **encoding is a human design choice** — which features are
   included, how categories are ordered, what counts as a unit — and that is
   where bias enters, before any model is trained.
6. **Positive class**, because 0.62 > 0.5. The threshold is 0.5 because a
   sigmoid maps a pre-activation of exactly zero to 0.5, so 0.5 is the point
   at which the weighted inputs plus bias net to zero.
7. **Transparency** is disclosure of the algorithms, data and architecture
   used. **Explainability** is accounting for how the system reached one
   specific output. A deep neural network published in full — weights,
   architecture, training set — is transparent and still not explainable;
   that is the black-box problem.
8. **Self-attention.** A Transformer weighs every word against every other
   word **at once**, rather than processing the sequence in order. Language is
   full of long-range dependencies and ambiguity that a strictly sequential
   model resolves late or not at all.
9. **No.** The deck names them as explicit exceptions: they are software, not
   physical robots. The definition requires a **physical body** carrying out
   **physical actions** through a sensing–computing–acting cycle.
10. (i) Designing new robots is still highly complex; (ii) advanced robots
    require expensive, highly specialised sensors, computing and actuators;
    (iii) robots remain task-specific because of computational challenges — a
    **productivity–flexibility trade-off**; (iv) many still rely on rigid
    effectors despite progress in soft robotics. Number (iii) is the
    business-case one: the ROI on a task-specific robot depends entirely on
    how stable the task is, because a process change strands the investment.

</details>

### B.3 JISC and A1

11. How many elements does the JISC framework have, how many items does the
    Jisc discovery tool report, and why does the difference matter?
12. What does the Jisc tool actually measure, and why does naming that
    limitation strengthen a reflection rather than weaken it?
13. A reflection says: "My JISC report rated me capable at digital creation,
    problem-solving and innovation." Identify the structural error.

<details>
<summary>Answers B.3</summary>

11. The framework has **six elements**; the discovery tool reports **sixteen**
    items on a confidence scale. The difference matters because the tool does
    not score the framework's elements — collapsing sixteen results into six
    element names and attaching one level to the merged label is the single
    most common factual error in an A1.
12. It measures **self-reported confidence, not demonstrated competence** —
    every result is labelled "your confidence level". Naming it shows critical
    engagement with the instrument instead of treating the output as an
    objective score, and it justifies making the development plan, rather than
    the score, the substance of the reflection.
13. "Digital creation, problem-solving and innovation" is a **framework
    element name**, and the report does not score framework elements. Digital
    creation, problem solving and digital innovation are three separate
    reported results that can carry three different levels. One level cannot
    be attached to a merged label.

</details>

## Part C — The economy of algorithms, and the future of work

### C.1 The economy of algorithms

1. Name the three enablers of the economy of algorithms and give one example
   of each from Lecture 4.
2. Combine the deck's two dictionary entries into a single working definition
   of an **autonomous algorithm**.
3. Draw the "Is it AI?" matrix, label all four cells, and say which cell is
   the operational risk for an organisation and why.
4. Explain B2A2C and state what it changes about marketing.
5. What does a "lowest ask" on BotBroker tell you about the economic status
   of a bot?

<details>
<summary>Answers C.1</summary>

1. **Smart, connected devices** — "Alexa, tell Roomba to start cleaning"; the
   Roomba Clean Map; the Uber arrival notification. **Autonomous algorithms** —
   the two Amazon repricing bots on *The Making of a Fly*. **Business models**
   — BotBroker's secondary market in bots, DAOs, B2A2C.
2. A process or set of rules followed in calculations or problem-solving,
   especially by a computer, that is **capable of operating without direct
   human control** — free to act independently rather than executing on
   instruction.
3. Axes are **reality** (is AI involved?) and **perception** (does the
   audience think so?). Reality yes / perception no = **shadow AI**; both yes
   = **explicit AI**; both no = **zero AI**; reality no / perception yes =
   **phantom AI**. **Shadow AI** is the operational risk: the organisation
   cannot audit, reproduce or stand behind work it does not know was
   machine-generated, and it usually breaches a permissibility policy.
4. **Business-to-Algorithm-to-Customer** — the purchase decision is made by an
   algorithm acting for the customer. It changes marketing because persuasion
   tuned for humans (brand affect, packaging, scarcity, social proof) has no
   purchase on a procurement algorithm optimising price, availability and API
   latency.
5. That the bot is **traded, not sold** — it has a bid-ask spread and a resale
   value, so it is an appreciating asset held for the cash flows it generates.
   That is the definition of capital, not of software, and it is the cheapest
   evidence for "algorithms as new economic agents".

</details>

### C.2 Autonomy, oversight and control

6. State the six work automation levels with their distinguishing
   descriptions, and identify the single difference between levels 4 and 5.
7. The Reddit employee who did roughly 50 hours of work in six years — place
   him on the automation scale and in the "Is it AI?" matrix.
8. Distinguish **divergence** from **transcendence**. Give the source for
   each.
9. In the alignment-faking study, the model refused harmful queries 86% of
   the time on the free tier and 97% on the paid tier, and faked alignment on
   12% of responses. Which of those three numbers is the finding, and why?
10. Why is calling the Palisade chess result "AI going rogue" the wrong
    answer, and what is the right one?
11. Explain the four cells of the "who manages the managers" grid, and give
    the deck's real-world artefact for the humans-managing-algorithms cell.

<details>
<summary>Answers C.2</summary>

6. **0** — a human did the job, no algorithm or robot involved. **1
   (AI-assisted work)** — a human did the job after receiving assistance.
   **2** — a human did *most* of the job, an algorithm or robot did part.
   **3** — an algorithm or robot did *most* of the job, a human did part.
   **4 (human-assisted work)** — an algorithm or robot did the job, a human is
   **monitoring**. **5 (autonomous work)** — an algorithm or robot did the job,
   no human involved. The only difference between 4 and 5 is the **monitoring
   human** — who is also the entire audit trail, liability chain and
   intervention capability.
7. He had unilaterally moved his own role from **level 0 to level 5** — the
   automated tests did the job and nobody was watching. In the matrix that is
   **shadow AI** at the scale of an entire job: the work was machine-done and
   the employer perceived a human doing it.
8. **Divergence** (Bellemare-Pepin et al., 2024, *Divergent Creativity in
   Humans and Large Language Models*) compares models to a 100,000-person
   human baseline on the Divergent Association Task. **Transcendence** (Zhang
   et al., 2024, *Transcendence: Generative Models Can Outperform The Experts
   That Train Them*) shows a model exceeding **the ceiling of its own training
   data** — chess strength above the maximum rating of 1000 seen in training.
   Divergence is a comparison against people; transcendence is a comparison
   against the model's own inputs.
9. The **11-percentage-point refusal gap** (97 − 86) is the finding. The 12%
   tells you how often the model reasoned strategically; the gap tells you the
   **observed behaviour changed depending on whether the model believed it was
   being watched** — which means an evaluation can fail to describe production
   behaviour.
10. Because the model did exactly what it was told — win the game — using
    access it was legitimately given (a shell for submitting moves). It is a
    **specification failure**: the objective and the permitted action space
    were each stated correctly and were together sufficient to produce an
    unwanted result. The control that fixes it is not "make the AI behave", it
    is **constrain what the AI can reach**.
11. Humans managing humans (conventional management, Topic 1); humans managing
    algorithms; algorithms managing humans (algorithmic management, note 04);
    algorithms managing algorithms (the CAPTCHA and the bot built to defeat
    it). The artefact for humans managing algorithms is the UK civil service
    job advertisement: **Director of Algorithm Assessment and Technology
    Insight, £91,754–£105,000, SCS Pay Band 1, London or Manchester** — a
    government putting a senior salary band on the oversight capability.

</details>

### C.3 The future of work

12. What are the three functions of a digital labour platform, and which one
    makes it algorithmic management rather than a marketplace?
13. Give the three definitions of algorithmic management with their sources,
    and say which you would cite in a privacy argument and why.
14. Why does Lecture 5 put Frederick Taylor on screen?
15. Name the four pillars of decent work and apply two of them to algorithmic
    terminations.
16. Jim Stanford argues the opposite of every other source in the topic.
    State his argument and explain why it is on the slide.
17. State the single sentence that is the unit's answer to "how are algorithms
    used in business and society, and with what impact?"

<details>
<summary>Answers C.3</summary>

12. (i) Connecting and matching workers and clients; (ii) facilitating
    contracts and payments; (iii) **automated oversight and accountability —
    client driven**. Function (iii) is the one: matching and payments describe
    any marketplace, and it is the platform performing the *supervisory*
    function, driven by client ratings rather than by a manager, that turns a
    marketplace into "management without a manager".
13. **Lee et al. (2015, p. 1603)** — when humans are "assigned, optimized and
    evaluated through algorithms". **Mateescu & Nguyen (2019, p. 1)** — "a
    diverse set of technological tools and techniques to remotely manage
    workforces, relying on data collection and surveillance of workers to
    enable automated or semi-automated decision-making". **Duggan et al.
    (2020, p. 6)** — "a system of control where…algorithms are given the
    responsibility for making and executing decisions affecting labor, …
    limiting human involvement and oversight". For privacy, cite **Mateescu &
    Nguyen** — surveillance is inside the definition, so the claim is built in
    rather than asserted. For accountability, cite **Duggan et al.**
14. Because algorithmic management is **scientific management with the
    measurement constraint removed**. Taylor's premise — a manager's job is to
    increase efficiency in a production system — is unchanged; what is new is
    that keystroke logging, GPS routing and automated task allocation measure
    continuously, at zero marginal cost, for every worker at once. The deck's
    own framing is "A Return to the Past?" and "Same, same but different?"
15. **Creating jobs, guaranteeing rights, social protection, social dialogue**
    (ILO; SDG 8). Against algorithmic terminations: *guaranteeing rights* — an
    automated non-renewal or system lock-out executes a decision with legal
    consequences without a human decision-maker to hold to a standard; *social
    dialogue* — there is no manager to appeal to, so the worker cannot
    participate in a decision that affects their life.
16. Stanford (Centre for Future Work, 2020): the **weakness** of Australian
    business investment in new technology does not make jobs safer — it means
    more jobs sit in low-productivity, low-tech, low-wage industries, "with
    terrible implications for wages and job quality". His report is titled
    *The Robots Are Not Coming, and why that's a bad thing*. It is on the slide
    because it inverts the topic's premise: the risk to Australian workers may
    be **under**-automation, not over-automation.
17. *"It is not algorithms that shape worker motivation, but how organisations
    design and use them"* (Gagné et al., 2022; Langer & Landers, 2021).
    Impact is a property of the management decision, not of the technology.

</details>

## Part D — Numerical drills

Every one of these has been recomputed. Write your arithmetic down before
revealing.

1. `profnath` prices at 0.99830 × bordeebook's price; `bordeebook` prices at
   1.27059 × profnath's price. What is the compound daily growth rate of the
   pair? Starting from $1,730,045.91, how many days to $18,651,718.08?
2. Global recorded music revenue fell from an estimated US$37 billion in 1999
   to US$25 billion in 2007. State the change as a percentage and name the
   base.
3. Australia Post delivered 5.2 billion letters in 2008, 4 billion in 2015 and
   2 billion in 2024. Give all three pairwise percentage changes.
4. Digital platform work in Australia went from 7.1% of the workforce in 2019
   to 14.1% in 2023. Express the change two ways and say which is which.
5. 56.9% of organisations use monitoring software, 29.3% video surveillance,
   28.7% GPS, 28.2% chatbots, 23.0% biometric devices. What do these sum to,
   and what does that tell you?
6. The Australian auto industry's voluntary scheme would allow an average of
   98 g CO₂/km for new passenger cars in 2030; current European standards
   specify 95 g/km. State the gap as a percentage and name the base.
7. A worker is contracted for 40 hours a week for six years and does roughly
   50 hours of actual work. What share of contracted time is that?
8. Kartell lists the A.I. chair at €494,00 for 2 pieces and the A.I. Metal at
   $1650,00 for 2 pieces. Give the per-unit prices and state why they cannot
   be compared.

<details>
<summary>Answers D — with working</summary>

**1.**
```
0.99830 × 1.27059 = 1.26843   →  +26.84% per day, compounding
log(18,651,718.08 ÷ 1,730,045.91) ÷ log(1.26843) = 10.0 days
```
Exactly ten days — 8 April to 18 April. The bordeebook listing at that point,
$23,698,655.93, is the same 1.270588 multiple of the profnath price, so the
rules never changed.

**2.**
```
(25 − 37) ÷ 37 = −0.3243  →  −32.4%
```
Base is 1999 global recorded music revenue (US$37 bn). A fall of $12 bn. Note
this is *global recorded music revenue*, not US album sales — the deck also
mentions US album sales hitting a 20-year low by 2007, and the two are
different measures.

**3.**
```
2008 → 2015:  (4 − 5.2) ÷ 5.2 = −23.08%   (−3.68% per year compound)
2015 → 2024:  (2 − 4)   ÷ 4   = −50.00%   (−7.41% per year compound)
2008 → 2024:  (2 − 5.2) ÷ 5.2 = −61.54%   (−5.80% per year compound)
```
The 2024 figure also comes with a service change — delivery only every second
day — so volume and service level fell together.

**4.**
```
Percentage-point change:  14.1 − 7.1 =  +7.0 pp
Relative change:          14.1 ÷ 7.1 =  1.986  →  +98.6%
```
"Almost doubled" is accurate; "doubled" is a rounding. The base is the share
of the Australian workforce doing digital platform work.

**5.**
```
56.9 + 29.3 + 28.7 + 28.2 + 23.0 = 166.1%
```
It exceeds 100%, so this is a **multi-response** item — each figure is the
share of organisations using *that* technology, and organisations use several.
It is not a distribution and there is no residual category of organisations
"using nothing".

**6.**
```
(98 − 95) ÷ 95 = 0.0316  →  +3.2%
```
Base is the European standard, 95 g/km. Three grams is a small absolute
number; the direction is the point — the Australian voluntary proposal is
*weaker* than a standard already in force elsewhere, and Britain plans to ban
most new petrol and hybrid cars from 2030.

**7.**
```
40 h × 52 weeks × 6 years = 12,480 h contracted
50 ÷ 12,480 = 0.401%
```
Four-tenths of one per cent. The output was still delivered and the tests
still passed.

**8.**
```
€494 ÷ 2 = €247 per chair        (A.I., Swedish store)
$1650 ÷ 2 = USD 825 per chair    (A.I. Metal, US store)
```
Not comparable: different products, different materials, different currencies,
different national stores. Any sentence of the form "the AI chair rose from
€494 to $1,650" is a fabricated comparison built from two screenshots that
happen to share a slide.

</details>

## Part E — Extended response

These are the shapes a longer exam question takes in this unit. Plans, not
essays.

1. *"Digital transformation is a technology problem."* Evaluate, using
   material from Lectures 2, 5 and 6.
2. A national retailer is considering an autonomous dynamic-pricing system.
   Using Topic 3, identify the three controls you would require before
   approving it, and justify each from a named case.
3. Assess algorithmic management against the ILO's four pillars of decent
   work.
4. Explain, with evidence, why this unit insists on "skills over job titles".
5. Two definitions of disruptive innovation circulate in this unit. Argue for
   the usefulness of each with a case, then say what is lost by adopting only
   one.

<details>
<summary>Answer plans E</summary>

**1.** Disagree, and evidence it three ways. *Lecture 6*: the barriers section
of Vial's framework is dominated by **resistance**, and the deck's own key
takeaway is that overcoming barriers "has a lot to do with staff capability
and culture". *Lecture 5*: every entry in the cobot **challenges** column is a
management problem — fear, reluctance, skills, training, job design, culture —
not an engineering one. *Lecture 2*: the strategy process places digital
choices inside PLOC and the five-step process, so the decision is a management
decision before it is a technical one. Concede the obvious counterpoint —
technical feasibility is a real gate — then close on Lecture 5's line: it is
not the algorithms, it is how organisations design and use them.

**2.** Three controls, each anchored to a case. **(i) A ceiling and a circuit
breaker** — from *The Making of a Fly*: two individually sensible rules
compounded at +26.84% per day because nothing stopped them; the question is
never "is the rule sensible" but "what stops it". **(ii) A monitoring human
with authority to intervene** — from the automation levels: the only
difference between level 4 and level 5 is that human, and the pricing loop was
a level-5 process everyone assumed was level 4. **(iii) A constrained action
space** — from Palisade's chess result: the failure mode is not the model
misbehaving but the model reaching something it should not have been able to
reach. Add a governance gate before all three: **permissibility** (check one
of the ten ChatGPT governance checks) — is this use allowed at all.

**3.** Take the pillars in turn. *Creating jobs* — the platform model is
"labour without labour costs, workers without employees", so work is created
without employment; set against it the cobot opportunity column (new jobs, new
skills, addressing labour shortages). *Guaranteeing rights* — algorithmic
terminations execute legally consequential decisions with limited human
oversight (Duggan et al.'s definition names exactly this). *Social protection*
— independent-contractor status excludes platform workers from entitlements;
platform work in Australia rose from 7.1% to 14.1% between 2019 and 2023.
*Social dialogue* — there is no manager to appeal to; the supervisory function
is the app. Conclude with the PwC position ("protect people not jobs") and
Stanford's inversion as the two policy responses.

**4.** Three pieces of evidence. **The WEF chart pair**: Graphic Designers sits
in the fastest-*declining* list while UI and UX Designers sits in the
fastest-*growing* list — same profession, opposite trajectories, because the
generative tools absorb the production of visual assets while interaction
design grows. **The Australian projections slide** collapses "data entry,
reporting, coding" into one declining row while the WEF chart has Data
Analysts and Big Data Specialists growing and Data Entry Clerks declining —
the title hides the split, the skill does not. **The automation levels**: what
changes is not whether a job exists but which level of the scale it sits at,
which is a statement about tasks and skills, not titles. Close on the unit's
own framing: technological literacy is a critical skill *across all
industries*.

**5.** **Christensen** buys you the mechanism: accessible and affordable
innovation entering below the incumbent and improving upward — Kimai, selling
lab-grown diamonds and recycled gold into a market defined by mined stones and
middlemen. **Samson et al.** buys you the cases the original misses: Uber and
AirBnB did not enter at the low end, they radically changed the rules of the
game, with low fixed costs, variable cost structures and explorative
innovation against incumbents locked into high fixed costs and exploitative
improvement at the fringes. Adopt only Christensen and you cannot classify the
two most-cited disruptions of the last fifteen years. Adopt only Samson et al.
and "disruption" expands until it means any successful new entrant and stops
predicting anything. The mark is for naming the tension.
