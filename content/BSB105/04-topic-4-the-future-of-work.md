## 4.2 Robots → cobots → humanoids

Topic 2 §2.10 defined a robot and named the productivity–flexibility
trade-off that keeps industrial robots task-specific. Lecture 5 is the
resolution of that trade-off.

**Cobots (collaborative robots)** are designed to work alongside people
rather than inside a safety cage. The Australian Cobotics Centre slide sets
the challenges and opportunities side by side:

| Management challenges | Opportunities |
|---|---|
| Impact on jobs — possibly fewer jobs | New, different jobs |
| Job security concerns — fear and resistance | Ability to address labour shortages |
| Technology reluctance — knowledge and access | New skills |
| Changing skill needs — what skills, and where do you get them? | Safer jobs |
| Training needs — costs, time and resources | Fewer tedious tasks — improved job quality |
| Workplace design, job design, workforce planning | Worker retention |
| Impact on workplace culture and team relationships | Improved workforce diversity and longevity |
| | Company and industry growth |

The slide's own summary: *"Cobots represent workplace change with potential
positive outcomes and potential downsides."*

> **Every entry in the left column is a management problem, not an
> engineering one.** Fear, reluctance, skills, training, job design, culture.
> This maps straight onto §1.18's barriers to digital transformation, and it
> is the reason the unit keeps insisting that transformation failure is a
> people failure. A business case that budgets for the cobot and not for the
> training, the job redesign and the resistance has costed roughly half the
> project.

**Humanoids** are the next step, and the deck's two projections need care:

- *"Billions of humanoid robots globally by 2040"* — World Economic Forum
  (2025), *Humanoid robots offer disruption and promise*. Working outside
  factories, in healthcare, public space maintenance, retail service,
  personal assistance and more.
- *"By 2050… there will be one robot for every eight humans"* — Forbes, July
  2025.

> **Those two projections are in tension, and you should say so rather than
> quote both approvingly.** Take any plausible 2050 world population — 9 to 10
> billion — and one robot per eight humans is **1.1 to 1.3 billion robots**.
> "Billions" (plural) by 2040 means at least two billion. The earlier
> forecast is therefore *larger* than the later one, on a comparable basis.
> They also may not count the same thing: the WEF figure is specifically
> humanoids, the Forbes figure just says "robot." Name the mismatch, pick
> one, and say which. **Confirm with your tutor which figure the unit expects
> you to use.**

The lecture leaves the humanoid section on two open questions rather than an
answer: *How will they change work? How will they change society?*

## 4.3 On-demand labour and the platform triangle

The transition slide is a set of Australia Post figures:

| Year | Letters delivered |
|---|---|
| 2008 | 5.2 billion |
| 2015 | 4 billion |
| 2024 | 2 billion (*"and only every second day"*) |

```
2008 → 2015:  (4 − 5.2) ÷ 5.2   = −23.08%   (−3.68%/yr compound)
2015 → 2024:  (2 − 4)   ÷ 4     = −50.00%   (−7.41%/yr compound)
2008 → 2024:  (2 − 5.2) ÷ 5.2   = −61.54%   (−5.80%/yr compound)
```

Against that, **AmazonFlex launched in Australia in 2020** and by **2023 was
one of the top five platforms for gig work.**

> **The letters figure is not a decline story on its own — it is a
> substitution story.** Letter volume fell 61.5% over sixteen years while
> parcel-delivery gig platforms scaled. The same national logistics demand
> moved from a public-sector employer with permanent staff to private
> platforms with independent contractors. That reframing — *the work did not
> disappear, the employment relationship did* — is the thesis of the entire
> back half of Lecture 5.

Two definitions the deck distinguishes:

- **Freelancer** — self-employed, earns per project or task.
- **Gig workers / digital platform workers** — independent contractors, paid
  by task, connected with clients via a digital platform. Can be in-person
  work, or online work.

**The triangular relationship** between worker, client and platform. The
platform operates in three ways:

1. Connecting and matching workers and clients
2. Facilitating contracts and payments
3. **Automated oversight and accountability — client driven**

The deck states the resulting business model in two lines:

> *"labour without labour costs, workers without employees."*
> *"Management without a manager — Algorithmic Management."*

> **Function 3 is the one that makes it algorithmic management.** Matching
> and payments are just a marketplace; eBay does both. It is *automated
> oversight* — the platform performing the supervisory function, driven by
> client ratings rather than by a manager — that converts a marketplace into
> an employer-shaped thing with no employer. If an exam question asks what
> distinguishes a gig platform from a job board, this is the answer.

## 4.4 Algorithmic management — three definitions

The deck gives three, from three sources, and they emphasise different
things. Learn which is which.

| Source | Definition | Emphasis |
|---|---|---|
| **Lee et al. (2015, p. 1603)** | When humans are *"assigned, optimized and evaluated through algorithms"* | The three managerial functions |
| **Mateescu & Nguyen (2019, p. 1)** | *"A diverse set of technological tools and techniques to remotely manage workforces, relying on data collection and surveillance of workers to enable automated or semi-automated decision-making"* | Data and surveillance as the input |
| **Duggan et al. (2020, p. 6)** | *"A system of control where… algorithms are given the responsibility for making and executing decisions affecting labor, … limiting human involvement and oversight"* | Control, and the removal of human oversight |

The deck's own gloss: algorithmic management *"involves surveillance,
tracking, automated scheduling or task allocation, nudging, reporting."*

**"App work"** — the gig-economy form — is where an algorithm:

- assigns gigs / allocates tasks
- tracks worker location and task time
- requests ratings and reviews
- determines who gets offered a gig
- determines visibility of worker profiles
- determines pricing (sometimes)
- limits worker access to the platform and to gigs

*(Duggan et al., 2020; Lee et al., 2015; McDonald et al., 2020, 2021;
Williams et al., 2021; Wood et al., 2019.)* And the crucial extension: *"now
extends to other work contexts"* (Kellogg et al., 2019).

> **Pick your definition to match your argument.** If you are writing about
> privacy, cite Mateescu & Nguyen — surveillance is in the definition itself.
> If you are writing about accountability or worker voice, cite Duggan et al.
> — "limiting human involvement and oversight" is the phrase that carries the
> claim. Quoting all three in a row and analysing none is the most common way
> to waste 150 words in this unit.

> **Kellogg et al. (2019) is the citation that makes this topic examinable
> beyond Uber.** Algorithmic management is *not* a gig-economy phenomenon
> any more. Everything in §4.5 happens inside conventional organisations
> with permanent employees.

## 4.5 Algorithmic management across the employee lifecycle

The deck's densest slide maps automation onto every stage of HRM. Reorganised
into lifecycle order:

| Stage | Technology | What it does |
|---|---|---|
| **Attract** | Recruitment & selection tech | Writing job ads; identifying and contacting passive candidates; candidate management chatbots |
| **Select** | Recruitment & selection tech | Screening applications; psych/cognitive testing and scoring; **AVIs (automated video interviews)** |
| **Onboard** | Onboarding tech | Automated issuance of offers and contracts; managing compliance training; employee self-service apps |
| **Schedule** | Rostering / scheduling software | Distributes rosters, manages employee availability |
| **Attend** | Time & attendance tech | Bioscan time clocks, log-in, screenshots |
| **Allocate** | Workforce optimisation | Allocates tasks, tracks employee location, time, routes |
| **Develop** | Training management tech | Managing training — prompts and completion |
| **Monitor** | Feedback tech | Individual analytics "insights" — feedback on productivity; real-time oversight of employee/team progress |
| **Appraise** | Performance appraisal tech | Online performance reviews, suggested comments |
| **Protect** | Safety & wellbeing apps | Hazard identification, sentiment analysis, biometrics |
| **Design** | Job/work design tech | Optimises task or workspace design, VR, robotics |
| **Secure** | Data / security / access tech | Automates access, blocks access, finds data and security risks |
| **Predict** | Predictive analytics | Predicting intention to leave; sentiment analysis; early intervention |
| **Plan** | Workforce planning tech | Business intelligence, reporting |
| **Exit** | Automated offboarding | Automating exit interviews and surveys |
| **Terminate** | **Algorithmic terminations** | Automating contract renewals (or not); providing evidence of non-performance; automatic lock-outs from systems |

The slide's caption for the whole thing: *"Monitoring employee activity,
productivity, behaviour, moods, attitudes, interactions…"*

Three of these get their own slide.

**Keystroke tracking / keylogger software.** Operates by running in the
background, capturing keystrokes and taking screenshots; used to detect
employee fraud and non-compliance. *"Raises questions relating to: privacy
and 'free time'; transparency and consent; fairness of performance
management."*

**Sentiment analysis.** Analyses communications and classifies them as
positive, negative or neutral, in order to *"improve employee experience;
boost productivity; spread positive behaviour; manage negative behaviour;
improve remote working."* *"Raises questions relating to: bias,
discrimination, performance management, selection, wellbeing."*

**Recruitment tech.** Asynchronous interview questions, chatbot questions,
automated ranking of candidates, assessment of traits, curated shortlist —
with a vendor claim quoted directly from Sapia.ai: *"Text is one of the best,
fairest, and most reliable ways to determine a candidate's soft skills,
behavior traits, and overall cognitive ability."*

> **Read the Sapia.ai line as a claim under test, not as a finding.** It is
> marketing copy on a vendor's homepage, and the deck reproduces it beside a
> list of the exact questions it raises — bias, discrimination, selection.
> "Best, fairest, and most reliable" are three separate empirical assertions
> with no citation attached. Using it in A2 as evidence that text-based
> screening is fair is the CRAAP-test failure the Week 7 tutorial is built to
> prevent; using it as an *example of an unevidenced vendor claim* is exactly
> right.

> **Algorithmic terminations is the row to notice.** Everything above it in
> the table automates *information*. That row automates a *decision with
> legal consequences* — non-renewal, lock-out, documented non-performance —
> and it maps onto automation level 4 or 5 in §3.11 of note 03 depending on whether a
> human signs it off. It is the clearest place in the unit where the
> monitoring human in level 4 is the entire difference between a managed
> process and an unreviewable one.

## 4.6 What Australian organisations actually use, and why

**Most common technology used in organisations:**

| Technology | Share of organisations |
|---|---|
| Monitoring or tracking software on laptops, phones or other devices | 56.9% |
| Video surveillance to monitor employees | 29.3% |
| GPS or other location tracking devices | 28.7% |
| Chatbots to answer employee questions | 28.2% |
| Biometric devices such as fingerprint or eye-scanning | 23.0% |

Sector split: **public sector** uses keystroke tracking and wearables;
**private sector** uses all other devices. Main GenAI tools in HR: **Microsoft
Copilot** and **ChatGPT**.

> **These percentages do not sum, and they are not meant to.** 56.9 + 29.3 +
> 28.7 + 28.2 + 23.0 = 166.1. Each is the share of organisations using *that*
> technology, and organisations use several — this is a multi-response item,
> not a distribution. Writing "56.9% of organisations use monitoring software,
> 29.3% use video surveillance, and the rest use nothing" is a
> misreading that a marker will catch immediately.

> **The slide carries no in-text citation for these figures.** The deck's
> reference list includes Williams, P., Kummer, T.-F., Maskor, M. & Kennon, C.
> (2024), *The State of AI in Australian Human Resources*, Australian HR
> Institute — which is the obvious source, and the lecturer is its first
> author. But the attribution is inferred, not stated. **Verify against the
> AHRI report before citing a specific percentage.**

**Why are they being used?** Four stated rationales, each with the employer
quotes the research collected:

| Rationale | Employer justifications quoted |
|---|---|
| **Safety & security** — safety of humans (clients or workers), security of data | *"For the safety & security of employees"*; *"to prevent and identify fraud"* |
| **Productivity & efficiency** — track productivity, improve efficiency | *"so we can efficiently allocate work"*; *"to ensure workers turn up on time"*; *"lack of trust and cost control"* |
| **Enable remote work** — offer and monitor remote work | *"to track productivity during COVID and remote work"*; *"because we have a remote workforce"* |

> **One of those quotes is not like the others.** *"Lack of trust and cost
> control"* is filed under productivity and efficiency, but it is not a
> productivity rationale — it is an admission about the employment
> relationship. Employers describe surveillance in the language of safety and
> efficiency; the quote that slipped through describes it in the language of
> distrust. If you are asked to critique employer justifications for
> algorithmic management, that single phrase is the evidence, and it comes
> from the employers themselves rather than from a critic.

## 4.7 A return to Taylor?

The deck puts **Frederick Taylor** and **Scientific Management Theory** on
screen with one line — *"a manager's job is to increase efficiency in a
production system"* — under the heading **"A Return to the Past?"**

Then the industrial-revolution parallel: *18th-century Great Britain —
technical innovation = new tools and machines, increasing production and the
pace of change. Technological change, socio-economic change, political and
cultural change.* The slide's verdict on the comparison: **"Same, same but
different?"**

> **This is the highest-value paragraph in Lecture 5 for an essay
> introduction.** Time-and-motion study measured what a worker did with a
> stopwatch and a clipboard, and Taylorism was criticised for reducing people
> to production inputs. Keystroke logging, GPS routing and automated task
> allocation measure the same things continuously, at zero marginal cost, for
> every worker at once. The management *philosophy* is a century old; what is
> new is that the measurement constraint that limited it has gone. Framing
> algorithmic management as *Taylorism without the clipboard* gets you a
> critical argument rather than a description.

## 4.8 How jobs are changing — the Australian evidence

**Multiple job holding** (ABS, latest release):

| Measure | Figure |
|---|---|
| Multiple job holders, 2024 | **961,400 — 6.5% of employed people** |
| Employed women who hold multiple jobs | 7.2% |
| Employed men who hold multiple jobs | 5.7% |
| Workers aged 20–24 (most likely age group) | 7.2% |

```
Implied employed population: 961,400 ÷ 0.065 ≈ 14.79 million
Gender gap: 7.2 − 5.7 = 1.5 percentage points
```

> **The two 7.2%s are different cuts of the same population, not the same
> people.** One is a gender breakdown, one is an age breakdown, and they
> coincide at one decimal place by chance. A sentence like "young women are
> the most likely to hold multiple jobs at 7.2%" combines them into a claim
> the source does not make. If you want that claim you need the cross-tab,
> and the slide does not have it.

**Platformisation and casualisation:**

- Digital platform work in Australia grew from **7.1% in 2019 to 14.1% in
  2023**.
- **1 in 5 workers in Germany and 1 in 3 in Spain** are subject to at least
  one form of algorithmic management.
- **10–20% of clerks, operators, high-tech industries, knowledge-intensive
  services and public administration** have been "platformised" (EC Joint
  Research Centre).

```
7.1% → 14.1%  =  +7.0 percentage points
              =  +98.6% relative increase over four years
```

> **"Doubled" is close enough to say, but say which measure.** +7.0
> *percentage points* and +98.6% *relative* are both correct and describe
> different things; 14.1 ÷ 7.1 = 1.986, so "almost doubled" is accurate and
> "doubled" is a rounding. The base is the share of the Australian workforce
> doing digital platform work, per the McDonald/Williams national surveys
> (2019 and 2023) — name the base or the number means nothing.

**GenAI and humanoid robot teams:**

- **GenAI** may affect **white-collar knowledge work** — clerical, admin,
  analyst.
- **Humanoids** may affect **blue-collar roles requiring mobility and
  dexterity** — manufacturing, logistics, cleaning and maintenance,
  construction, health, care, food preparation, personal services.
- Human–robot workforces, robot assistants, HRI teams.
- Blurred work–life boundaries; hybrid work; working anywhere; *working less
  or working more?*

**Projected jobs growth and decline in Australia** (Dixon & Lennox, August
2025 — *These jobs will thrive — but others may vanish — as AI transforms
Australia's workforce*), consolidated with McKinsey Global Institute (2023),
Seek (Feb 2024) and Jobs and Skills Australia employment projections:

| Declining | Growing |
|---|---|

| Retail roles — warehousing and sales | Sustainability and environment |
| Office support, clerks, admin | Construction |

## 4.9 Decent work

The normative frame for the whole lecture. The ILO definition, quoted in
full on the slide:

> *"Decent work sums up the aspirations of people in their working lives. It
> involves opportunities for work that is productive and delivers a fair
> income, security in the workplace and social protection for families,
> better prospects for personal development and social integration, freedom
> for people to express their concerns, organize and participate in the
> decisions that affect their lives and equality of opportunity and treatment
> for all women and men."*

*(ILO, 2021; United Nations, 2021; Blustein et al., 2019; Somavia, 2008.)*
Four pillars: **creating jobs, guaranteeing rights, social protection, social
dialogue.** Decent work is UN Sustainable Development Goal 8.

> **Decent work is a standard, and it is the one algorithmic management is
> measured against.** Take the four pillars against §4.5: *guaranteeing
> rights* versus algorithmic terminations; *social dialogue* versus a system
> where there is no manager to appeal to; *social protection* versus
> independent-contractor status. Each of the four gives you a specific,
> citable criticism instead of a general unease. That is the difference
> between a credit and a distinction answer on the social-implications
> question.

Two positions the deck sets against each other:

**PwC (2022)** — *"Organisations cannot protect jobs which are made redundant
by technology — but they do have a responsibility to their people. Protect
people not jobs. Nurture agility, adaptability and re-skilling."*

**Jim Stanford, Director, Centre for Future Work Australia (2020)** — *"The
unprecedented weakness of business investment in new technology does not mean
that Australian jobs are somehow safer. To the contrary, the failure of
business investment means that even more jobs will be located in
low-productivity, low-tech, low-wage industries — with terrible implications
for wages and job quality."*

> **Stanford inverts the entire premise of the topic, and that is why he is
> on the slide.** Every other source treats technology as the threat to jobs.
> Stanford's report is titled *The Robots Are Not Coming, and why that's a bad
> thing*: under-investment traps workers in low-productivity, low-wage work.
> If an exam question invites you to argue that automation threatens
> Australian employment, the highest-scoring move is to present the opposite
> risk with this citation and then adjudicate between them.

**The 5th Industrial Revolution** (Gauri & Van Eerden, 2019): *maximising
human–machine strengths; sustainable production = sustainable work.*

**Lecture 5's four key takeaways, verbatim:**

1. The future of work is shaped by humans.
2. How technology is used impacts decent work.
3. Skill development will be increasingly critical.
4. Management decisions about technology and work have social implications.

Under them, the line that carries the argument — *"It is not algorithms that
shape worker motivation, but how organisations design and use them"* (Gagné
et al., 2022; Langer & Landers, 2021).

> **That last sentence is the answer to the tutorial question.** Week 5 asks
> *how are algorithms used in business and society and with what impact?* The
> unit's position is that impact is not a property of the technology; it is a
> property of the management decision. Every case in this topic supports it —
> the repricing bots did what they were told, o1-preview did what it was
> told, the platform's rating system does what it was designed to do. If your
> answer blames the algorithm, you have written the wrong answer.

## Exam traps in this topic

1. Quoting *"billions of humanoid robots by 2040"* and *"one robot per eight
   humans by 2050"* together without noticing the earlier figure is the larger
   one.
2. Treating the Australia Post letter decline as a story about decline. It is
   a story about substitution — the work moved, the employment relationship
   changed.
3. Describing a gig platform as algorithmic management on the strength of
   matching and payments alone, without automated oversight.
4. Quoting all three definitions of algorithmic management in a row and
   analysing none. Pick the one that carries your argument.
5. Treating algorithmic management as a gig-economy phenomenon. Kellogg et al.
   (2019) is the citation that extends it to conventional employment.
