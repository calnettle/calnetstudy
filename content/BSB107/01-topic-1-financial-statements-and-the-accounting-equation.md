## 1.1 The vocabulary is the exam

Almost every mark lost in this half of the unit is lost by putting an item on
the wrong statement. The definitions below are quoted or paraphrased from the
unit's own vocabulary sheet — learn them in these words, because the
questions are written in them.

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| **Asset** | A resource expected to result in future financial benefits. Colloquially, something of value that you possess. |
| **Liability** | An obligation or debt — something you owe. Often has "Payable" in its name. |
| **Equity** | Assets less liabilities. Also called **Net Worth** or **Capital**. |
| **Revenue** | Money earned from your usual work or investments. Can include gifts, donations and allowances. Revenue increases net worth. |
| **Expense** | A cost incurred or "used up" in the current period. |
| **Cost** | An outflow of money. If it clearly gives future benefit it is an **asset**; if it does not, and reduces net worth, it is an **expense**. |
| **Receivable** | An amount someone owes you. All receivables are assets. |
| **Payable** | An amount you owe someone else. All payables are liabilities. |
| **Debtor** | Someone who owes you money — a receivable, therefore an asset. |
| **Creditor** | Someone you owe money to — always a liability. |
| **Prepaid asset** | A cost paid before it is used. An asset when paid; becomes an expense month by month as it is consumed. |
| **Net assets** | Total assets − total liabilities. **Always equals equity.** |
| **Bottom line** | The last line of the income statement: net income or net loss. |
| **Budget** | An estimate of future finances, used to control spending, test whether a purchase is viable, or work out whether borrowing is needed. |

Two lines from the vocabulary sheet are worth more than the rest put
together, because they are what separates a revenue from a non-revenue:

> **"Swapping one asset for another, or taking a loan, does not generate
> revenue."** Selling your car for exactly what it was worth is not income.
> Borrowing $27,000 is not income. Both change the *shape* of your balance
> sheet without changing your net worth at all.

## 1.2 The three statements

| Statement | Question it answers | Period or point? |
|---|---|---|
| **Income Statement** | Am I better or worse off? | **Over a period** — "for the month of March" |
| **Balance Sheet** | What am I worth right now? | **At a point in time** — "as at 31 March" |
| **Cash Flow Statement** | Where did the money physically go? | **Over a period** — "for the month of March" |

Get the headings right. A balance sheet headed "for the month of March" is
wrong, and a marker will take the mark.

```
Income Statement       Revenue − Expenses            = Net Income
Balance Sheet          Assets − Liabilities          = Equity (Net Worth)
Cash Flow Statement    Receipts − Payments           = Net cash movement
                       Opening cash + movement       = Closing cash
```

### The one idea the whole unit rests on

**Revenue is not receipts, and expenses are not payments.** They coincide only
by accident. This is *accrual* accounting: you record revenue when it is
**earned** and an expense when it is **used up**, regardless of when the cash
moves.

| Situation | Income Statement | Cash Flow Statement | Balance Sheet |
|---|---|---|---|
| Wages earned but not yet paid to you | Revenue now | Nothing yet | **Receivable** (asset) |
| Electricity used, bill due in June | Expense now | Nothing yet | **Payable** (liability) |
| 12 months of insurance paid upfront | 1 month expensed | Full amount out now | **Prepaid** (asset) for the other 11 |
| You buy a jumper | Nothing | Payment now | Asset value increases |
| You donate clothes to charity | Expense now | **Nothing** | Asset value decreases |

The last two rows are where marks go missing. Buying an asset is *not* an
expense. Giving an asset away *is* an expense even though no cash moved.

> **Trap — the prepayment split.** A 12-month car insurance and rego premium
> of $1,305 paid in March is **not** a $1,305 March expense. One month is
> consumed: `1,305 / 12 = $108.75` expense, and `1,305 × 11/12 = $1,196.25`
> sits on the balance sheet as a prepaid asset. The cash flow statement still
> shows the full $1,305 going out. One transaction, three different numbers
> on three different statements — that is the whole point of the exercise.

### Net income $1,441.25, but cash fell by $305. Why?

This reconciliation is the single most likely short-answer question in the
unit. The gap is $1,746.25, and it decomposes exactly:

```
Net income                                              1,441.25
− Revenue earned but not received (wages receivable)     (600.00)
+ Expense incurred but not paid (electricity)             125.00
− Cash paid but not yet expensed (prepaid insurance)   (1,196.25)
+ Non-cash expense (clothes donated)                       30.00
− Cash paid to acquire an asset (clothes purchased)      (105.00)
                                                       ──────────
Net cash movement                                        (305.00)  ✓
```

Every line is a *timing* or *classification* difference. Nothing is missing
and nothing is double-counted.

> **Trap — the tutorial's own cash flow statement does not quite tie.** The
> data says the $22 of interest was "credited directly to Investment
> account", yet the supplied solution lists it among cash receipts and then
> reconciles the **bank account** from $3,730 to $3,425. Those cannot both be
> true. Without the $22, receipts are $3,078, the net movement is −$327 and
> closing cash is **$3,403**, not the $3,425 the question states. The stated
> closing bank balance is the anchor, so the numbers were built with the
> interest landing in the bank and the narrative wording is loose. Either
> treat the interest as a bank receipt (which reconciles) or exclude it and
> say in your workings why closing cash then differs by $22. **Confirm the
> intended treatment with your tutor.**

### The advice question

Every one of these cases ends with "is there any advice you need to give?".
Markers want observations tied to the numbers, not general life coaching.
From these statements:

- Steve is **profitable but cash-negative** — net income $1,441.25 against a
  $305 fall in the bank. That is fine for one month, but it is happening
  because $600 of wages are outstanding and $1,196.25 of insurance was paid
  in advance.
- The **June cash cliff** is real: a $125 quarterly utilities bill and a
  $2,100 holiday both land in June with nothing set aside.
- Most of his net worth is in **non-income-producing assets** — a piano, a
  car, furniture. Only the $5,780 investment account earns anything, at
  roughly $22 a month.

## 1.4 The accounting equation

Four forms, all the same statement, each useful for a different question:

```
(1)   Assets − Liabilities = Equity
(2)   Assets = Liabilities + Equity
(3)   Assets − Liabilities = Opening Equity + Net Income
(4)   Assets − Liabilities = Opening Equity + (Revenue − Expenses)
```

Form (4) is the one the Week 3 worksheet is laid out in, and the one to
memorise, because it links the balance sheet and the income statement in a
single line:

```
   ASSETS   −   LIABILITIES   =   OPENING EQUITY   +   ( REVENUE  −  EXPENSES )
```

Every transaction must keep both sides equal. If your row doesn't balance,
you have missed a leg.

### How to attack a transaction

Ask three questions in this order:

1. **Did anything of value come in or go out?** → assets column.
2. **Did I take on or discharge an obligation?** → liabilities column.
3. **Did my net worth change?** → if yes, it is revenue or an expense. If no,
   it is a swap and the income columns stay blank.

Question 3 is the one people skip, and it is the one that catches the trick
transactions.

## 1.5 Worked example — Steve's April transactions

*Week 3 tutorial. Opening equity $28,801.25 (rounded to $28,801 in the
worksheet), opening assets $28,926.25, opening liabilities $125.*

| # | Transaction | Δ Assets | Δ Liabilities | Revenue | Expenses |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Pays weekly rent of $200 | −200 | | | 200 |
| 2 | Transfers $4,000 from investment account to bank | **0** | | | |
| 3 | Sells his car for $6,200, no profit or loss | **0** | | | |
| 4 | Receives wages $1,150 into his bank account | +1,150 | | 1,150 | |
| 5 | Buys crypto worth $5,000: $4,000 cash, $1,000 loan | +1,000 | +1,000 | | |
| 6 | Spends $80 on petrol | −80 | | | 80 |
| 7 | Lends $100 to his friend Adam | **0** | | | |
| 8 | Crypto investment rises in value by $600 | +600 | | 600 | |
| 9 | $25 interest credited to his bank account | +25 | | 25 | |
| 10 | Buys a car for $32,000: $5,000 deposit, rest financed | +27,000 | +27,000 | | |
| 11 | Receives Austudy allowance $639 | +639 | | 639 | |
| 12 | Sydney trip: $1,200 travel, $600 entertainment | −1,800 | | | 1,800 |
| 13 | Sells his piano for $5,000 (he thought it worth $3,800) | +1,200 | | 1,200 | |
| 14 | Electricity and water $125, payable in June | | +125 | | 125 |
| 15 | First car payment $500, of which $200 is interest | −500 | −300 | | 200 |
| | **Totals** | **+29,034** | **+27,825** | **3,614** | **2,405** |

### Check it

```
Change in equity = ΔAssets − ΔLiabilities = 29,034 − 27,825 = 1,209
Net income       = Revenue − Expenses     =  3,614 −  2,405 = 1,209   ✓
```

Those two must agree. If they don't, a row is missing a leg.

```
Closing assets      = 28,926.25 + 29,034 = 57,960.25
Closing liabilities =      125.00 + 27,825 = 27,950.00
Closing equity      = 28,801.25 +  1,209 = 30,010.25

Check:  57,960.25 = 27,950.00 + 30,010.25   ✓
```

### The rows that decide the mark

**#2, #3 and #7 are all zero.** Swapping an asset for another asset of equal
value — cash for a bank balance, a car for its exact book value, cash for a
receivable from Adam — changes nothing. Net assets unchanged, no revenue, no
expense. Most people correctly zero #2 and get caught by #3 or #7.

**#5 and #10 are not expenses.** Buying $5,000 of crypto or a $32,000 car is
an asset acquisition. Cash falls, a new asset appears, and a loan appears.
Net worth is untouched. Transaction #10 is the largest single line on the
sheet at $32,000 and has **zero effect on net income and zero effect on net
worth**, which is exactly why the tutorial asks about it.

**#8 and #13 are revenue.** A revaluation ("crypto rises by $600") increases
net worth without any cash moving, so it is revenue. Selling the piano for
$5,000 when it was carried at $3,800 produces $1,200 of revenue — the
**gain**, not the $5,000 proceeds. Booking the full $5,000 as revenue is the
classic error: $3,800 of it was already on the balance sheet.

**#15 is the split that catches everyone.** A $500 loan repayment where $200
is interest:

```
Cash               −500   (the whole payment leaves)
Loan liability     −300   (only the principal portion reduces the debt)
Interest expense   +200   (the cost of borrowing)

Check:  ΔAssets − ΔLiabilities = −500 − (−300) = −200
        Revenue − Expenses     = 0 − 200       = −200   ✓
```

The principal part is a balance-sheet swap; only the interest hits the income
statement.

> **Trap — the sign convention on liability reductions.** In transaction 15
> the liabilities column takes **−300**, not +300 and not −500. Writing −500
> makes it look as though the whole payment reduced the debt, and the row
> stops balancing. If you cannot make a row balance, you have almost
> certainly put the wrong number in the liabilities column.

> **Trap — a wrong answer inside the tutorial file.** In the Week 3 tutorial
> worksheet, the prompt *"Did net income change by more or less than net
> worth? Why?"* is answered with **"They are the same because the accounting
> equation always needs to be the same."** The first half is right for this
> question set and the reason is wrong. Net income and the change in net
> worth agree **only because every equity movement here came through revenue
> or an expense.** Introduce a capital contribution, an owner drawing, or a
> dividend, and net worth moves without net income moving at all — which is
> exactly what happens in the business half of the unit, where Adam and Eva
> contribute $60,000 of capital. Say *"they agree here because there were no
> capital contributions or withdrawals"*, not *"they always agree"*.

### Non-cash transactions in the set

Transactions **8** (revaluation) and **14** (accrued utilities) involve no
cash at all. That is what makes net income differ from net cash flow.

## 1.6 Checkpoint

<details><summary>Questions</summary>

1. You pay $2,400 in March for a 12-month gym membership starting 1 March.
   What appears on the March income statement, and what appears on the
   31 March balance sheet?
2. You sell a bike carried at $700 for $900 cash. What is the revenue?
3. You take out a $15,000 personal loan. By how much does your net worth
   change?
4. Your employer owes you $1,300 of wages at month end. Which statement(s)
   does this appear on?
5. Net income is $2,000 and net cash flow is −$500. Name three kinds of item
   that could explain the gap.
</details>

<details><summary>Answers</summary>

1. **$200 expense** (2,400 ÷ 12) on the income statement; **$2,200 prepaid
   asset** on the balance sheet. The cash flow statement shows the full
   $2,400 out.
2. **$200.** The gain, not the $900 proceeds — $700 was already an asset.
3. **Zero.** Cash +15,000, liability +15,000. Taking a loan is not revenue.
4. **Income statement** ($1,300 revenue, because it was earned) and
   **balance sheet** ($1,300 receivable). **Not** the cash flow statement —
   no cash has moved.
5. Any three of: revenue earned but not received (receivables), expenses
   incurred but not paid (payables), cash paid in advance of consumption
   (prepayments), non-cash expenses (donated or written-off assets), cash
   spent acquiring assets, cash received from borrowings, loan principal
   repayments.
</details>

## Topic 1 summary

- Three statements, three questions: **am I better off** (income statement,
  over a period), **what am I worth** (balance sheet, at a point), **where did
  the money go** (cash flow statement, over a period).
- **Accrual, not cash.** Revenue when earned, expense when used up. Timing
  differences become receivables, payables and prepayments.
- A cost that buys future benefit is an **asset**; a cost that does not is an
  **expense**.
- `Assets − Liabilities = Opening Equity + (Revenue − Expenses)`. Every
  transaction keeps this true.
- **Swaps and loans are not revenue.** Only the *gain* on a sale is revenue.
- On a loan repayment, only the **interest** is an expense.
- Always cross-check: `ΔAssets − ΔLiabilities` must equal `Revenue − Expenses`
  when there are no capital contributions or withdrawals.
