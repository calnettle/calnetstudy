## 1. Gross Income Strategies

Gross income = base rent + outgoings recoveries + other income (car
parking, signage, licences, percentage rent). The Week 9 SAM tutorial's
list, in the slide's words, with what each means:

- **Expenditure to maximise rent increases**: refurbish so the space supports a higher rent
- **Tenant mix to maximise patronage and turnover**
- **Promotional activities to increase patronage**
- **Diversify income sources – signage, licenses, car parking and kiosk**
- **Timely market reviews**: a missed review is income lost for good
- **Minimise vacancy periods**
- **Manage delinquencies**: arrears
- **Active lease renewal strategies**: start talking to tenants early, before the option window

Sort them by time frame. The tutorial set this as a class activity and the
slides give no answer key, so this is one defensible sort. Justify yours
the same way: how long it takes to act, and when the lease lets you.

| Short term (1–2 yrs) | Medium term (3–5 yrs) | Long term (6–10 yrs) |
|---|---|---|
| Timely reviews, arrears control, promotions, leasing up vacancies, casual mall and signage licences | Lease renewal programme, tenant mix changes as leases expire, refurbishment to lift rents | Repositioning, extensions, new income streams (EV charging, solar, telco), redevelopment |

The other three strategy families, for completeness:

| Family | Strategies |
|---|---|
| **Outgoings** | New technology to cut utilities and maintenance · timers and meters · individual metering (user pays) · structure leases for maximum recovery · long-term service contracts · tender service contracts · lobby government on statutory costs · preventive maintenance |
| **Cap rate** (market sets the range, the manager controls asset quality) | Income and outgoings strategies · attractive WALE · frequency and terms of reviews · good quality tenants · historically low vacancy · limited arrears · few disputes · well maintained |
| **Capital value / portfolio** | Protect property rights · monitor the local environment · adapt to changing needs · buy and sell · regular valuations · funding |

> **Why it matters:** each extra $1 of recurring net income is worth
> `1 / CR` in value. At 6.5%, $1 a year = $15.38, so $20,000 of new signage
> income ≈ **$307,700** of capital value.

## 2. Financial Performance of Buildings (Rent, Outgoings)

The income ladder, top to bottom:

```
  Base (face) rent
+ Outgoings recoveries
+ Other income (parking, signage, licences, % rent, electricity profit)
= GROSS INCOME
− Outgoings (statutory + operating + non-recoverable)
= NET INCOME (NOI)
÷ Cap rate
= CAPITAL VALUE
```

Worked example:

```
Base rent              1,120,000
+ Recoveries             180,000
+ Other income            60,000
= Gross income         1,360,000
− Outgoings             (250,000)
= Net income           1,110,000
Value at 6.5% cap     17,076,923     (1,110,000 / 0.065)

Recovery rate   180,000 / 250,000     = 72.0%
Expense ratio   250,000 / 1,360,000   = 18.4%
```

The measures to quote:

| Measure | Formula | Tells you |
|---|---|---|
| Recovery rate | recoveries ÷ outgoings | How much of the running cost tenants pay back |
| Expense ratio | outgoings ÷ gross income | Running-cost efficiency |
| Outgoings per m² | outgoings ÷ NLA (office) or GLA (retail) | Compare against the **PCA benchmark** |
| Vacancy rate | vacant area ÷ lettable area | Leasing performance |
| Arrears | rent overdue (days, $) | Tenant quality and collection |
| Yield | net income ÷ price | State **passing** or **fully leased** |
| GOCR (retail) | tenant's occupancy cost ÷ its turnover | Can the tenant afford the rent? Anchors ≈ 2–4%, specialties 8–15% |
| Productivity (retail) | turnover ÷ m² | How well a shop trades |

**Outgoings: in and out**

| ✅ Outgoings | ❌ Not outgoings |
|---|---|
| Council rates, water and sewerage | Capital expenditure |
| Land tax (**not** under a QLD retail shop lease) | Depreciation, sinking funds |
| Building insurance | Landlord's interest and loan costs |
| Cleaning, security, fire testing, HVAC servicing | Marketing funds |
| Common-area power, repairs and maintenance | Insurance excesses |
| Management fees | |

Rule of thumb: **capital, financing or promotional costs are never
outgoings.** Servicing the lift is an outgoing; replacing it is not.

Kallangur Fair (Tutorial 4): only **27.7%** of outgoings were recovered,
because the supermarket (68.9% of the area) pays nothing towards them. A
low recovery rate isn't automatically bad management; check what the
anchor's lease says first.

## 3. Risk Management Matrix

Risk = **variance from what you expected.** It can be positive or negative.

| | **Low impact** | **High impact** |
|---|---|---|
| **High probability** | Adjust systems to lower the probability · benchmark · low-cost system fixes | **Concentrate effort here** · specific risk management plan · major strategy changes |
| **Low probability** | Maintenance management and response · systems and records · watch for trends | **Insurance** · lower the risk profile in specific areas |

```
Risk inside the property   → NON-SYSTEMIC  (the manager can control it)
Risk outside the property  → SYSTEMIC      (market-wide, e.g. the cash rate)
```

Examples to place on the matrix:

| Risk | Probability / impact | Response |
|---|---|---|
| Specialty tenant arrears | High / low | Arrears system, bank guarantees, credit checks |
| Anchor doesn't renew | Low–medium / high | Early renegotiation, backfill plan, longer WALE |
| Fire or flood | Low / high | **Insurance**, essential services compliance |
| Lift breakdowns | High / low | Preventive maintenance contract |
| Cash rate rise | Systemic | Can't control: hedge debt, lengthen the WALE |

> **Trap:** insurance is the answer for **low probability, high impact**.
> It is never the answer for a high-probability risk.

Risk areas from the guest lecture: workplace health and safety · fire and
essential services compliance · environmental (contamination, waste) ·
insurance and hidden costs.

## 6. Tenant Selection and Tenant Mix for Specific Assets

> The unit's slides cover tenant **mix** (Week 1, 4 and 9) but say little
> on **selection**, so the selection checklist below is standard practice
> rather than slide content.

**Tenant selection: what to check before signing**

- **Covenant strength:** financial statements, trading history, credit check, references
- **Security:** bank guarantee or bond, personal or parent-company guarantee
- **Fit with the mix:** does the use add something, or clash with an existing tenant's exclusive-use or permitted-use clause?
- **Can they afford it?** In retail, project the tenant's GOCR: rent ÷ expected turnover
- **Lease term fit:** does their term help the WALE and the expiry profile, or bunch expiries together?
- **Fitout and brand quality:** does it lift the centre or building?
- **Industry concentration:** don't load one industry. Week 9 feedback named mining-boom tenants and RTOs (training colleges) as examples of tenants that leave together in a downturn

**Tenant mix: what it is.** The combination of tenants that maximises
foot traffic and turnover for the whole asset. In retail the manager
curates it through the **permitted use** clause.

| Asset | Mix that works |
|---|---|
| **Neighbourhood centre** | Supermarket anchor + daily needs: pharmacy, bakery, newsagent, butcher, medical, café, takeaway. Convenience, not fashion |
| **Sub-regional** | Discount department store + supermarket + 40+ specialties, adding services and some fashion |
| **Regional / super regional** | Department store(s), DDS, 2 supermarkets, fashion, food court, entertainment (cinema), dining. It's a destination, so experience matters |
| **CBD office** | Strong covenant anchor tenants on long leases (government, banks, ASX-listed), plus smaller tenants on staggered expiries. Spread across industries |
| **Industrial** | Logistics and transport tenants with strong covenants. Location, access and clear height matter more than the mix |

> **Anchor placement:** put anchors at the ends (the dumbbell idea) so
> shoppers walk past the specialty shops between them. Specialties pay the
> high rent per m²; anchors bring the people.

## 7. Legal Responsibilities of the Property Manager

The manager is the **owner's agent**. Agency carries explicit duties
(contracts, legislation, codes of conduct, rules) and implicit ones (client
expectations, community values, **ethics and morals**, cultural issues).

**The nine legal duties** (Week 1):

1. Duty to **perform**
2. Act with **professional skill and due care**
3. Give **professional advice**
4. **Not delegate** authority
5. Act in the principal's interests with **utmost good faith**
6. **Not act on their own behalf**
7. **No secret commissions or profits**
8. Keep the principal's business **confidential**
9. **Account and keep the owner informed**

Duties 5, 6 and 7 are one idea, **no conflicts of interest**: a manager
who takes a hidden rebate from the cleaner breaches all three (and 9).

**The three points from your list:**

| Point | What to write |
|---|---|
| **Contract negotiations** | The manager negotiates leases, renewals and service contracts **within the authority the agency agreement gives them** (financial delegations). Four contract types: property (leases, licences), capital works, professional services, service contracts (cleaning, security, maintenance, waste) |
| **Moral recommendations** | Beyond the law: give honest advice even when the owner won't like it (e.g. the building needs capex), treat tenants fairly, consider the community. These are the implicit agency duties: ethics, morals, community values |
| **Never act without the owner's consent** | The manager can't sign, commit money or change lease terms outside their delegation without the owner's approval. They also can't hand their authority to someone else (duty 4) |

**The agency agreement** must be **in writing** and cover: parties and the
property · duration · specific duties · payment · financial delegations ·
confidentiality · reporting · termination and variation · dispute
resolution.

**The manager's role is more than collecting rent** (Week 9): lease
renewals · stakeholder management · legal management (reviewing leases) ·
contractor management · budget management · strategic planning.

## 8. Benefits of the Lease: What It Contains

**What a lease is:** a legal contract where the landlord (lessor) gives the
tenant (lessee) **exclusive possession** of the premises for a set term, in
exchange for rent. A licence gives no exclusive possession; that's the
difference.

**What each side gets (the benefits):**

- **Certainty for both parties** about their obligations (Week 8)
- **Landlord:** a secure income stream that a valuer capitalises and a bank lends against. *"Buildings without tenants generate little revenue."*
- **Tenant:** security of tenure, a platform for their business, and **quiet enjoyment**
- Strong leases **lower risk, improve finance terms and increase value**

**Each side's obligations:**

```
LESSOR   provide quiet peace and enjoyment of the premises for the term
LESSEE   pay rent · take reasonable care of the property · use it only for the
         permitted use · no sublease or assignment without approval ·
         hand the premises back at the end (make good)
```

**What a lease contains: the checklist**

| Clause | What to find |
|---|---|
| Parties and premises | Lessor, lessee, the leased area on a plan, guarantors, signatures |
| Term | Commencement date, term, expiry date: these feed the WALE |
| **Options** | Period, conditions for taking it up, and **the window the tenant must exercise it in** |
| **Rent and reviews** | Initial rent, review timing and mechanism (minimum increases, ratchet), arbitration |
| **Outgoings** | Defined, who's responsible, how they're paid, and the effect on the income stream |
| Permitted use | Restrictions on use, which control the tenant mix |
| Alterations and fitout | Altering, fitting out, signage, who owns the fixtures |
| Repairs and maintenance | Landlord's rights to inspect and repair; who replaces capital equipment |
| **Make good** | Actions at the end of the lease: cleaning, repaint, re-carpet |
| Car parking | Exclusive use often by separate licence; visitor parking |
| Insurance | Public liability amounts |
| Damage and destruction | What happens if the building can't be occupied (rent abatement, right to terminate) |
| Default and security | Late payment, personal guarantees, bank guarantee, level of security |
| Access | Rights of access and use of shared facilities (multi-tenant buildings) |
| Demolition / relocation | The landlord's right to end the lease for redevelopment |
| Assignment and subletting | Provisions for it, and the lessor's consent |
| Registration | Is the lease registered (and duty paid)? If not, is the tenant protected if the building is sold? |
| Option to purchase | First right of refusal, if any |

**The Week 8 tutorial, answered from the Kessels Road lease.** These 11
questions are the lecturer's own list of "the important things". The
lease: Lease E, about 506 m² on the ground floor of 521–531 Kessels Road,
Macgregor, let for **children's amusement** from 11 November 2024 for
5 years. It's a **retail shop lease**: clause 1.1 defines "the Act" as the
*Retail Shop Leases Act 1994* (Qld), and clause 26 says the Act prevails.

| # | Question | Answer from the lease |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Rent at commencement? | **$144,210 a year plus GST** (Item 10), about $285/m², paid monthly in advance (cl 3.1) |
| 2 | How is rent reviewed? | **Fixed 4% increases** each anniversary (Item 11, cl 3.2). **Market review on 10/11/2029** (the option start). CPI: not applicable. The landlord sends a review notice 9–12 months ahead; the tenant has 14 days to dispute it; then an independent valuer decides and the parties share the cost (cl 3.3–3.6) |
| 3 | Statutory outgoings recoverable? | **Yes.** "Outgoings" includes rates, taxes, levies and charges (definition, para (a)), and the tenant pays its proportion, **21.19%** (Item 12, cl 4.1). Not capital repairs. Because it's a Qld retail lease, **land tax can't be passed on**, whatever the clause says |
| 4 | Option exercise date? | Option of **1 × 2 years**. Notice **at least 9 and not more than 12 months before expiry** (cl 15.1), so between **10 Nov 2028 and 10 Feb 2029**, and only if the tenant has paid on time and complied with the lease |
| 5 | Landlord's inspection and repair rights? | On **reasonable notice** (none in an emergency) the landlord may enter to view the state of repair, maintain or repair the building and services, deal with fire hazards, comply with laws and fix the tenant's breaches at the tenant's cost (cl 18.3) |
| 6 | Notice to vacate at the end? | **No separate notice clause.** The lease simply ends on **10/11/2029** unless the option is exercised. If the tenant holds over with consent, it becomes a **monthly tenancy at rent + 5%**, ended by **1 month's notice** either way (cl 24.1) |
| 7 | Car parks included? | **None.** "The Tenant has no right to park cars on the Land" |
| 8 | Ratchet in the market review? | **No.** The valuer sets the current market rent, so rent can go down. (And under the Qld Act a ratchet in a retail shop lease would have no effect anyway) |
| 9 | Public liability insurance? | The lease puts it on the **tenant: at least $20 million**, protecting the landlord (cl 9.1). The landlord insures the building as it sees fit, and those premiums are a recoverable outgoing |
| 10 | Building damaged and can't be used? | **Rent abates** by a reasonable amount until the premises are usable. The tenant can terminate on 7 days' notice if the landlord hasn't started restoring within a reasonable time (at least 2 months after the tenant's written request). The landlord can terminate if the law requires demolition or it decides not to restore (cl 22) |
| 11 | Carpets on vacating? | **Remove the floor coverings and replace them with ones the landlord approves** (cl 11.6), as part of returning the premises **as an empty shell**: remove fitout and partitions, paint walls 2 coats, reinstate the ceiling. The landlord can waive this for an agreed payment |

> **Security deposit:** 6 months' rent + outgoings + GST, **$96,280.43** at
> commencement (Item 14). The guarantors are three individuals (Item 3).
> Both are the "security" row of the checklist above.

## 9. Gross vs Net (Income and Leases)

Two meanings. Be clear about which one the question is asking.

**Gross vs net INCOME**

```
Gross income  = everything received (rent + recoveries + other income)
Net income    = gross income − outgoings
Value         = net income ÷ cap rate        ← valuers use NET
```

**Gross vs net LEASES** (your note: "gross includes outgoings")

| Lease | Tenant pays | Who carries the risk of outgoings rising |
|---|---|---|
| **Gross lease** | One amount; **the outgoings are built into the rent** | **Landlord** |
| **Net lease** | Rent **plus** their share of outgoings, budgeted and reconciled each year | Tenant |
| Semi-gross | Rent plus some outgoings, or increases above a base year | Shared |

> **Trap:** a $500/m² gross rent and a $500/m² net rent are not the same
> deal. The net-lease tenant also pays maybe $100/m² of outgoings on top.
> Convert to the same basis before comparing.

> **Trap:** a tenancy schedule shows gross income only. You can't get net
> income without the outgoings budget or the income and expenditure statement.

## 10. GLA and NLA Calculations (PCA)

| | **NLA** (Net Lettable Area) | **GLA** (Gross Lettable Area) |
|---|---|---|
| Used for | **Office** | Industrial, warehouses, showrooms, free-standing supermarkets |
| External walls | Measured to the **INSIDE** surface | Measured to the **OUTSIDE** face |
| Walls between tenancies | Centreline | Centreline |
| Structural columns | Included | Included |
| Lift lobbies, shafts, stairs, toilets, plant rooms, foyers | **Excluded** | **Excluded** |
| Size | Smaller | Larger, for the same building |

**GLAR** (retail): from the inside of external walls, to the centreline of
walls between tenancies, to the **mall or shop line**. A shopfront that
sticks out past the mall line is included.

**The dominant portion rule** (applied wall by wall): if windows make up
**more than 50%** of the wall, measure to the inside of the glass. If less,
measure to the wall surface.

> **GLA: single vs multiple tenancy.** The slides say "note the difference
> when let as a single or multiple tenancy". The exclusions (lift lobbies,
> shafts, stairs, toilets, foyers) apply **in a multiple-tenanted
> building**. A single tenant who has the whole building gets it all in
> the GLA. Protruding columns on the outside face are never included.

**Worked example:** a floor in a **multi-tenanted** building, measuring
40 m × 25 m to the outside face, external walls 0.3 m thick, an 80 m² lift
and stair core, 30 m² of toilets.

```
GLA   40 × 25                      = 1,000.00 m²   outside face
      − core 80 − toilets 30       =   890.00 m²   GLA

NLA   inside: (40 − 0.6) × (25 − 0.6)
           = 39.4 × 24.4           =   961.36 m²   inside face
      − core 80 − toilets 30       =   851.36 m²   NLA
```

> **Trap:** never compare $/m² across NLA and GLA. GLA is always bigger, so
> the same dollars give a lower $/m².

## 11. Types of Shopping Centres (PCA)

This is a **size and tenant-mix** ladder, **not** a quality ladder.

| Type | GLA | Anchors |
|---|---|---|
| City centre | > 1,000 m² | CBD arcades and malls dominated by specialty shops. Generally no supermarket; often sit next to department stores |
| **Neighbourhood** | < 10,000 m² | Supermarket + ~35 specialties |
| Sub regional | 10,000–30,000 m² | DDS + major supermarket + ~40+ specialties |
| Regional | 30,000–50,000 m² | Department store + DDS + supermarket(s) + ~100+ specialties |
| Major regional | 50,000–85,000 m² | Department store + DDS(s) + supermarket(s) + ~150+ specialties |
| **Super regional** | > 85,000 m² | **2 department stores** + DDS(s) + 2 supermarkets + ~250+ specialties |
| Bulky goods | > 5,000 m² | Bulky goods retailers, few specialties |

DDS = discount department store (Kmart, Big W, Target).

**Layouts:** strip centre (no enclosed walkway, parking out front) · mall
(enclosed, air-conditioned walkway) · cluster or town centre (separate
buildings joined by walkways or streets).

> **Trap:** check the GLA band first, then the anchors. A 42,000 m² centre
> can't be Major Regional however strong its tenants are. And with no
> supermarket or department store it doesn't fit any row. Classify it by
> layout instead.

|---|---|---|---|---|
| NABERS Energy | 5★ | 4.5★ | 4★ | 3★ |

| Green Star Performance | 4★ | 3★ | 2★ | 2★ |

**Size (NLA), from the same guide:**

| | Premium | A | B |
|---|---|---|---|
| Existing, other CBDs (Brisbane falls here) | > 20,000 m² | > 5,000 m² | > 2,500 m² |
| Existing, floor plate, other CBDs | > 1,000 m² | > 800 m² | — |
| **New, Brisbane / Perth CBDs** | **> 25,000 m²** | **> 10,000 m²** | **> 5,000 m²** |

**What separates the grades:** location and views, size and floor plate,
lift performance, air-conditioning quality, end-of-trip facilities (bikes
and showers), sustainability ratings, and the quality of on-site

- **Premium:** landmark, best location, top ratings, high-quality lobby and services
- **A:** high quality, modern, good ratings
- **B:** older or refurbished, reasonable services
- **C / D:** basic, older, low ratings, likely to need capex or a change of use

> Market point: **flight to quality**. Tenants are moving to Premium and A
> grade buildings after COVID, so B and C grade vacancy rises. Brisbane CBD
> vacancy was 10.7% in July 2025.

## 13. Green Buildings, NABERS Ratings and Regulatory Tools

**Why it matters (the 40 / 40 / 20):** buildings use **40%** of energy,
emit **40%** of carbon emissions and use **20%** of drinking water.

**A green building** uses energy, water and materials more efficiently
while reducing harm to health and the environment, **across its whole
life**: siting, design, construction, operation, maintenance and removal.

**The rating tools:**

| Tool | Run by | Rates |
|---|---|---|
| **NABERS** | Government (NSW-run national programme, since 1998) | **Actual operating performance**: energy, water, waste, indoor environment. 1–6 stars, **renewed annually** from 12 months of real bills |
| **Green Star** | Green Building Council of Australia (industry, since 2002) | **Design and construction** across nine categories. 1–6 stars, a one-off certification |
| **EnviroDevelopment** | UDIA (Qld 2006, national 2009) | Development projects: ecosystems, waste, energy, materials, water, community |
| **NatHERS** | National | Houses: thermal performance. The minimum moved from 6 to 7 stars (the slide says "NCC – 2023") |
| **WELL** | International | Health and wellbeing of the people inside. The slides call it "the next evolution": Green Star and NABERS focus on the building, WELL on the people in it |

**NABERS star scale:** 1 Poor · 2 Below average · 3 Average · 4 Good ·
5 Excellent · **6 Market leading**.

> **Green Star vs NABERS is the exam question.** Green Star = how it was
> **designed and built** (intent). NABERS = how it **actually runs**
> (measured). A 6-star Green Star building can still have a poor NABERS
> rating if it's run badly. The manager's performance shows in the NABERS
> trend.

**Regulatory tools:**

- **Commercial Building Disclosure (CBD) program** under the Building Energy Efficiency Disclosure Act 2010 (see 14)
- **Green leases** (Week 5): a framework for both parties to achieve and keep energy, water and waste efficiencies through the term. How they differ from a standard lease: green provisions in the lease or an attached schedule · **consequences if either party misses the agreed targets** · an **Environmental Management Plan** and a **Building Management Committee**. Benefits: tenants get lower outgoings, CSR and staff satisfaction, a healthier workplace; landlords get operating savings + higher rent = higher value, better retention (less vacancy), better-calibre tenants. The Qld Government Green Lease Policy targets 90% for leases over **2,000 m²**
- Qld Government Sustainable Office Building Rating Policy
- NCC energy-efficiency minimums
- NABERS **commitment agreements:** a developer commits to a target rating before there's 12 months of data

**Value case:** higher rents and occupancy, lower outgoings, lower cap
rates (a "green premium"), tenant demand (ESG reporting), and a "brown
discount" for poor performers.

## 15. Taxation of Property: CGT, GST, Capex vs Opex

**The tax stack:**

| Level | Taxes | Can it be recovered from tenants? |
|---|---|---|
| Federal | Income tax, CGT, GST (flood levy) | No (owner's tax). GST passes through |
| State | **Land tax**, stamp duty (sale, lease registration, mortgage stamping) | Land tax: yes, **except QLD retail shop leases**. Stamp duty on purchase: no |
| Council | General rates, fire levy | Yes (outgoing) |
| Other | Water usage and service charges | Yes (outgoing) |

The slide also lists government sustainability incentives (solar rebates,
FBT treatment of EV charging) as part of the picture.

**Negative gearing ≠ negative cash flow** (a favourite). Negative gearing
is a *tax* position: total deductions (interest, outgoings, depreciation)
exceed rental income, and the loss reduces tax on other income. Negative
cash flow is a *cash* position: money out exceeds money in. Depreciation is
a deduction but not a cash cost, so a property can be negatively geared
while its cash flow is positive. The slide adds that the deduction only
offsets part of the holding cost, and that the property generally becomes
positively geared over the long term.

> **From the Week 6 slide, word for word:** *"recent legislative changes
> passed by the Australian Government with respect to Capital Gains tax and
> Negative Gearing have no impact on commercial property, these changes
> only impact residential."* If a question is about a commercial asset,
> say this.

**CGT**

- Not a separate tax. The net capital gain is added to taxable income
- Applies to assets bought **after 20 September 1985**
- Gain = sale price − selling costs − **cost base** (purchase price + buying costs)
- **50% discount** for individuals who've held the asset 12+ months
- Building allowance (division 43) deductions **reduce the cost base**, so they increase the gain later
- Your home (principal place of residence) is exempt

```
Cost base     5,000,000 + 250,000 costs   = 5,250,000
Net proceeds  8,000,000 − 150,000 costs   = 7,850,000
Capital gain                              = 2,600,000
Individual, held 12+ months: × 50%        = 1,300,000 added to taxable income
```

(The 50% discount is for individuals and trusts. A company gets no
discount and pays tax on the full $2.6m.)

**GST** (10%)

| Transaction | GST? |
|---|---|
| Commercial rent, purchase, costs | **Yes**, and input tax credits can be claimed |
| Residential rent | **No**, and no input tax credits (so GST is a real cost for residential landlords) |
| New residential and land | Yes |
| Sale of a leased commercial building as a **going concern** | **GST-free** if both parties are registered, the leases continue and it's agreed in writing |

**Capex vs opex**

| | **OPEX** (repairs and maintenance) | **CAPEX** (capital works) |
|---|---|---|
| Tax | **100% deductible this year** | Deducted over time (depreciation) |
| Tests | Replaces a **worn part, not the whole** · restores original condition · restores function · fixes an early fault or health risk | **New or different material** · an **improvement** · **increases value** · reduces future repairs |
| Example | Replace 20 damaged roof sheets · repair the chiller · repaint | New roof · bigger, more efficient chiller · solar, EV chargers |
| Outgoing? | Usually recoverable | **Never** an outgoing |

**Building allowance:** for income-producing buildings **constructed after
20 September 1985**, 2.5% a year (written off over 40 years), straight
line, on the **construction cost only** (not land, not purchase price).
Plant and equipment is depreciated over its effective life (prime cost or
reducing balance).

> **Trap, and the slide disagrees with tax practice.** The slide's OPEX
> test includes restoring a building to its *"original condition or
> condition at purchase"*. Under ATO practice, fixing defects that were
> already there when you bought the building is **initial repairs**, which
> is capital. In the exam, give the slide's test, then add the
> initial-repairs exception in one line. Confirm with the tutor if it
> comes up.

## 16. Retail Leases Act

The slides mention the Act in one place: **land tax can't be recovered
under a Qld retail shop lease** (Week 7 outgoings tutorial). Two pieces of
unit material show it working: the Week 7 Auburn Quarter 2026/27 budget
carries an outgoings audit fee *"as required under Retail Leases act"*, and the
Kessels Road lease (item 8) is written to it. Everything else below is
standard **Retail Shop Leases Act 1994 (Qld)**, not slide content. Check
any of it you plan to rely on.

**Why it exists:** to protect small retail tenants, who have far less
bargaining power than large centre owners. A lease clause that conflicts
with the Act is read down, and the Act wins. The Kessels Road lease says
exactly that (cl 26.1).

- **Disclosure statement:** the landlord must give the tenant a disclosure statement and the draft lease **before** the lease is signed (at least 7 days). If it's missing or misleading, the tenant gets termination rights. (Kessels Road cl 26.3: the tenant warrants it got every document the Act requires, on time)
- **Ratchet clauses on market reviews have no effect** under the Act. In an office or industrial lease a ratchet stands
- **Land tax can't be recovered** as an outgoing (the one point that is on the slides)
- **Outgoings:** the landlord gives an estimate before each financial year and an **audited statement within 3 months** after it ends. Kessels Road cl 4.3 sets out both, in the form the Retail Shop Leases Regulations prescribe
- **No key money:** the landlord can't charge a fee for granting the lease
- **Relocation and demolition** clauses need proper notice, and compensation in some cases
- **Disputes:** mediation through the Queensland Small Business Commissioner first, then QCAT
- **Who it doesn't cover:** premises of **1,000 m² or more**. Since amendments in November 2016 that applies **whoever the tenant is**. Before 2016 the large-premises exclusion only applied if the tenant was also a listed company. This is why big anchors (supermarkets, department stores) usually fall outside the Act

> **The exam point:** retail tenants get protection that office and
> industrial tenants don't. Ratchets and land tax are the two places this
> changes the numbers.

## 17. Rent Terms: Effective, Face, Base, Reviews, Ratchet, Cap and Collar, Make Good

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Face rent** | The rent written on the lease |
| **Base rent** | The fixed rent before outgoings and before any percentage (turnover) rent |
| **Effective rent** | Face rent minus the value of incentives, spread over the term: what the deal really earns |
| **Rent review** | A scheduled change in rent. The Week 5 list: **no increase** · **fixed increases** ($) · **percentage increases** · **CPI** · **market review** ("return to market") |
| **Review modifiers** | **Ratchet** · **"either or"** (the higher of two methods, e.g. CPI or 4%) · a **floor** · **minimum increases** · **collar and cap** |
| **Ratchet clause** | Rent **can't go down** at a market review. Not allowed in QLD retail shop leases |
| **Cap and collar** | The review is held between a minimum (collar) and a maximum (cap) |
| **Make good** | At the end of the lease the tenant returns the premises to their original condition: remove fitout, repaint, recarpet, or pay cash instead. A common dispute at lease expiry |
| **Incentive** | Rent-free period, rent abatement (reduced rent), fitout contribution, cash |

**Effective rent, worked:**

```
1,000 m² · face rent $500/m² · 5-year term
Incentives: 6 months rent-free + $150,000 fitout contribution

Total face rent    500 × 1,000 × 5                 = 2,500,000
Rent-free          6 months × 500,000 a year       =   250,000
Total incentive    250,000 + 150,000               =   400,000   (16%)
Effective rent     (2,500,000 − 400,000) ÷ 5       =   420,000 a year = $420/m²
```

**Why landlords give incentives instead of cutting the face rent:**
valuers capitalise the face rent, so a lower face rent cuts value
permanently. An incentive is a one-off cost.

**Cap and collar, worked** on $400,000 (CPI review, collar 2%, cap 4%):

```
CPI 1.1%  →  collar applies  →  2.0%  →  $408,000
CPI 3.2%  →  neither applies →  3.2%  →  $412,800
CPI 6.8%  →  cap applies     →  4.0%  →  $416,000
```

**Ratchet, worked:** market falls 8% on $400,000. With no ratchet the rent
drops to $368,000. With a ratchet it stays at $400,000.

**A real review pattern: Kessels Road** (item 8). $144,210 a year on about
506 m², fixed 4% each anniversary, then a market review at the option
start. No ratchet.

```
Year 1  (from 11 Nov 2024)   144,210.00   ≈ $285/m²
Year 2                       149,978.40   ≈ $296/m²
Year 3                       155,977.54   ≈ $308/m²
Year 4                       162,216.64   ≈ $321/m²
Year 5                       168,705.30   ≈ $333/m²
10 Nov 2029  market review → rent can go UP or DOWN (no ratchet)
```

Fixed increases give the landlord certainty in a flat market. The market
review at the option is where the rent resets to the market, in either
direction.

## 18. Corporate Real Estate (Week 8)

**Definition:** property a company uses **for its own operations**, not as
an investment. Offices, data centres, factories, logistics centres,
headquarters, retail stores, hotels. **Its main value to the company is how
it helps the business operate, not its investment value.**

**The corporate real estate executive:** anticipates and responds to the
company's property needs as the business grows or shrinks; manages large,
often international portfolios; works with external providers (brokerage,
site selection, design, lease administration, facilities management,
technology). The role has moved from managing buildings to **adding
strategic value** and improving **worker productivity and wellbeing**.

Corporate real estate and asset management (**CREAM**) was established as a
discipline in Australia in **1995**, after starting in the US. The private
sector led it; it's now well established in the public sector, and
legislated in some countries.

**The five types (memorise these):**

| Type | Own or lease? | Example / term |
|---|---|---|
| **Strategic** | Must **own and control** | Manufacturing centre, long term |
| **Landmark** | Owned or leased, but must be **controlled** | Shows the company's image or culture (a head office), medium to long term |
| **Core** | Must **control**, doesn't have to own | Offices, industrial and retail sites it operates from, medium term |
| **Peripheral** | **Nearly always leased** | Short-term or seasonal needs: extra warehousing, serviced offices |
| **Surplus** | For **sale** | Doesn't fit the business plan, reviewed constantly |

Sub-categories: buildings · construction in progress · land · leases ·
natural resources · other.

**The four levels:** business strategy → CRE portfolio management →
CRE asset management → CRE operations (transactions, facilities, project
management).

**Why portfolio management helps:** corporate real estate ties up a lot of
capital; it supports strategy; provides governance and planning; gives
better decision support and reporting data. Badly managed, it's costly and
a distraction.

**Challenges:** short-term flexibility vs long-term leases or ownership ·
changes in the external environment (economic, market, demographic,
political, global) and the business · industry volatility · business
demands · real estate is inflexible (supply and demand mismatch, low
supply).

**Flexible CRE portfolios, what to weigh:** purchase vs lease · cost-benefit
analysis · why the extra space is needed · land and building banks for
future growth · the impact on the balance sheet · operational and
organisational issues · workplace, HR and organisational strategies.

**Private and public sector:** corporate real estate sits on the balance
sheet (the slides use Tabcorp's 2026 annual report and Brisbane City
Council's 2024–25 report). The questions the slides pose: where it sits,
how it's listed, how and how often it's valued, and why it needs valuing.

**Space portfolio strategy:**

| Space type | Share of space | What it is |
|---|---|---|
| **Core** | **70–80%** | Owned or long-term leased, staggered expiries, fully controlled, market rent with fixed review patterns |
| **Flexible** | **15–20%** | Extra space held on short-term call or put options, sometimes shared with other companies |
| **Serviced** | **5–10%** | Very short-term, shared facilities, expensive but very flexible |

**Efficiency vs effectiveness:**

```
EFFICIENCY      reduce cost · use space to full CAPACITY
EFFECTIVENESS   increase value · use space to full POTENTIAL ·
                the right work settings · get the most from your people
```

**Leased vs owned inventory:**

- **Leased:** lease term · actual vs market rent · does the lease have value? · clauses · right to assign or sublet · excess space
- **Owned:** title details · size · zoning · encumbrances · what's actually owned

**Future of work:** AI is changing work tasks; human skills are worth more;
organisations are early in AI adoption; portfolio decisions are waiting on
workforce strategy (PwC and JLL 2026 surveys). **Benefits of workplace
change:** organisational flexibility · drives business strategy · lower
costs · employee wellbeing · sustainability · responds to external global
shifts.

> **Trap:** the space split is **core 70–80 · flexible 15–20 · serviced
> 5–10**. Serviced is the smallest and the most expensive per m², but the
> most flexible. Don't mix this list up with the five *types* (strategic,
> landmark, core, peripheral, surplus): "core" appears in both.

## 19. WALE: How to Calculate It, Step by Step

**WALE = Weighted Average Lease Expiry**: the average time left on the
leases, weighted by **income** or by **area**.

```
WALE (income) = Σ (tenant income × years remaining) ÷ Σ (tenant income)
WALE (area)   = Σ (tenant area   × years remaining) ÷ Σ (tenant area)
```

**The steps:**

1. **Pick the date** you measure from: the EOI or valuation date (or the tenancy schedule's "as at" date)
2. **List every tenant** with their area, income and lease expiry date
3. **Work out years remaining** for each: `(expiry − measure date) ÷ 365.25`
4. **Handle the special cases:**
   - **Vacant space:** **include at 0 years in area WALE, leave out of income WALE**
   - Options: assume they are **not** exercised
   - Holdover or month-to-month: leave out, and state the assumption
   - Separately licensed car parks and signage: include in the income WALE
5. **Multiply** each tenant's weight (income or area) by their years remaining
6. **Add up** the products (the top line)
7. **Add up** the weights (the bottom line)
8. **Divide** top by bottom
9. **Interpret:** 5+ years = secure income, investors like it. Under 5 years = leasing risk, but also a chance to reset rents

**Worked example:**

| Tenant | Area m² | Income $ | Years left | Area × yrs | Income × yrs |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| A Supermarket | 3,000 | 900,000 | 10 | 30,000 | 9,000,000 |
| B Pharmacy | 400 | 240,000 | 4 | 1,600 | 960,000 |
| C Café | 200 | 160,000 | 2 | 400 | 320,000 |
| D Vacant | 150 | — | 0 | 0 | *left out* |
| **Total** | **3,750** | **1,300,000** | | **32,000** | **10,280,000** |

```
Income WALE = 10,280,000 ÷ 1,300,000 = 7.91 years
Area WALE   =     32,000 ÷     3,750 = 8.53 years
```

Area WALE is higher than income WALE because the long lease is on cheap,
large anchor space. The specialty income turns over faster than the floor
space does.

**Years remaining from dates:** lease expires 31 Mar 2031, measured from
1 Jul 2026 → 1,734 days ÷ 365.25 = **4.75 years**.

**Common errors** (Week 9 revision, straight from the lecturer):

- **Mixing the weighting methods.** Use area or income; don't mix them
- Using the **wrong lease expiry dates**
- **Getting vacancy wrong:** include it in area WALE, exclude it from income WALE
- Using **gross rather than net rents** where net figures are available
- **Rounding errors.** Round only at the end

> **Portfolio WALE** is weighted by each asset's income or area, **never**
> a simple average of the asset WALEs.

> **WALE drives value:** longer WALE → lower cap rate → higher value. On
> the unit's numbers, 25 basis points on the cap rate ≈ 4% of value.

## 20. Also Worth Having Ready: SAM and Scenario Analysis (Weeks 9–10)

These aren't on the content list by name, but most of the list sits inside
them.

**Strategic asset management (SAM):** deliberate, evidence-based decisions
about a property or portfolio so that it supports the owner's business
model · delivers the required financial performance · stays competitive ·
is maintained and improved at the right time · **manages risk proactively**
(market, physical, financial, regulatory). *"It's not just about
maintaining buildings, it's about optimising them as business assets."*

```
CV = NI / CR       NI = gross income − outgoings     CR = risk weighting
Value levers: gross rent · outgoings · cap rate · capital value
Time frames:  short 1–2 yrs · medium 3–5 yrs · long 6–10 yrs
```

**Scenario analysis:** examining possible future events and predicting
their effect. A structured way to assess possible outcomes, and it helps
decisions by **bookending a best case against a worst case**. Review the
plan regularly, and change it when the assumptions behind it change.
Consider the effect on the **whole portfolio**, not one building.

Part B's three scenarios, as exam examples: anchors not renewing (income
falls *and* the cap rate rises, so value falls by more than the lost rent:
on Richlands' numbers, a 47.1% income fall became a 57.7% value fall) · the
cash rate falling to 2.5% (cap rates compress, values rise) · tax
concessions for leases of 5+ years (longer WALEs).
