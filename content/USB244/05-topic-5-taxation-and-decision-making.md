## 5.1 Three Strategic Shifts in Property Taxation

| Shift | From | To |
|---|---|---|
| **What is taxed** | Tax on *ownership* | Tax on *income and capital gains* |
| **Who taxes it** | The States | The Commonwealth |
| **Three modern components** | — | **Assets** — CGT and related, from 1985 · **Company** — the Ralph Report reforms, from 1999 · **Expenditure / consumption** — GST, from 2001 |

The first shift explains why land tax and rates feel like the "old" taxes
(they attach to holding the asset) while income tax, CGT and GST are the
ones that move with what the asset *does*. The second explains why a
property manager deals with three governments at once.

## 5.2 The Tax Stack — Multilayered

| Layer | Taxes and charges | Who levies |
|---|---|---|
| **Federal** | Income tax · Capital gains tax (part of income tax) · GST · flood levy | Commonwealth (ATO) |
| **State** | Land tax · Stamp (transfer) duty — on the **sale**, on **registration of leases**, on **mortgage stamping** | Office of State Revenue (in Queensland, now Queensland Revenue Office) |
| **Council** | General rates · fire levy | Local government |
| **Other** | Water usage and service charges | Utility |
| **Incentives** (both levels) | Renewable energy — solar panel rebates · electric vehicle charging (FBT treatment) — *"a new source of income?"* | Federal and State |

> **Sort the stack by who can recover it.** Rates, water and land tax are
> **outgoings** — a net lease passes them to the tenant (land tax excepted
> for Queensland retail shop leases; see
> [Tutorial 4](#/USB244/10-tutorial-4-outgoings)). Income tax, CGT and
> stamp duty on the purchase are the **owner's** and never recoverable. An
> exam question on "the impact of taxation on net return" wants that split,
> not a list.

> **The flood levy is stale.** It was the Commonwealth's one-year Temporary
> Flood and Cyclone Reconstruction Levy (2011–12). It sits on the slide as
> an example of a federal layer, not as a current charge.

## 5.3 Taxation on Net Income

```
Income  −  Deductions  =  Taxable income
```

For an income-producing property the deductions are interest on the debt,
operating costs and maintenance (**OPEX**, §5.6), and the *non-cash*
deductions — depreciation of plant and equipment and the building
allowance (**CAPEX**, §5.7). Income is rent plus, on sale, the capital gain
(§5.5). That one line is the whole of §5.4 to §5.7; each section is one of
its terms.

## 5.4 Negative Gearing — and Why It Is Not Negative Cash Flow

The lecture's definition: **total deductions exceed total income** — the
rental return is less than interest, outgoings (OPEX) and depreciation
(CAPEX). The loss reduces the owner's tax on *other* income, which is why
it is more common in residential property, where yields are lower.

The lecture then asks: *what is the difference between negative gearing
and negative cash flow?* The answer is depreciation. It is a deduction the
owner never pays out in cash, so a property can be negatively **geared**
(a tax loss) while still being cash-flow **positive**.

Worked, on a small commercial building with $450,000 of rent, $110,000 of
OPEX, $60,000 of depreciation and building allowance, and a 30% tax rate
(the rate is an illustration — substitute the owner's actual marginal rate):

| | Interest $280,000 | Interest $320,000 | Interest $360,000 |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Rent | 450,000 | 450,000 | 450,000 |
| less interest | (280,000) | (320,000) | (360,000) |
| less OPEX | (110,000) | (110,000) | (110,000) |
| **Pre-tax cash flow** | **60,000** | **20,000** | **(20,000)** |
| less depreciation (non-cash) | (60,000) | (60,000) | (60,000) |
| **Taxable income** | **0** | **(40,000)** | **(80,000)** |
| Tax saving at 30% | 0 | 12,000 | 24,000 |
| **After-tax cash flow** | **60,000** | **32,000** | **4,000** |
| Geared / cash flow | Neutral / positive | **Negative / positive** | **Negative / negative** |

```
Taxable income   = 450,000 − 320,000 − 110,000 − 60,000 = −40,000   ← negatively geared
Pre-tax cash     = 450,000 − 320,000 − 110,000          = +20,000   ← positive cash flow
Tax saving       = 40,000 × 30%                         =  12,000
After-tax cash   = 20,000 + 12,000                      =  32,000
```

> **The deduction offsets only part of the holding cost.** Move from the
> middle column to the right one: interest rises $40,000, the tax saving
> rises only $12,000, and after-tax cash falls $28,000. At a 30% rate every
> extra dollar of loss returns 30 cents. Nobody gets rich losing money; the
> lecture's point is that the property "generally becomes positively geared
> over the long term" as rents grow and debt is repaid — negative gearing is
> a *phase*, funded from other income, not a strategy.

> **The slide's note on legislative change is residential-only.** It says
> recent Australian Government changes to CGT and negative gearing *"have no
> impact on commercial property, these changes only impact residential."*
> Quote it as the lecturer's position and check the QUT readings for the
> current law before relying on it in an assessment — tax settings move.

## 5.5 Capital Gains Tax

```
Income = Rental income + Capital gains
```

CGT is **not a separate tax** — a net capital gain is added to assessable
income and taxed at the owner's rate. All assets acquired since CGT began
on **20 September 1985** are subject to it unless specifically excluded
(the principal place of residence being the obvious exclusion). Real
estate is the most common way a capital gain is made.

```
Bought 2010   purchase price 5,000,000 + acquisition costs 250,000 = cost base 5,250,000
Sold  2026    sale price     8,000,000 − selling costs      150,000 = net proceeds 7,850,000
Capital gain                 7,850,000 − 5,250,000                  = 2,600,000
```

> **Not on the slides, but it bites: building-allowance deductions you have
> claimed generally reduce the cost base** for properties acquired after
> May 1997 — so the $4.8m of allowance the buyer in §5.7 inherits is a
> deferred tax bill on sale, not free money. The slide says "refer to QUT
> readings"; this is what to look for in them.

## 5.6 OPEX — Operating Costs and Maintenance

Maintenance is deductible **100% in the period it is incurred** — the most
valuable classification an expense can have. The lecture gives four tests;
an item qualifies as maintenance if it is any of:

1. The replacement or renewal of a **worn-out part** of something — **but
   not the whole**; and/or
2. The **restoration to original condition** (or condition at purchase) or
   function **without changing character**; and/or
3. Restoration of **efficiency in function**, not necessarily the exact
   repetition in form or substance; and/or
4. Rectification of an **incipient fault** in the building, or the control
   of **health risks**.

## 5.7 CAPEX — Depreciation and Capital Expenditure

Capital spending cannot be deducted immediately; it is claimed **over time**
as the asset declines in value. The lecture's four markers of capital:

1. Renewal in **material different** from the original; and/or
2. Work that has effectively been an **improvement**; and/or
3. Has **increased the value** of the asset; and/or
4. Expenditure to **reduce the likelihood of further repair**.

### Classification table

| Spend | OPEX or CAPEX | Which test |
|---|---|---|
| Replace 20 storm-damaged roof sheets like-for-like | **OPEX** | Worn part, not the whole; original condition |
| Replace the entire roof | **CAPEX** | The whole, not a part |
| Repaint the external walls | **OPEX** | Restoration to original condition, character unchanged |
| Replace timber windows with double-glazed aluminium | **CAPEX** | Different material; improvement |
| Repair a failed chiller compressor | **OPEX** | Restoration of function; incipient fault |
| Replace the chiller with a larger, more efficient unit | **CAPEX** (plant — depreciated) | Improvement; different in substance |
| Remove mould / asbestos, treat a legionella risk | **OPEX** | Control of health risks |
| Install EV chargers, new BMS sensors, solar array | **CAPEX** | New asset / improvement |
| Upgrade the fire system beyond compliance so it fails less | **CAPEX** | Reduces likelihood of further repair |
| Patch a car park pothole | **OPEX** | Worn part, not the whole |
| Resurface the whole car park | **CAPEX** | The whole; improvement |

> **The same dollar lands in a different year depending on the label.** A
> $120,000 chiller *repair* is a $120,000 deduction this year. A $120,000
> chiller *replacement* is depreciated over the plant's effective life — the
> deduction arrives as $24,000 a year for five years (prime cost) or front-
> loaded (reducing balance), never all at once. Classification is a cash-flow
> decision, and the ATO does not accept "we called it maintenance" if the
> whole thing was replaced. Keep the records the lecture asks for: reasons,
> photos, reports, receipts.

> **"Condition at purchase" is a trap phrase.** The slide's OPEX test says
> restoration to "original condition *or condition at purchase*". Repairs
> that fix defects which already existed when you bought the building are
> treated as *initial repairs* and are capital under Australian tax
> practice, whatever the slide's wording suggests. Confirm in the readings.

### Plant and equipment — prime cost vs reducing balance

Plant is written off over its **effective life**, by one of two methods.
$100,000 of plant with a 10-year life:

```
Prime cost        = cost / effective life          = 100,000 / 10 = 10,000 every year
Reducing balance  = rate × opening written-down value
                    (Australian "diminishing value" rate = 200% / effective life = 20%
                     for plant acquired after 10 May 2006 — the slide says only
                     "reducing balance"; the 200% figure is from the ATO rules)
```

| Year | Prime cost | Reducing balance (20% of WDV) | WDV at year end | Cumulative PC | Cumulative RB |
|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| 1 | 10,000 | 20,000.00 | 80,000.00 | 10,000 | 20,000.00 |
| 2 | 10,000 | 16,000.00 | 64,000.00 | 20,000 | 36,000.00 |
| 3 | 10,000 | 12,800.00 | 51,200.00 | 30,000 | 48,800.00 |
| 4 | 10,000 | 10,240.00 | 40,960.00 | 40,000 | 59,040.00 |
| 5 | 10,000 | 8,192.00 | 32,768.00 | 50,000 | 67,232.00 |
| 6 | 10,000 | 6,553.60 | 26,214.40 | 60,000 | 73,785.60 |
| 7 | 10,000 | 5,242.88 | 20,971.52 | 70,000 | 79,028.48 |
| 8 | 10,000 | 4,194.30 | 16,777.22 | 80,000 | 83,222.78 |
| 9 | 10,000 | 3,355.44 | 13,421.77 | 90,000 | 86,578.23 |
| 10 | 10,000 | 2,684.35 | 10,737.42 | 100,000 | 89,262.58 |

Reducing balance claims **$67,232 in five years against prime cost's
$50,000** — the deductions arrive earlier, which is worth more after
discounting. The trade: the annual RB deduction drops below prime cost from
year 5, and RB never quite reaches zero ($10,737 of WDV is still sitting
there after ten years).

### The building depreciation allowance — 2.5% over 40 years

For **income-producing buildings constructed after 20 September 1985**, on
the **construction cost only** (not the land, not the purchase price),
**straight-line**, using a depreciation schedule prepared for that building.
The rate has varied with construction date; in most cases it is **2.5%,
i.e. written off over 40 years**.

```
Construction cost (from the QS depreciation schedule)     12,000,000
Annual allowance      12,000,000 × 2.5%                  =   300,000  for 40 years
Check                 300,000 × 40                       = 12,000,000  ✓ fully written off
Cash value at 30%     300,000 × 30%                      =    90,000  a year
```

Now buy that building mid-life. Completed 1 July 2010, purchased 1 July
2026 for $20m (of which $8m is land):

```
Allowance base        construction cost 12,000,000 — NOT the 20,000,000 price, NOT the land
Years already claimed 2010 → 2026                         = 16 years × 300,000 = 4,800,000
Years remaining       40 − 16                             = 24 years × 300,000 = 7,200,000
Runs to               30 June 2050
```

> **The 40-year clock starts at completion, not at your purchase.** A buyer
> inherits whatever is left of the previous owners' schedule — which is why
> the lecture lists "maintenance of depreciation schedules and understanding
> of the financial history of the asset" and "the importance of
> apportionment on sales or transfer" as ownership implications. The
> contract's split of price between land, building and plant fixes the
> depreciable base for the next 24 years. Get the QS schedule at due
> diligence, not after settlement.

## 5.8 GST and Real Property

GST applies to all goods and services supplied in Australia for
consumption here (including imports). The principal exceptions the lecture
lists: food (except prepared food and meals), health, education, child
care, exports, water and sewerage, certain government dealings, inward duty
free shops, **sale of going-concern businesses**, religious items.
Operationally it runs on an **ABN** and a quarterly **BAS**.

### The property decision table

| Transaction | GST on the supply? | Input tax credits on the costs? | Slide basis |
|---|---|---|---|
| Sale of the family home / principal place of residence | **No** | **No** — GST on agents' fees, renovations, repairs is a real cost | "not subject to GST (but GST is applicable to input costs)" |
| Sale of land / **new** housing | **Yes** | Developer claims credits | "GST payable on land / new housing (First Home Buyers Scheme to compensate)" |
| **Residential rent** | **No** — GST is not added to the rent | **No** — input tax credits do not apply | "not to be added to residential rents and input tax credits do not apply" |
| Farmland farmed 5+ years, sold for farming | **No** — GST-free | — | "Land used for farming for at least five years and on-sold for farming is GST-exempt" |
| **Commercial property — purchase, leasing, costs, opex, repairs** | **Yes** | **Yes** — credits generally apply | "For commercial property, GST applied to purchase, leasing, costs… input tax credits will generally apply" |
| Sale of a tenanted commercial building as a **going concern** | **No** — GST-free if the conditions are met | Yes, as usual for the business | "Sale of going concern businesses" is a principal exception |

### Worked — the same $100,000 repair, two owners

```
Repair invoice        100,000 + GST 10,000 = 110,000

Commercial landlord   pays 110,000, claims 10,000 input tax credit on the BAS
                      → true cost 100,000

Residential landlord  pays 110,000, no input tax credit (residential rent is input-taxed)
                      → true cost 110,000
```

And on the income side, the commercial lease is GST-neutral for both
registered parties:

```
Net rent 500,000  →  invoice 500,000 + 50,000 GST = 550,000
Tenant pays 550,000, claims 50,000 back   →  tenant's cost   500,000
Landlord banks 550,000, remits 50,000     →  landlord keeps  500,000
```

> **Residential is where GST actually costs money.** Because residential
> rent is input-taxed, every GST-inclusive cost the residential landlord
> incurs is 10% dearer than the same cost in a commercial building. That
> compounds the lower yields that make residential the negative-gearing
> sector in §5.4. In an exam comparison of the two sectors, GST and
> negative gearing are the two tax points to make.

> **Going concern matters at the size of a building.** A $20m tenanted
> commercial sale is $2m of GST if taxable — cash the buyer must fund at
> settlement and wait to recover through the BAS. Sold as a going concern
> (both parties registered, leases in place, agreed in writing), there is
> no GST to fund. It is the reason IMs say "sold as a going concern" and a
> reason purchasers insist the tenancies survive settlement.

## 5.9 Implications for Asset Ownership

The lecture's list, with what each means for a property manager:

| Implication | In practice |
|---|---|
| Understand the impact of taxation provisions on **net return** | After-tax NI is what the owner keeps; classification (OPEX/CAPEX) and GST status move it |
| Understand the general parameters of the system — **and stay current** | Rates, thresholds and residential-vs-commercial rules change; the slide's own note on negative gearing is dated |
| The importance of **apportionment** on sales or transfer | Land / building / plant split fixes the depreciable base for 40 years |
| Maintain **depreciation schedules** and the asset's **financial history** | Inherited allowance, claimed allowance, cost base for CGT |
| **Maintain good records** — reasons, photos, reports, receipts | The evidence for calling something maintenance |
| The importance of **classification / allocation** of expenditure | 100% now vs 2.5% for 40 years |
| Effects on **prioritisation of works** | A deductible repair before year-end vs a capital replacement after — timing is a tax decision |

## 5.10 Asset Optimisation — the Queensland Framework

From the *Queensland State Infrastructure Strategy* (June 2022): the
Government prioritises investment in four tiers, in this order.

| Tier | Meaning | Lecture examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Reform** | Change how the asset is used or run without touching the fabric | Digital services — sensors, energy management systems |
| **Better use** | Re-purpose existing space | Wellness centre instead of archive storage at 1 Eagle Street · office → apartment / student accommodation |
| **Improve existing** | Physical upgrade | Refurbishment |
| **New** | Replace | Demolish and rebuild |

(The slide types "censor" for sensor.)

> **The order is the point — cheapest first.** Reform costs software; new
> costs a building. Read it against Topic 1's Quay Quarter Tower (an
> "improve existing" that kept 70% of the structure) and Topic 2's 360
> Queen Street (four tired buildings, "new"). A Part B SAM plan that jumps
> straight to demolition without showing the first three tiers were
> considered has skipped the framework the unit teaches.

## 5.11 Decision-Making Techniques

Five, each with the lecture's "issues / comments". Then the two that carry
formulas, worked.

### The comparison

| Technique | What it is | Use it when | Key weakness |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Cost benefit analysis (CBA)** | "Macro" technique: identify and quantify *all* costs and benefits — direct/indirect, tangible/intangible — regardless of who bears or enjoys them; discount to PV; compute a **benefit-to-cost ratio**; rank competing, often dissimilar, projects | Major public enterprises; ranking unlike projects against each other | Can you really measure everything in dollars? Which discount rate, which sunset, how far do you follow effects? Winners/losers compensation is a separate political question; open to statistical and data manipulation; complex, slow and expensive |
| **Feasibility study** | A formal, independent professional analysis of a proposed project to establish its **viability and risk areas** before the go/no-go decision. Structure: scope, methodology, assumptions, physical parameters, delivery method, cash flows / returns / sensitivities, programme, lease-up, risk, recommendations | New projects, acquisitions, refurbishments, refinancing. **USB245** does the DCF version | Who sponsored it, and who wrote it? Watch the assumptions, the depth of analysis, whether there is a market "reality check", and the quality of the risk profiling |
| **Life cycle costing (LCC)** | Mathematical model of the **total cost of an asset over its whole life**, not its initial cost — `LCC = PV[Ca + Cia + Co + Cm] − PV[R]`. Historically a QS method to amortise capital cost | Choosing plant and equipment; assessing the "stage" of an entire asset | Deals with **cost, not value** (effectiveness, function, obsolescence); needs reliable long-run data; "life" is ambiguous — physical? functional? tax? economic?; parallels the problems of summation valuation. **Not recommended as a single method** |
| **Value management (VM)** | Structured, facilitated process to deliver **all necessary functions at the lowest total cost** consistent with the real objectives. Rests on a platform of facts, continually asking "why", involving everyone, "all cost is for function", staged workshops — **divergent then convergent** | Strategic or project level; when assumptions need challenging | Only works if properly structured and facilitated; needs time and commitment |
| **Scenario planning** | Treat the asset / project as a **strategy**, not a single-outcome sequence of steps; keep several likely scenarios open as long as practical — pessimistic / most likely / optimistic — and design for flexibility (tenants, uses, configuration) | Complex ventures where "simple steps just don't occur"; the Part B scenario task | Must keep tight structure and discipline or it degenerates into "no planning, no decisions ever" |

### Cost benefit analysis — the ratio, and its trap

```
BCR = PV(benefits) / PV(costs)          NPV = PV(benefits) − PV(costs)

Project 1   PV benefits 12,400,000   PV costs 9,800,000   BCR 1.27   NPV 2,600,000
Project 2   PV benefits  4,200,000   PV costs 3,000,000   BCR 1.40   NPV 1,200,000
```

> **CBA ranks by ratio; the ratio and the NPV can disagree.** Project 2
> "wins" on BCR and delivers less than half the net benefit. The ratio is
> right for rationing a fixed budget across many small projects; the NPV is
> right when the question is which single project creates most value. The
> lecture's warning about "statistics and reality" is exactly this — say
> which measure you ranked on and why.

### Life cycle costing — worked

```
LCC = PV[Ca + Cia + Co + Cm] − PV[R]

Ca  = cost of acquisition          Co  = cost of operation (per year)
Cia = cost of installation and     Cm  = cost of maintenance (per year)
      adaptation                   R   = residual value at end of life
PV  = present value at the chosen discount rate over the chosen life
```

Two chillers, 15-year life, 6% discount rate. Annual costs are converted to
PV with the annuity factor; the residual with the single-sum factor:

```
Annuity factor  AF(6%, 15) = (1 − 1.06^−15) / 0.06 = 9.7122
PV factor       PVF(6%, 15) = 1.06^−15              = 0.41727
```

| | Chiller A | Chiller B |
|---|---:|---:|
| Ca acquisition | 400,000 | 550,000 |
| Cia installation | 50,000 | 60,000 |
| Co operation p.a. | 60,000 | 38,000 |
| Cm maintenance p.a. | 15,000 | 10,000 |
| R residual, year 15 | 40,000 | 60,000 |

```
Chiller A
  PV[Ca + Cia]      = 400,000 + 50,000                  =   450,000
  PV[Co + Cm]       = (60,000 + 15,000) × 9.7122        =   728,419
  PV[R]             = 40,000 × 0.41727                  =    16,691
  LCC               = 450,000 + 728,419 − 16,691        = 1,161,728

Chiller B
  PV[Ca + Cia]      = 550,000 + 60,000                  =   610,000
  PV[Co + Cm]       = (38,000 + 10,000) × 9.7122        =   466,188
  PV[R]             = 60,000 × 0.41727                  =    25,036
  LCC               = 610,000 + 466,188 − 25,036        = 1,051,152

B is cheaper over its life by 110,576 despite costing 160,000 more up front.
Simple payback of the extra capital: 160,000 / 27,000 p.a. saving = 5.9 years.
```

Sensitivity — the two things the lecture says LCC is fragile on:

| Change | LCC A | LCC B | Winner |
|---|---:|---:|---|
| Base: 6%, 15 years | 1,161,728 | 1,051,152 | B by 110,576 |
| Discount rate 10% | 1,010,880 | 960,728 | B by 50,152 |
| Discount rate 3% | 1,319,671 | 1,144,509 | B by 175,161 |
| Life cut to 8 years (6%) | 890,638 | 870,425 | B by 20,213 |
| Discount rate 16%+ | 863,842 | 871,146 | **A** |

> **LCC answers "which costs less", never "which is worth more".** Nothing
> in that table knows that Chiller B might hold a NABERS rating, keep a
> tenant, or be obsolete in year 9. A high discount rate or a short assumed
> life flips the answer, and both are judgement calls. That is why the
> lecture says LCC parallels summation valuation's problems and is *not
> recommended as a single decision-making methodology* — pair it with a
> feasibility study or VM.

### Value management — the process

Five phases; the first three **diverge** (open up options), the last two
**converge** (close on a decision):

| Phase | Questions asked |
|---|---|
| **Information** | Identify all assumptions · time / programme · environment · required outcomes · key issues |
| **Analysis** | What does it do (verb–noun)? What must it do to be successful? What does the function cost? What is it worth / value? Key performance / quality criteria? Functional hierarchy |
| **Creative** | Shared knowledge and experience · what else satisfies the function? · alternative costs? · is the function necessary? · "outfield" answers · can we eliminate, change, combine, relocate, add, simplify? |
| **Decision making** | Options that work within parameters, with manageable risk, with (as much as possible) flexibility, that meet required outcomes / functions · decide best · sign off, obtain approvals |
| **Implementation** | Action plan with milestones · announcement / advice · resources · reporting and review |

```
Information → Analysis → Creative   |   Decision making → Implementation
◄──────────── DIVERGENT ───────────►|◄──────── CONVERGENT ────────►
```

> **"All cost is for function" is the sentence to quote.** VM is not
> cost-cutting; it asks what each element is *for* (verb–noun: "cool air",
> "secure entry") and whether a cheaper thing does the same job. Cutting the
> function to save cost is the failure mode VM is designed to avoid.

### Scenario planning — the Part B connection

The lecture pairs it with feasibility studies: run the feasibility under
pessimistic, most-likely and optimistic scenarios, and keep options open
"whilst not procrastinating". Assessment 1 Part B's Task 3 is this
technique applied — three factors, actions that vary by column, and a
recommendation that changes across scenarios. See
[Part B §7.7](#/USB244/14-assessment-1-part-b-sam-plan).

## 5.12 Summary

- Australian property tax has shifted from **ownership to income and
  capital gains** and from the **States to the Commonwealth**; the stack is
  federal (income tax, CGT, GST), state (land tax, stamp duty), council
  (rates), plus incentives.
- **Taxable income = income − deductions.** Negative gearing is a tax
  *loss*; negative cash flow is a cash *shortfall*; depreciation is the
  non-cash wedge between them. The deduction returns only the marginal rate
  on each dollar lost.
- **CGT** applies to assets acquired from 20 September 1985 and is part of
  income tax, not a separate tax.
- **OPEX** (maintenance — part not whole, restoration, function, health) is
  100% deductible now; **CAPEX** (different material, improvement, added
  value, fewer future repairs) is deducted over time — plant by prime cost
  or reducing balance, buildings at **2.5% straight-line over 40 years on
  construction cost**, clock running from completion.
- **GST**: residential rent has no GST and no input credits; commercial is
  fully in the net with credits; going-concern sales are GST-free.
- **Asset optimisation** runs reform → better use → improve existing → new.
- Five decision tools: **CBA** (ratio, macro), **feasibility** (viability,
  DCF), **LCC** (`PV[Ca + Cia + Co + Cm] − PV[R]`, cost not value), **VM**
  (function, divergent → convergent), **scenario planning** (keep options
  open, design for flexibility).

## Checkpoint

1. A building earns $300,000 of rent, pays $250,000 of interest and $70,000
   of OPEX, and claims $50,000 of depreciation. Is it negatively geared? Is
   it cash-flow negative? After-tax cash at 30%?
2. A $6m building was completed on 1 July 2018. What is the annual building

6. Information → Analysis → Creative (**divergent**) | Decision making →
   Implementation (**convergent**). The line falls after the Creative
