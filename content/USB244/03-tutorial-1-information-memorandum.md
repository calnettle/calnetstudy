# TUTORIAL 1 — Reading an Information Memorandum

Week 2 tutorial. The skill being taught is reading an IM critically, because
Part A is built on one. Worked end to end on the tutorial's own example:
**Richlands Plaza, 511 Archerfield Rd, Richlands QLD**, marketed by JLL.

Every figure below was recomputed from the IM and reconciled. Where the
document disagrees with itself, the arithmetic is shown.

## 3.1 What an IM Is — and What It Is Not

> An IM is a **sales and marketing document** summarising the relevant property
> particulars and information that a prospective purchaser may need to know
> about a potential investment.

Read that definition twice. It is a **marketing** document produced by the
selling agent for the vendor. It is written to make the asset attractive.
Everything in it is true-ish and selected.

The IM's own disclaimer says so explicitly: it does not purport to contain
all the information a purchaser may require, figures may be based on
assumptions which may not be correct, information provided by third parties
has not been independently verified, and past performance is not a reliable
indicator of future performance.

> **This is the tutorial's real lesson.** Your Part A is a *historical
> analysis* — you are asked what the property manager actually did over three
> years. The IM tells you what the selling agent wants a buyer to believe. The
> gap between those two things is where your marks are. Quote the IM for
> facts (areas, income lines, lease terms) and interrogate it for claims
> ("exceptional", "monopolistic", "defensive").

## 3.2 The Standard IM Structure

| Section | What to extract |
|---|---|
| **Executive summary** | The headline facts — GLA, occupancy, WALE, net income |
| **Investment highlights** | What can you determine about the building? These are *claims*; test them |
| **Asset overview** | Site particulars, building services, building improvements |
| **Stacking plan** | Visualise tenants; the basis for calculating WALE by area |
| **Income summary** | Base rent, recoveries, percentage rent, sundry |
| **Outgoings** | Statutory and operating expenses — what are they, and can we influence them? How do we benchmark them? |
| **Centre / tenancy plan** | Tenant names, renewal types, areas |
| **Financial overview** | The full income and outgoings reconciliation |
| **Sale process** | EOI requirements, due diligence, the date that anchors your three-year look-back |

The tutorial activity asks one question of all of it:

> **What has the property manager done to keep the building in good condition
> for the past 3 to 5 years?**

Sections to mine for that: executive summary, investment highlights, major
occupier overview, asset overview.

Verification:

```
Base rent    925,000 + 914,886 + 19,706 + 118,976 + 475,668 = 2,454,236  ✓
Recoveries   105,873 +  75,802 +  9,391 +  74,035           =   265,101  ✓
Sundry        59,181 +   2,946                              =    62,127  ✓
GMI        2,454,236 + 265,101 + 10,441 + 62,127            = 2,791,905  ✓
Outgoings    147,349 + 345,032 + 111,400                    =   603,781  ✓
NPI        2,791,905 − 603,781                              = 2,188,124  ✓
NFLI       2,188,124 + 50,400                               = 2,238,524  ✓
```

> **The executive summary says $2,238,523; the financial summary computes
> $2,238,524.** A one-dollar rounding difference between two pages of the same
> document. It changes nothing, but noticing it is exactly the habit Part A
> rewards — and if you quote a figure, quote the one you can reconcile.

### Passing vs fully leased — do not confuse them

```
Net PASSING income      = 2,188,124   ← what the asset actually earns today
Net FULLY LEASED income = 2,238,524   ← passing + $50,400 of hypothetical rent
                                        on the 36 sqm vacancy (T13B)
```

> **"Fully leased" income includes rent nobody is paying.** The 36 sqm vacancy
> is assumed let at $50,400 pa — that is **$1,400/sqm**, well above what the
> small specialty tenancies around it achieve. A buyer capitalising the fully
> leased figure is paying today for income that may take a year to
> materialise, at a rate that may not be achievable. Always ask which income
> a quoted yield is struck on.

### Income concentration

```
Woolworths       1,030,873 / 2,791,905 = 36.9%
Specialties        990,688 / 2,791,905 = 35.5%
Business Centre    549,703 / 2,791,905 = 19.7%
Zarraffa's         138,808 / 2,791,905 =  5.0%
Sundry              62,127 / 2,791,905 =  2.2%
ATMs                19,706 / 2,791,905 =  0.7%
                                        ──────
                                        100.0%  ✓
```

Note the inversion against area: Woolworths is **56.8% of GLA but only 36.9%
of income**, while specialties are **22.6% of GLA and 35.5% of income**.

> **That inversion is the single most useful analytical fact in this IM.**
> Anchor tenants pay low rent per square metre in exchange for covenant
> strength and the foot traffic they generate; specialties pay much more per
> sqm and carry much more risk. The centre's income is far more dependent on
> its 12 small specialty tenants than the "70% Woolworths anchored" headline
> suggests. Nearly two thirds of income sits outside the anchor.

### The Woolworths GOCR — verify it

Gross Occupancy Cost Ratio = the tenant's gross occupancy cost as a
percentage of its sales.

```
GOCR = Woolworths gross rent / Woolworths MAT
     = 1,030,873 / 30,850,000
     = 3.34%          → IM states 3.3%  ✓
```

> **A 3.3% GOCR is very low, and that is a *good* sign.** Supermarket anchors
> typically sit around 2–4%; specialty retailers often 8–15%. A low ratio
> means the rent is easily affordable relative to the tenant's sales, so the
> tenant is unlikely to fail or to fight the renewal — which supports a
> tighter cap rate. It also means there is limited scope to push the anchor's
> rent, because the lease has 20 years to run with options to 2070.

## 3.5 Turnover, Productivity and Percentage Rent

| Tenant | MAT (ex GST, at Jun 2025) | Area | Stated productivity |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Woolworths | $30.85m | 2,808 sqm | $10,989/sqm |
| Zarraffa's Coffee | $2.50m | 102 sqm | $24,531/sqm |

```
30,850,000 / 2,808 = $10,986/sqm   (IM: $10,989)
 2,500,000 /   102 = $24,510/sqm   (IM: $24,531)
```

> **Small discrepancies, and they tell you the MATs are rounded.** Back-solving,
> $24,531/sqm × 102 sqm implies a Zarraffa's MAT of $2,502,162 — so "$2.50
> million" is a rounded presentation of a more precise figure. Same for
> Woolworths. Use the IM's productivity figures rather than recomputing from
> the rounded MATs, and say which you used.

**Zarraffa's pays percentage rent** — $10,441 on top of $118,976 base. That
is the only percentage-rent line in the centre, and it is the tenant with by
far the highest productivity. Percentage rent means the landlord shares
turnover upside, which is why the IM flags "Moving Annual Turnover linked
mechanisms" as part of the income growth profile.

## 3.6 Lease Expiry and WALE

- **WALE — income: 3.76 years. WALE — area: 4.02 years.**
- Lease expiry profile: **10% of GLA over the next 24 months** — the "expiry
  capture window" marked on the lease-expiry chart.
- The expiry chart shows a large cluster of initial expiries in **Year 5**
  (roughly 3,250 sqm of initial expiries plus renewals, over 3,500 sqm total).
- Woolworths holds an **exceptional 20-year initial lease term with options
  extending through to 2070**.

> **Two WALEs, and the gap between them is diagnostic.** Area-weighted WALE
> (4.02) exceeds income-weighted WALE (3.76). That means the *longer* leases
> are attached to the *lower-rent-per-sqm* space — i.e. Woolworths, with its
> huge area and long term, pulls the area WALE up but contributes less
> income. The shorter leases are the higher-rent specialties. Income is
> therefore rolling over faster than floor area is. Formula and full method
> in [Formula Sheet & Exam Traps](#/USB244/05-formula-sheet).
>
> Calculating WALE from a tenancy schedule is the **Week 3 tutorial** — this
> tutorial only asks you to find the figure in the IM and understand it. The
> full method at an EOI date, including every edge case the assessment FAQ
> decides (vacant space, holdover tenants, car parking, signage, options), is
> in [Assessment 1 Part A](#/USB244/06-assessment-1-part-a-guide).

## 3.7 Outgoings

```
Statutory expenses        147,349   (24.4% of outgoings)
Operating expenses        345,032   (57.1%)
Non-recoverable expenses  111,400   (18.5%)
                          ───────
                          603,781
```

Outgoings are **21.6%** of gross market income (603,781 / 2,791,905).

The tutorial asks two questions about these:

**"What are these and how can we impact?"**

- **Statutory** — council rates, land tax, water, insurance-adjacent statutory
  charges. Largely outside the manager's control; the Week 1 outgoings
  strategy of "lobby government for reduced statutory costs" is the only
  lever, plus appealing valuations.
- **Operating** — cleaning, security, HVAC maintenance, gardening, management
  fees. Highly manageable: tender contracts, negotiate long-term deals,
  install timers and meters, preventive maintenance.
- **Non-recoverable** — the portion the leases do not permit passing to
  tenants. At $111,400 this is 18.5% of outgoings and it comes straight off
  net income. Reducing it is a *leasing* problem — "structure lease terms for
  maximum recoverable" — not an operations problem.

**"How do we benchmark the outgoings?"** — the PCA benchmark, covered in the
Week 4 tutorial.

> **Non-recoverable outgoings are the line to attack in Part A.** Every dollar
> of the $111,400 is a dollar off NI, and at a 6% cap rate each dollar
> destroys **$16.67** of capital value (`1 / 0.06`). Cutting non-recoverables
> by $20,000 through better lease drafting at renewal is worth roughly
> **$333,000** of capital value — with no rent increase at all. Method for
> presenting this against the PCA benchmark is in
> [Assessment 1 Part A](#/USB244/06-assessment-1-part-a-guide).

The **asset overview** section covers building services — HVAC, gardening,
cleaning — and the tutorial asks how these impact. They are simultaneously
an operating cost, a recoverable (or not), and a driver of tenant
satisfaction and therefore retention and cap rate.

## 3.8 Replacement Cost — Read the Labels Carefully

The IM presents a replacement cost analysis:

| | $/sqm |
|---|---:|
| Entry level pricing | 7,479 |
| New build replacement cost — vacant | 13,116 |
| New build replacement cost — fully leased | 14,904 |

Applied to 4,947 sqm of GLA:

```
Entry level             7,479 × 4,947 = $36,998,613  (≈ $37.0m)
Replacement, vacant    13,116 × 4,947 = $64,884,852  (≈ $64.9m)  ✓ IM says "around $64 million"
Replacement, leased    14,904 × 4,947 = $73,730,088  (≈ $73.7m)
```

The IM labels two statistics **"50% — Replacement costs (fully leased) above
entry level pricing"** and **"43% — Replacement costs (Vacant) above entry
level pricing."** Test them:

```
Is vacant replacement 43% ABOVE entry level?
   13,116 / 7,479 = 1.7537  → it is 75.4% above.   ✗

Is entry level 43% BELOW vacant replacement?
   1 − (7,479 / 13,116) = 0.4298 → 43.0% below.    ✓

Is entry level 50% BELOW fully-leased replacement?
   1 − (7,479 / 14,904) = 0.4982 → 49.8% below.    ✓
```

> **The IM's labels are the wrong way round.** The percentages are **discounts
> from replacement cost**, not premiums over entry pricing. Vacant replacement
> cost is 75% *above* entry level, not 43%. Both readings describe the same
> two numbers, but "43% above" is simply not what 7,479 and 13,116 do.
>
> This is a genuine, checkable error in a real marketing document, and it is
> the best possible illustration of the tutorial's point: **do the arithmetic
> yourself.** If you cite this in Part A, say "entry level pricing sits 43%
> below vacant replacement cost" and show the working.

Implied yield on the entry-level figure:

```
2,238,524 / 36,998,613 = 6.05%
```

Context for the replacement argument: construction costs have risen **30%+**
over three years, with Brisbane facing some of the highest year-on-year
escalations nationally as contractors commit to the Government infrastructure
pipeline through to 2032.

> table, state your base year, and show the calculation.

## 3.10 What the Property Manager Actually Did

The tutorial's core question. Evidence available in this IM:

| Evidence | Where | What it shows |
|---|---|---|
| 99% occupancy (by GLA) | Investment highlights | Active leasing; only 36 sqm vacant out of 4,947 |
| "Exceptional tenant retention" | The Opportunity | Renewals negotiated rather than replacements found |
| Woolworths 20-year term, options to 2070 | Secure Woolworths covenant | Long-term income secured |
| GOCR of 3.3% | Executive summary | The anchor's rent is comfortably affordable — sustainable, not over-rented |
| Only 10% of GLA expiring in 24 months | Lease expiry profile | Expiry risk actively spread |
| Fixed rent reviews and MAT-linked mechanisms | Net income highlight | Growth built into the leases |
| Zarraffa's percentage rent of $10,441 | Financial summary | Turnover upside captured, not given away |
| Embedded electricity network earning $62,127 | Sundry income | Income diversified beyond rent — a Week 1 "diversify income sources" strategy, executed |
| TerryWhite Chemmart "NOW OPEN" signage | Photography | Recent leasing activity |

> **The embedded electricity network is the most underrated item.** $62,127 of
> sundry income capitalises at 6.05% to just over **$1 million** of capital
> value, created by metering arrangements rather than by leasing a single
> extra square metre. That is precisely the Week 1 gross-income strategy
> "diversify income sources — signage, licences, car parking and kiosk"
> turned into money.

Where the IM is **silent** — and silence is evidence too: no capital
expenditure history, no condition report, no arrears history, no detail on
what was spent on HVAC, roofing or car park resurfacing over the period. For
a historical analysis you would need the tenancy schedule and the outgoings
budgets, not just the IM.

**Q1. The IM headlines "70% Woolworths anchored" but the composition chart shows 56.8%. Which is wrong?**

Neither. 70% is Woolworths as a share of the **main centre** GLA
(2,808 / 4,031 = 69.7%); 56.8% is its share of **total** GLA including the
Business Centre (2,808 / 4,947 = 56.8%). The footnote on the cover says
"Main centre GLA percentage only". Always state the denominator.

**Q2. A buyer says "the yield is 6.05%". On which income figure, and why does it matter?**

On **Net Fully Leased Income** ($2,238,524), which includes $50,400 of
assumed rent on the vacant 36 sqm that nobody is currently paying. On **Net
Passing Income** ($2,188,124) the yield at the same price is
2,188,124 / 36,998,613 = **5.91%**. The fully leased figure flatters the
yield by 14 basis points and assumes a letting at $1,400/sqm.

**Q3. Woolworths occupies 56.8% of GLA but produces 36.9% of income. Is that a problem?**

It is a risk profile, not a problem. The anchor provides covenant strength,
foot traffic and a 20-year term at a low, sustainable rent (3.3% GOCR). But
it means **63.1% of income comes from outside the anchor** — mostly 12
specialty tenants and 6 business suites on much shorter leases. The centre's
income risk sits with the specialties, not with Woolworths, which is the
opposite of what the "Woolworths anchored" marketing implies.

**Q4. Verify the claim "Replacement costs (Vacant) 43% above entry level pricing."**

It is mislabelled. 13,116 / 7,479 = 1.754, so vacant replacement cost is
**75.4% above** entry level pricing. What is 43% is the *discount the other
way*: 1 − (7,479 / 13,116) = 43.0%, i.e. entry level pricing sits 43% **below**
vacant replacement cost. The 50% figure is the same relationship against
fully-leased replacement cost (49.8%).

**Q5. Non-recoverable outgoings are $111,400. At a 6.05% cap rate, what are they worth in capital value?**

```
111,400 / 0.0605 = $1,841,322
```

Eliminating them entirely — not realistic, but as a bound — would add roughly
$1.84m of capital value. It shows why lease drafting on recoverability is a
capital-value issue, not an administrative one.
