## 6.1 What a Green Building Is

> A sustainable building, or green building, is an outcome of a design
> philosophy which focuses on **increasing the efficiency of resource use**
> (energy, water and materials) while **reducing building impacts on human
> health and the environment** during the building's lifecycle — through
> better siting, design, construction, operation, maintenance and removal.

Two things in that definition matter for this unit. It covers the **whole
lifecycle**, not just construction — which is why there are two families of
rating tool (§6.7 vs §6.8). And it has two objectives — resource efficiency
*and* human health — which is where WELL (Topic 4) comes from.

The lecture's history: sustainability entered property with the **late-1970s
energy crisis** and its focus on pollution and renewable materials; the
green building movement in the UK, Europe and the US grew from the demand
for more energy-efficient, environmentally viable construction; it now
includes **retrofitting older buildings**, not just new design, and a
greater focus on the **lifecycle** rather than initial construction. Its
evolution has been led by **government, industry, research and education —
generally in that order**.

Europe and the UK run **Energy Performance Certificates (EPC)**: an A (very
efficient) to G (inefficient) rating required when a home is rented out or
sold (the slide cites an EU floor-area threshold of 250 m² from 7 July
2015), when a building under construction is finished, and when changes to
separately-occupied parts involve providing or extending fixed heating, air
conditioning or mechanical ventilation. It is the overseas template for the
Australian mandatory disclosure regime in §6.11.

## 6.2 Why All the Fuss — 40 / 40 / 20

Buildings account for a large amount of land use, energy and water
consumption, and air and atmosphere alteration. The three numbers to
memorise — buildings:

```
use   40%  of the total energy consumed
emit  40%  of the world's carbon emissions
use   20%  of the world's available drinking water
```

## 6.3 The Value Case

Energy efficiency and green design have become a **specific property class**
across sectors, and the lecture insists sustainability is now central to
"property development, management and valuation and not just a marketing
gimmick". The evidence it cites, from the *2021 World Green Building Trends*
report:

| Reported benefit | Global | Australia / NZ |
|---|---:|---:|
| Reduced operating costs, one year | 10.5% | 12.2% |
| Reduced operating costs, five years | up to 16.9% | 17.8% |
| Increase in building value, green vs non-green | up to 9.2% | 6.3% |

Plus higher ROI, higher occupancy, and productivity, well-being and health
gains for workers (the slide cites a *Conversation* article).

## 6.4 The Flip Side, and the Question

**What is the cost of *not* being a green-rated building?** Reduced tenant
demand and tenant type; higher operating costs; failure to maintain rental
growth. The issues the lecture raises: older properties; increasing the
star rating vs rebuilding (and the carbon footprint of rebuilding); green
buildings vs heritage; and *can all tenants or owners afford green leases
or buildings?*

Then the question the lecture says the valuer, owner and asset/property
manager all have to answer:

> Does a reduction of **66% in energy costs** actually result in a
> significant reduction in **total business operating costs**? What is the
> payback period? And therefore what is the actual impact on the **total
> business value (not just the building value)**, and what would the
> prudent space occupier pay?

Worked, from the tenant's side first:

```
Tenant's total business operating costs               10,000,000
Tenant's energy cost inside that                         150,000   (1.5%)
66% reduction                                              99,000   = 0.99% of business costs
```

A two-thirds cut in the energy bill is a **one per cent** cut in the
business's costs. That is the honest ceiling on what a rational tenant will
pay extra in rent for it — before the harder-to-price benefits (staff
attraction, CSR, productivity) that the lecture lists.

And from the owner's side:

```
Base-building energy upgrade                              600,000   capital cost
Owner's own base-building energy saving                    45,000   p.a.
Rent premium achieved   $20/sqm × 2,000 sqm                40,000   p.a.
Annual benefit                                             85,000
Simple payback          600,000 / 85,000                =  7.1 years
Capitalised at 6%       85,000 / 0.06                   = 1,416,667 of value
Net value created       1,416,667 − 600,000             =   816,667
```

> **The payback looks slow; the value uplift does not.** Seven years of
> payback is unattractive on a cash basis, but `CV = NI / CR` capitalises
> the $85,000 into $1.4m of value the day it is proven — and that is before
> the cap-rate effect of moving up a PCA grade. This is why owners fund
> upgrades tenants will not pay for directly, and why the green lease
> (Topic 4) exists to split the saving so both sides sign.

### The "15% per star" rule of thumb, applied

```
Office energy cost at 4 stars                    300,000
One-star improvement (4 → 5)     15%          →   45,000 saving

Two-star improvement (4 → 6):
   additive reading     30%                   →   90,000
   compounding reading  1 − 0.85² = 27.75%   →   83,250
```

> **The slide gives 15% per star with no compounding rule** — so a
> two-star question has two defensible answers ($90,000 or $83,250 here).
> State which reading you used. Under a **net lease** the tenant pockets
> the saving (energy is a recovered outgoing), which is exactly why the
> owner needs a green lease or a rent premium to see any of it.

## 6.5 Why Rating Tools Exist

The drivers: government regulation, corporate social responsibility,
market perception and demand, market acceptance, productivity and
efficiency. What a rating tool does:

- Establishes a **common set of criteria and targets**
- Sets a **standard of measurement** for green buildings
- Promotes **integrated, whole-building design**
- Recognises environmental leadership
- **Links the tools to government policies and regulations**
- Identifies building **life-cycle impacts**
- Raises awareness of the benefits
- **Improves property management** and the prioritisation of maintenance
  and operational needs

Globally there are dozens — the lecture shows the Bilos, Reed & Schulte
(2010) world map: LEED and Green Globes (North America), BREEAM (UK), HQE
(France), DGNB (Germany), CASBEE (Japan), HK-BEAM (Hong Kong), Green Mark
(Singapore), Green Star (Australia, also adopted in NZ and South Africa),
and many national Green Building Councils. The lecture's "which is
better?" questions:

- Does it cover commercial, industrial, retail, educational **and** health?
- Global, local **and** internal environments?
- New build **and** refurbishment?
- **Ongoing operation** and management, not just construction?
- Can the rating be used to **market** the building?
- Scale: **Green Star rates 1 to 6 stars; BREEAM runs Pass to Excellent**.
- Is regional variation appropriate for local environmental conditions?

## 6.7 Green Star

- Established by the **Green Building Council of Australia** — a national,
  not-for-profit organisation; launched **2002** on the slide (GBCA itself
  dates the first Green Star tool to 2003 — quote the deck in the exam).
- Supported by both industry and governments.
- Covers most sectors: office, industrial, public buildings, education,
  health care, apartments, residential development.
- **Four rating tools** available for certification:

| Tool | Scope |
|---|---|
| **Green Star – Communities** | Precinct planning and development |
| **Green Star – Design & As Built** | Building design and construction |
| **Green Star – Interiors** | Fitout design and construction |
| **Green Star – Performance** | Building operations and maintenance |

- Ratings are available in both **Design** and **As Built** form.
- The lecture's summary line: Green Star is **useful for CONSTRUCTION rather
  than OPERATION** — the Performance tool exists, but the brand's weight is
  in the design-and-build ratings.

### The nine categories

| Category | Aims to… |
|---|---|
| **Management** | Reward practices and processes supporting best-practice sustainability through design, construction and ongoing operation |
| **Indoor environment quality (IEQ)** | Enhance occupant comfort and well-being — air quality, thermal comfort, acoustic comfort |
| **Energy** | Reduce operational greenhouse emissions — demand reduction, use efficiency, generation from alternative sources |
| **Transport** | Reduce dependency on private car use; encourage alternative transport |
| **Water** | Reduce potable water consumption — efficient fixtures and systems, water re-use |
| **Materials** | Reduce resource consumption through low-impact material selection |
| **Land use and ecology** | Reduce harm to and enhance the site's ecological value |
| **Emissions** | Reduce "point source" pollution to atmosphere, watercourses and native animals |
| **Innovation** | Recognise innovative practices, processes and strategies (lecture example: 180 Ann Street, Brisbane) |

Lecture case: **West Village**, Brisbane — a $1 billion Sekisui House
project that earned **Six Star Green Star – Communities** (World Leadership
status, the highest rating for master-planned precincts in Australia), with
beehives on the Westpac building and 10,000+ plants under the *satoyama*
("ribbons of green") philosophy.

### The certified ratings ladder

| Stars | Label | Which tools certify at this level |
|---:|---|---|
| 1 | Minimum Practice | Performance only |
| 2 | Average Practice | Performance only |
| 3 | Good Practice | Performance only |
| 4 | **Best Practice** | Performance · Design & As Built · Interiors · Communities |
| 5 | **Australian Excellence** | All four |
| 6 | **World Leadership** | All four |

> **A Design & As Built certificate starts at 4 stars.** Only the
> Performance tool certifies 1–3 stars. So "a Green Star rated new
> building" means at least Best Practice — and the PCA benchmark table in
> Topic 2, which requires Green Star *Performance* 4 stars for Premium and
> 3 for Grade A, is talking about the operational tool, not the design one.
> Read the tool name, not just the star count.

## 6.8 NABERS

- **National Australian Built Environment Rating System**, previously the
  Australian Building Greenhouse Rating (ABGR). A national government
  programme administered by the NSW environment department as National
  Administrator; operating since **1998**.
- Rates **energy, water, waste and indoor environment**.
- Rates **apartment buildings, office buildings, office tenancies, shopping
  centres, data centres and hotels**.
- **Rates the OPERATIONAL sustainability of buildings** — the direct
  counterpart to Green Star's construction focus.

### How an office energy rating is calculated

- An **annual** rating of a building or a tenancy, **renewed annually**.
- Based on **verified operational data** — utility bills and on-site
  measurement: **12 months of real, measurable information** (energy and
  water bills, waste data).
- Compares the building's performance to **benchmarks representing other
  similar buildings in the same location**.
- Gives the market a **common language** to evaluate performance and set
  efficiency targets.
- **Commitment agreements** — a developer or owner agrees to design and
  build to a target rating before there is 12 months of data to rate.

### The seven principles

1. NABERS measures **actual impact, not intent**
2. NABERS is relevant to **building operations**
3. Ratings are meaningful
4. Ratings are simple and easy to perform
5. Ratings are reliable
6. Management is trustworthy
7. Development is collaborative

### The star scale

| Stars | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 | 5 | 6 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Label | Poor | Below Average | Average | Good | Excellent | **Market Leading** |

Current ratings are public: *nabers.gov.au/ratings/find-a-current-rating*,
with the full rating dataset downloadable. The FY25 distribution tables
the lecture shows, read from the slides and summed here:

|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| 6 stars | 8.4% | 6.6% | 17.7% | 13.7% |
| **5 stars or better** | **63.7%** | **56.9%** | 45.1% | 45.7% |
| 4.5 stars or better | 77.5% | 73.1% | 58.0% | 65.3% |
| 4 stars or better | 86.2% | 83.6% | 67.7% | 75.6% |
| 3 stars or worse | 9.6% | 11.2% | 16.0% | 15.4% |

```
QLD office, FY25:  6★ 8.4 + 5.5★ 24.7 + 5★ 30.6 = 63.7% at five stars or better
```

## 6.9 Green Star vs NABERS — the Comparison

| | **Green Star** | **NABERS** |
|---|---|---|
| Run by | GBCA — industry not-for-profit | Government — NSW-administered national programme |
| Since | 2002 (slide) | 1998 (as ABGR) |
| Measures | **Design and construction** (Performance tool aside) — intent and delivery | **Operation** — actual measured performance |
| Data | Design documentation, as-built verification | 12 months of bills and measurements |
| Frequency | One-off certification | **Annual**, must be renewed |
| Scale | 1–6 stars; Design & As Built certifies 4–6 only | 1–6 stars (half-stars used) |
| 6 stars means | World Leadership | Market Leading |
| 4 stars means | Best Practice | Good |
| Breadth | Nine categories (management, IEQ, energy, transport, water, materials, land use, emissions, innovation) | Energy, water, waste, indoor environment — rated separately |
| Used for | Marketing a new build or refurbishment; procurement specs | Disclosure at sale/lease (CBD); green-lease targets; PCA grading; outgoings management |
| In this unit | Topic 2 pipeline towers ("6-star Green Star"); Quay Quarter Tower (Topic 1) | PCA grade benchmarks (Topic 2); BEEC (§6.11); green-lease targets (Topic 4) |

> **"6 Star Green Star" tells you nothing about the current power bill.**
> It says the building was *designed and built* to World Leadership
> standard. Whether it is *operated* that way is a NABERS question, answered
> annually, from bills. A Part A manager review that credits the manager
> with the building's Green Star rating is crediting the developer; the
> manager's performance shows up in the NABERS trend.

## 6.10 EnviroDevelopment

A scientifically based assessment scheme that independently reviews
**development projects** and certifies those achieving outstanding
performance. Developed and operated by the **Urban Development Institute of
Australia (UDIA) Queensland since October 2006**, adopted nationally in
**2009**; initial certification with **annual re-certification**; national
technical standards for all sectors. Six key elements, each a leaf on the
logo:

| Element | Aim |
|---|---|
| **Ecosystems** | Protect and enhance natural ecosystems — habitat conservation, biodiversity, rehabilitation of degraded sites |
| **Waste** | Reduce landfill through responsible construction practice and long-term waste management prioritising reuse, recycling and resource recovery |
| **Energy** | Efficiency through smart design, efficient systems, renewable or non-polluting sources, reduced emissions |
| **Materials** | Recycled, reused and responsibly sourced materials, with lifecycle impacts considered |
| **Water** | Efficient use — rainwater harvesting, stormwater reuse, water-efficient fixtures |
| **Community** | Vibrant, inclusive, connected communities — access to facilities, diverse housing, reduced reliance on private vehicles |

## 6.11 Mandatory Disclosure

### Residential

- **Queensland Sustainability Declarations** — introduced as part of the
  sales process on **1 January 2010**, **ceased in August 2012**.
- The more recent initiatives: the national **Residential Efficiency
  Scorecard** and **NatHERS**.

### Commercial — the Building Energy Efficiency Disclosure Act 2010 (Cth)

The **Commercial Building Disclosure (CBD) Program** is a regulatory
programme requiring energy efficiency information to be provided, in most
cases, when **commercial office space of 1,000 square metres or more** is
offered for **sale, lease or sublease**. Its aim: improve the energy
efficiency of Australia's large office buildings and ensure prospective
buyers and tenants are informed. An initiative of COAG, established by the
BEED Act 2010 and managed by the Australian Government Department of
Industry.

```
WHO      most sellers and lessors of office space ≥ 1,000 sqm
MUST     obtain a Building Energy Efficiency Certificate (BEEC)
WHEN     BEFORE the space goes on the market for sale, lease or sublease
BEEC     valid 12 months, and includes
           • a NABERS Energy for offices star rating
           • a Tenancy Lighting Assessment of the relevant area
BY WHOM  only CBD-accredited assessors can apply for a BEEC on the owner's behalf
```

What the Act requires:

- BEECs must be **provided to potential buyers or lessees when requested**
  at the time of sale, lease or sublease — areas greater than 1,000 m².
- BEECs must be **publicly accessible** on the Building Energy Efficiency
  Register — *cbd.gov.au*.
- The building's **NABERS Energy star rating must be included in any
  advertising material** for the sale, lease or sublease.
- Lecture example: 111 Eagle Street, Brisbane — look it up under "Find a
  rated building" on cbd.gov.au.

NABERS supports the CBD programme by providing information and advice on
ratings and by **conducting audits** to ensure compliance with the BEED Act.

### Worked — who needs a BEEC?

| Transaction | BEEC? | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Sale of a 12,000 m² office tower | **Yes** | Office, ≥ 1,000 m², sale |
| Lease of a 950 m² office suite | **No** | Below the 1,000 m² threshold |
| Sublease of 1,200 m² by a head tenant | **Yes** | Sublease is captured; ≥ 1,000 m² |
| Sale of a 3,000 m² retail centre | **No** | The programme covers **office** space |
| Advertising a 2,000 m² office floor for lease | **Yes** — and the ad must show the NABERS Energy rating | Advertising requirement |
| Re-marketing a floor 14 months after the last BEEC | **New BEEC** | Valid 12 months only |

> **Disclosure is the enforcement arm of the rating system.** NABERS
> measures; the green lease commits; the CBD programme forces the number
> into the IM and the advertisement, where a buyer or tenant prices it. That
> is why the Australian survey respondents in §6.3 rank "reporting
> requirements" so far above the global average — and why an IM for a
> ≥1,000 m² office asset that does not quote a NABERS Energy rating is
> itself a finding for Part A.

## 6.12 Back to the Green Lease — and the Lease-Reading Checklist

Topic 4 §4.6 described the green lease: green provisions, consequences for
missing targets, and joint governance through an Environmental Management
Plan and Building Management Committee. This topic supplies what those
targets are made of:

| Green-lease element (Topic 4) | Rating-tool content (Topic 6) |
|---|---|
| The **target** | A NABERS Energy (and water/waste) star rating for the base building and, often, the tenancy |
| The **evidence** | 12 months of bills — the same data NABERS rates on; sub-metering makes tenancy ratings possible |
| The **consequence** clause | Triggered by a rating falling below the agreed stars; rent-review or cost-sharing mechanics |
| The **Building Management Committee** | The forum that reviews the annual NABERS result and the Environmental Management Plan |
| The **government tenant** | The Qld Green Lease Policy's target for leases over 2,000 m² is a NABERS-denominated target; government tenants also trigger the CBD disclosure as lessees |
| The **PCA grade** | NABERS Energy 5 / 4.5 / 4 / 3 for Premium / A / B / C — the rating the green lease protects is the rating the grade depends on |

> **A green lease is how a NABERS rating survives contact with a tenant.**
> The base building can be designed to 5.5 stars and still rate 4 if the
> tenancies run the plant after hours and refuse sub-metering. Green Star
> certifies the design; NABERS measures the outcome; the green lease is the
> only instrument that binds the party who determines the difference. In
> Part B terms, "green-lease every renewal" is a SAM action that defends
> rent, grade and cap rate at once — and now you can name the metric.

## 6.13 Summary

- A green building increases **resource efficiency** and reduces impacts on
  **health and environment** across the whole lifecycle; the movement dates
  from the 1970s energy crisis and has been led by government → industry →
  research → education.
- Buildings use **40%** of energy, emit **40%** of carbon, use **20%** of
  drinking water.
- Value case (WGBT 2021): operating costs −10.5% in year one (Aus/NZ 12.2%),
  −16.9% over five (17.8%); value +9.2% (Aus/NZ 6.3%); **15% energy saving
  per NABERS star**. Australians rank future-proofing and reporting far
  above the global average.
- **Green Star** (GBCA, 2002, 1–6 stars, four tools, nine categories) rates
  **design and construction**; Design & As Built certifies only 4–6.
  **NABERS** (government, 1998, 1–6 stars) rates **operation** from 12
  months of real data, renewed annually. **EnviroDevelopment** (UDIA, 2006)
  certifies developments on six elements.
- **Mandatory disclosure**: BEED Act 2010 (Cth) → CBD Program → **BEEC**
  before marketing office space **≥ 1,000 m²** for sale, lease or sublease;
  valid 12 months; NABERS Energy rating + Tenancy Lighting Assessment;
  rating in all advertising; public register at cbd.gov.au.
- The green lease (Topic 4) is the contract that turns a NABERS target into
  a shared obligation.

   **Performance** tool, which rates existing operations, certifies
   Minimum, Average and Good Practice (1–3 stars).
5. A **NABERS Energy for offices star rating**, a **Tenancy Lighting
   Assessment** of the relevant area, and it must be obtained by a
   **CBD-accredited assessor** before marketing; valid **12 months**; must
   be given to buyers or lessees on request and published on the register.
6. That it is a share **of rated buildings**, not of all buildings — rating
   is voluntary outside the CBD disclosure trigger, so poor performers are
   under-represented. Say "of NABERS-rated QLD offices in FY25".
7. Design fixes the building's *capacity* to perform; the tenancies
   determine whether it does — after-hours plant use, lighting, fitout
   loads and sub-metering are all in tenant hands. NABERS rates the
   *measured outcome* annually, so a 5.5-star design can rate 4 in
   operation. The green lease is the only instrument that binds the tenant
   to the target (green provisions), attaches a consequence to missing it,
   and creates the joint Building Management Committee that reviews the
   annual result — which is why Topic 4 called it the contractual arm of the
   ESG story.
