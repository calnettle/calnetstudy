## 3.1 The Role of the Shopping Centre Manager

Same three owner priorities as Topic 2's commercial property management
content — **income, risk, value** — but retail adds a fourth dimension that
office and industrial don't carry to the same degree: **the customer**. A
shopping centre manager curates a tenant mix and a physical experience for
people who have no contractual relationship with the landlord at all. Get the
mix or the experience wrong and the damage shows up in every tenant's
turnover before it shows up in the rent roll.

> Prompt from the lecture: *"Where do you shop? What works? What doesn't?
> What would you change?"* It's a deliberately unscored question, but it is
> the manager's actual job description — a shopping centre is evaluated by
> the people using it, continuously, whether anyone asks them or not.

## 3.2 History of the Australian Shopping Centre

**Allen & Stark Chermside, opened May 1957**, is described in the lecture as
the first modern shopping centre in Australia. A local newspaper at the time
called it *"an island of retailing in a lake of parking."*

> The pioneer developers and major retailers realised it was more sensible to
> **go into the suburbs, where their customers lived**, rather than expecting
> customers to keep coming to them in the CBD.

That thesis — retail follows rooftops, not the other way around — is the
seed of every classification in §3.3: centres are sized and typed by the
catchment population they are built to serve.

## 3.3 Shopping Centre Classifications

The PCA ladder, from smallest to largest. **GLA** here means the centre's
total gross lettable area across all tenants — not any one tenancy's area.

| Classification | GLA | Typical tenant mix | Examples |
|---|---|---|---|
| **City centre** | > 1,000 sqm | Arcade/mall within a CBD; dominated by specialty shops; generally **no supermarket**; often co-exists with department stores | — |
| **Neighbourhood** | < 10,000 sqm | A supermarket + ~35 specialty shops; local, extended trading hours, day-to-day needs | Warrigal Square, Greenslopes Shopping Mall |
| **Sub regional** | 10,000–30,000 sqm | At least one full-line discount department store, a major supermarket, ~40+ specialties | Inala Plaza, Kenmore Village |
| **Regional** | 30,000–50,000 sqm | One full-line department store, one discount department store, one or more supermarkets, ~100+ specialties | Westfield Helensvale, Strathpine Centre, Redbank Plaza, Brookside |
| **Major regional** | 50,000–85,000 sqm | At least one full-line department store, one or more discount department stores, one or more supermarkets, ~150+ specialties | Westfield North Lakes, Mt Ommaney, Orion Springfield |
| **Super regional** | > 85,000 sqm | Two full-line department stores, one or more discount department stores, two supermarkets, ~250+ specialties; one-stop shopping, entertainment/leisure | Indooroopilly SC, Westfield Carindale, Chermside, Garden City |
| **Bulky goods** | > 5,000 sqm | Dominated by bulky-goods retailers needing large display areas; small number of specialties; purpose-built around a landscaped central area | Aspley Homemaker City, Homemaker The Valley |

> **This ladder is a size-and-tenant-mix classification, not a quality
> ladder.** Topic 2's PCA office grades (Premium / A / B / C) rank *quality
> of the same building type*. The retail classifications instead rank *type
> of centre* — a Neighbourhood centre isn't a "lower grade" of a Super
> Regional one, it is a different kind of asset built for a different
> catchment. Don't try to map "grade" language from Topic 2 onto this table.

> **Not every property is a "shopping centre" under this ladder.** All seven
> tiers require a **supermarket or department store anchor**. A small ground-
> floor-retail-plus-upper-floor-office building — a strip of specialty shops
> and medical/allied-health suites with no supermarket — doesn't fit any row,
> no matter how many tenants it has. It's better described by the
> **configuration** in §3.4 (most often a strip centre) than forced into a
> classification it doesn't meet. [Tutorial 2](#/USB244/06-tutorial-2-auburn-quarter)
> works exactly this case.

> **Numerous ≠ dominant.** The count chart and the GLAR chart tell opposite

> Woolworths inverts between GLA share and income share (see

> GLAR — they support different conclusions.

## 3.4 Basic Shopping Centre Configurations

Three physical layouts, independent of the size classification in §3.3 — a
Neighbourhood centre and a Sub Regional centre can both be built as a strip.

| Configuration | Description |
|---|---|
| **Strip centre** | An attached row of stores managed as one retail entity, on-site parking usually **in front**. Open canopies may connect storefronts but there is **no enclosed walkway**. Laid out linear, "L"-shaped, or "U"-shaped. |
| **Mall** | Enclosed, climate-controlled walkway between two facing strips of stores. The most common design for Regional and Super Regional centres — informally, "mall" has become shorthand for that scale of centre. |
| **Cluster / racetrack / town centre** | A group of retail buildings separated by pedestrian walkways or streets, rather than one connected structure. |

> **The enclosed walkway is the strip-vs-mall test.** An open canopy over a
> row of shopfronts is still a strip centre; only a climate-controlled
> enclosed walkway makes it a mall. This distinction matters for a site
> visit — "mall" is not a generic word for "shopping centre" in this
> vocabulary.

## 3.5 PCA Method of Measurement — Gross Lettable Area Retail (GLAR)

From the PCA's *Method of Measurement: Commercial*. GLAR applies to shopping
centres, commercial buildings, and strip/free-standing/semi-detached or
terrace shops in suburban streets — a **third** measurement basis alongside
Topic 2's NLA (office) and GLA (industrial/large-format retail).

### The measurement boundaries

GLAR for each tenancy is measured in square metres:

- **from** the internal surface of the dominant portion of external walls,
  firewalls and service areas
- **to** the **centreline** of inter-tenancy walls
- **to** the **mall or shop line** — **regardless of whether the front is set
  back**
- **to** the **external surface** — but **only if** the front extends
  **beyond** the mall or shop line

| Include | Exclude |
|---|---|
| Window mullions, window frames | Areas set aside for services (ducts, a/c risers, fire hose reel cupboards, plant/motor rooms) |
| Structural columns or piers | Public spaces not for the exclusive use of tenants (toilets, tea rooms) |
| Engaged perimeter columns | Lift lobbies, lift shafts, stairs, escalators |
| Fire hose reels attached to a wall | |
| Additional facilities for individual tenants | |

Areas with **less than 1.5 metres clearance** above floor level (e.g. space
under stairs) are measured and **recorded separately**, not folded into the
main GLAR figure.

> **GLAR shares the "dominant portion" and centreline rules with Topic 2's
> NLA and GLA** — inside face of the dominant external wall, centreline of
> inter-tenancy walls, columns included. What's genuinely new is the **third
> boundary**: the **mall line**. NLA and GLA only ever measure to *a wall*.
> GLAR measures to a wall on three sides and to an imaginary line — the
> shopfront plane set by the centre — on the fourth.

### Worked example — the mall-line rule, both directions

A specialty tenancy has inter-tenancy walls 8.0 m apart (centreline to
centreline) and runs 12.0 m deep from the internal face of its rear external
wall to the centre's mall line.

**Case A — the shopfront is set back 1.5 m from the mall line** (a recessed
entry alcove, common where a tenant wants a display nook):

```
GLAR = 8.0 × 12.0 = 96.0 sqm
```

The recess is unbuilt space, but the rule measures **to the mall line
regardless of setback** — the alcove counts in the GLAR exactly as if the
shopfront sat flush with the line.

**Case B — the shopfront extends 0.8 m beyond the mall line** (a kiosk-style
protrusion into the mall):

```
GLAR = 8.0 × (12.0 + 0.8) = 8.0 × 12.8 = 102.4 sqm
```

Here the measurement plane moves to the **external surface** of the
protruding front, because the tenancy physically extends past the mall line
— so the extra 0.8 m adds real area rather than being excluded the way a
protruding column is excluded under GLA (§2.7 in Topic 2).

> **The trap is applying the GLA "exclude what protrudes" rule to GLAR.**
> They point opposite ways at the shop front. Under GLA, a column protruding
> past the external wall is *excluded*. Under GLAR, a shopfront extending
> past the mall line is *included*, because the mall line — not the building
> envelope — is the reference plane, and physically extending past it adds
> lettable area a customer can walk into.

## 3.6 The Changing Face of Retail

Two industry commentaries cited in the lecture, both UK/pandemic-era:

- Ashfords LLP (10 Jan 2019) — the **"death of the high street"**, and the
  push to create **"whole day" experiences** rather than pure transactional
  visits.
- Charlotte Hamlyn (15 Jul 2020) — bricks-and-mortar decline **accelerated by
  COVID-19**, threatened by online sales; **click and collect (concierge)**
  as the retail response.

A cited "future of retail" report projects five digital-transformation trends
"by 2025": subconscious commerce, elastic brands, multimedia storefronts,
circular retail, and evolved ecosystems (source: *wns.com*).

> **That trend list is dated to a target year that has now passed.** The
> lecture presents "by 2025" as a forward-looking projection, but today is
> **2026** — treat these as things to check against current retail practice
> rather than quote as upcoming. If you use this source in an assignment,
> say when it was written and that its target year has elapsed.

## 3.7 Key Design Considerations

- Provide retail services **conveniently, safely and enjoyably**, targeted to
  the catchment population — both major and specialty shops.
- The concept must be **inviting**, with a **logical, easily understood**
  layout.
- **Satisfy tenant requirements** and maintain **uniformity of style, concept
  and design** to promote identity.
- **Aesthetically appealing**, with good exposure, easy access, and
  accommodation for large volumes of customer parking.
- **Safe segregation** of pedestrians, customer vehicles and service
  vehicles.
- Compliance with **town planning, development and statutory requirements**.
- **Affordable costs** — within budget, though not necessarily maximum space
  at minimum cost.
- Meet the **specific requirements** of the owner, developer or anchor
  tenant(s).
- **Flexibility, adaptability, expansion potential.**

## 3.8 Development Issues

| Category | What to analyse |
|---|---|
| **Catchment area** | Primary, secondary and tertiary catchments; local economy; employment |
| **Competition** | Existing and proposed centres; new-build and refurbishment activity; over/under capacity |
| **Demographics** | Population number and growth, age profile, family structures, disposable income, vehicle ownership, racial groups, education/employment type, and **foreseeable changes** to any of these |
| **Site characteristics** | Slope/topography, zoning, road network and traffic direction, access/egress (multiple points), visibility, public transport, size |
| **Design** | Opportunity to theme, general layout (worth the time to get right), ratio of major to specialty tenancies, car parking configuration (underground/multilevel/on-grade/covered/open), pedestrian flow, access, community use |

> **The catchment tiers matter for the classification table in §3.3.** A
> Neighbourhood centre is sized for its **primary** catchment only; a Super
> Regional centre is built to draw from all three tiers. Getting the
> catchment analysis wrong doesn't just misjudge demand — it argues for the
> wrong classification and the wrong tenant mix entirely.

## 3.10 Topic 3 Summary

- The Australian shopping centre traces to **Allen & Stark Chermside
  (1957)** — retail follows the suburbs, not the CBD.
- The **PCA classification ladder** (City Centre → Neighbourhood → Sub
  Regional → Regional → Major Regional → Super Regional → Bulky Goods) sorts
  centres by **GLA and tenant mix**, not by quality — every tier from
  Neighbourhood up requires a **supermarket or department-store anchor**.
  Properties without one don't fit the ladder at all.
- Three physical **configurations** — strip, mall, cluster/racetrack — cut
  across the size classification.
- **GLAR** is the retail measurement basis: same dominant-portion and
  centreline rules as NLA/GLA, plus a third boundary unique to retail — the
  **mall line**, measured to regardless of setback, but to the external
  surface if the shopfront extends past it.
- QLD retail stock is **Neighbourhood-heavy by centre count but not by
  GLAR** — small centres are numerous, large ones hold the floor area.
- Retail's structural risk sits with **discretionary spend**, which is why
  Topic 2 named retail as the sector hit first when consumer spending falls.

<summary>✅ Checkpoint — retail classification and GLAR</summary>

**Q1. A centre has 42,000 sqm GLA, one full-line department store, one
discount department store, two supermarkets and 165 specialty shops. Which
classification?**

**Regional.** GLA of 42,000 sqm falls inside the **Regional** band
(30,000–50,000 sqm) — it is too small to be Major Regional, which needs
50,000–85,000 sqm. The tenant mix here (two supermarkets, 165 specialties)
is actually richer than a typical Regional centre's minimum profile (one
department store, one discount department store, one or more supermarkets,
~100+ specialties), but **GLA is the binding constraint**: no matter how
strong the tenant mix, 42,000 sqm cannot classify as Major Regional. Check
GLA against the band first, then confirm the tenant mix is at least
consistent with it.

**Q2. A tenancy's shopfront sits flush with the mall line on its left half
and is recessed 2 m on its right half (an angled glass entry). How do you
measure the front boundary?**

To the **mall line**, for the whole tenancy — GLAR measures to the mall or
shop line "regardless of whether the front is set back". The recess doesn't
create a partial exclusion; the boundary is the line, not the built
shopfront.

**Q3. Why doesn't a two-storey building with ground-floor specialty shops and
upper-floor medical suites, no supermarket, necessarily fit any row of the
PCA classification table?**

Because every tier from Neighbourhood upward requires a **supermarket or
department-store anchor**, and the table classifies *shopping centres*
specifically. A mixed retail/office building without that anchor is better
described by its physical **configuration** (most likely a strip centre) —
forcing it into "Neighbourhood centre" because it has some retail tenants
misapplies the table.

**Q4. Distribution-by-centre-type and GLAR-by-centre-type charts for the same
state show Neighbourhood centres dominant in the first and much less
prominent in the second. Is that a contradiction?**

No — they measure different things. The count chart shows **how many**
centres are Neighbourhood-format; the GLAR chart shows **how much floor
area** sits in that format. Neighbourhood centres are individually small, so
a state can have far more of them than any other type while they still
occupy a modest share of total retail floor area, because a handful of
Regional/Major Regional/Super Regional centres carry disproportionate GLAR.
