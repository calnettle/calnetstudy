## 4.1 What a Lease Is — and Lease vs Licence

A lease is a contract granting **exclusive possession** of defined premises
for a defined term in exchange for rent. That exclusivity is what separates
it from a **licence**, which grants permission to occupy or use without
exclusive possession — and the distinction has run through this unit already:

| | Lease | Licence |
|---|---|---|
| Exclusive possession | Yes | No |
| Typical term | Years, with options | Days to years, often terminable |
| Survives sale of the building | Yes (registered / protected) | Generally no |
| WALE treatment | In both WALEs | Only if a formal fixed-term licence (e.g. the car park rule) |
| Examples from this unit | Woolworths at Kallangur, every Auburn Quarter tenancy | Casual mall leasing, ATM sites, most car parking |

Why leases exist at all: the tenant gets a secured platform for their
business; the owner gets a secured income stream that a valuer will
capitalise and a financier will lend against. *Buildings without tenants
generate little revenue* — the lease is the mechanism that converts space
into income.

## 4.2 Terminology — the Glossary

The lecture lists two slides of terms. Most have already appeared in these
notes; this table is the one place they are all defined.

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **NLA / GFA** | Net lettable area (PCA-measured, what rent is paid on) vs gross floor area (the whole envelope, a planning measure). Never mix them in $/sqm |
| **Net rent / gross rent** | Net: tenant pays rent *plus* outgoings. Gross: outgoings are inside the rent. Same building, very different headline $/sqm |
| **Face (stated) rent / effective rent** | The rent on the lease document vs the rent after incentives are netted off — worked in §4.5 |
| **Fixed / market rent review** | Predetermined increase vs reset to market evidence — §4.4 |
| **Lease / licence** | §4.1 |
| **Incentive** | Rent-free, rent abatement, fitout contribution or cash paid to secure a tenant |
| **Tenant = lessee · Landlord = lessor** | Same parties, statutory language |
| **Headlease / sublease** | The head tenant holds the lease from the owner and grants a sublease to an occupier; the head tenant remains liable |
| **Profit rent** | What a head tenant earns when sublease income exceeds their head rent: sublet at $360,000 against head rent of $300,000 → **$60,000** profit rent |
| **Rental shortfall** | The reverse — passing rent below market (or sublease income below head rent); a purchaser prices the gap |
| **Permitted use** | The clause limiting what business may operate — the mix-control lever in a shopping centre |
| **Notice of attornment** | The written notice given to every tenant at settlement when a tenanted building sells: the property has a new owner, and rent under the **existing** lease is now payable to that owner (or their agent). The lease itself continues unchanged — see §4.3 |
| **Recoverables / non-recoverables** | Outgoings the lease lets the owner pass on vs those it doesn't. Every non-recoverable dollar is a dollar off NI — $16.67 of value at a 6% cap rate |
| **Ratchet clause** | Prevents rent falling at review — §4.4 |
| **Cap and collar** | Bounds a review between a floor and a ceiling — §4.4 |
| **Percentage sales / turnover rent** | Rent linked to the tenant's sales above a breakpoint — §4.4 |
| **HVAC** | Heating, ventilation and air-conditioning — the biggest operating cost line and after-hours charging clause |
| **Electricity profit** | On-selling margin from an embedded network — Richlands earns $62,127 a year this way |
| **FSR** | Floor space ratio (the planning density measure) — the lecture lists it without definition; confirm the intended sense in class |
| **Workspace ratio** | Sqm of NLA per workpoint — drives office tenant demand for space |

## 4.3 Anatomy of a Commercial Lease

The lecture's six slides of "lease characteristics" are, read together, a
**due-diligence checklist** — the list a property manager (or a Part A
student with a tenancy schedule) interrogates a lease against.

| Group | What to find | Why the manager cares |
|---|---|---|
| **Parties & premises** | Lessor, lessee, the demised area ("…m² as identified on the plan"), signatures | Who is actually liable — trading entity vs holding company vs personal guarantee |
| **Term** | Commencement, term, expiry | Feeds straight into the WALE and the critical-date diary |
| **Options** | Period, conditions, **mechanism and timing** for exercise | A missed option-notice window ends a tenancy or locks one in — pure lease-administration risk |
| **Rent & reviews** | Initial rent; review timing, mechanism, minimums, time limits, arbitration; date and effect of last review | §4.4 — this is the income growth engine |
| **Outgoings** | How defined, who is responsible, payment mechanism | Decides recoverable vs non-recoverable — the effect lands directly on the income stream |
| **Use & alterations** | Permitted use, restrictions; ability to alter, fit out, affix signs; ownership of fixtures | Mix control; make-good disputes start here |
| **Maintenance & make-good** | Periodic obligations (clean, re-paint, re-carpet); responsibility for repair or **replacement of capital equipment** | Whether a $400k chiller replacement is the owner's or tenant's problem is a lease clause |
| **Parking & access** | Car parking (exclusive use often by **separate licence**), visitor parking, access/egress, joint facilities | The separate-licence structure is exactly the WALE car-park edge case |
| **Default & security** | Late-payment provisions, default, personal guarantees, security (bond/bank guarantee) | Arrears management runs on these clauses |
| **Demolition / relocation clause** | Owner's right to terminate for redevelopment | Kills income security — see the trap below |
| **Registration & duty** | Registered or registrable? Stamp duty paid? Protection on change of freehold ownership | An unregistered lease can leave the lessee exposed when the building sells |
| **Options to purchase / first right of refusal** | Any pre-emptive rights over the freehold | Constrains the owner's exit — a purchaser will price it |

> **A demolition clause is a WALE with an asterisk.** A ten-year lease the
> owner can terminate on twelve months' notice for redevelopment is not ten
> years of secure income, and a purchaser or valuer will not price it as
> ten. If a tenancy schedule shows a long WALE on a site marketed for its
> "development upside" — Kallangur's mixed-use zoning pitch, for instance —
> the first question is whether the leases contain demolition clauses. The
> IM won't volunteer it.

> **Registration matters at sale.** If a lease is unregistered and contains
> no protection on a change of freehold ownership, the lessee's position on
> sale depends on statute and notice. For the exam: registration (or a
> registrable lease with protective clauses) is what secures the lessee's
> interest against an incoming owner.

### When the building sells — the notice of attornment

An investment property is sold **subject to its tenancies** — the buyer is
buying the income stream, so the leases survive settlement. What changes is
who the rent is owed to, and the document that effects the change is the
**notice of attornment**.

```
WHAT   Written notice to each tenant, issued at (or immediately after)
       settlement, typically by the outgoing owner or jointly with the
       incoming owner
SAYS   1. The property has been sold — here is the new lessor
       2. From the settlement date, pay rent and all other money due
          under your lease to the new owner / their managing agent
       3. Your lease otherwise continues on the same terms
```

"Attornment" is the tenant acknowledging the new owner as their landlord
under the **existing** lease. Nothing is re-signed and no new lease is
granted — the landlord's rights and obligations pass with the ownership
(the covenants "run with the reversion"), and the tenancy simply carries
on. The notice also protects the tenant: once received, paying the new
owner is a good discharge of the rent obligation, and the tenant is no
longer safe paying the old one.

For the incoming property manager, the notice is the first item on a
settlement-day checklist:

| Settlement task | Why it matters |
|---|---|
| Issue notices of attornment to every tenant | Redirects the income stream from day one — a tenant who pays the old owner creates an arrears problem that isn't the tenant's fault |
| Reissue / replace **bank guarantees** | A guarantee names the outgoing owner as beneficiary — it does **not** follow the sale automatically; until replaced, the new owner holds no security |
| Transfer cash bonds and security deposits | Adjusted or handed over at settlement |
| Settlement adjustments | Rent and outgoings paid in advance (or in arrears) are apportioned between the two owners at the settlement date |
| Take over the critical-date diary | Reviews, options and expiries don't pause for a sale — a review missed during handover is permanent income loss |

> **Attornment is the landlord changing; assignment is the tenant changing.**
> The two get confused in exams. An **assignment** transfers the *lease* from
> one tenant to another (with the lessor's consent). **Attornment** happens
> when the *freehold* changes hands: same lease, same tenant, new landlord.
> Neither one creates a new lease — which is why the WALE, the review dates
> and every other lease term are unaffected by the sale.

## 4.4 Rent Reviews — the Income Growth Engine

Five base mechanisms:

| Mechanism | How it moves | Who bears the risk |
|---|---|---|
| **No increase during term** | Flat — real income falls with inflation | Owner |
| **Fixed increases** | e.g. 3.5% p.a., certain | Shared — tenant certain, owner gives up upside |
| **Percentage increases** | Stated % steps | As fixed |
| **CPI** | Tracks inflation | Owner protected in real terms; tenant wears inflation spikes |
| **Market review** | Reset to market evidence, usually at option or mid-term | Both — genuine repricing, plus dispute/arbitration risk |

Kallangur Fair's specialty book — CPI or fixed 3.0–5.0%, weighted average
3.7% — is a textbook fixed/CPI structure: certain, compounding, and
marketable as a "growing, long-dated income stream".

### The modifiers

Individual leases then bolt on:

- **Ratchet** — rent cannot fall at review.
- **"Either-or"** — the higher (or lower) of two mechanisms, e.g. higher of
  CPI or 3% fixed.
- **Floor** / **minimum increase** — a lower bound on the outcome.
- **Cap and collar** — both bounds at once.

Worked, on a $400,000 passing rent:

```
CPI review with a 2% collar and 4% cap
   CPI = 1.1%  →  collar binds  →  2.0%  →  $408,000
   CPI = 3.2%  →  neither binds →  3.2%  →  $412,800
   CPI = 6.8%  →  cap binds    →  4.0%  →  $416,000

Market review, market has fallen 8%
   no ratchet   →  $368,000
   ratchet      →  $400,000 (held at passing)

"Either-or": higher of CPI (3.2%) or fixed 3.0%
                →  3.2%  →  $412,800
```

> **Ratchets and retail legislation.** Retail shop lease legislation
> restricts ratchet provisions in retail tenancies — in Queensland, under
> the Retail Shop Leases Act, a clause preventing a market review from
> decreasing the rent is generally of no effect for a retail shop lease.
> Commercial (non-retail) leases are not so constrained. Check the current
> Act before relying on this in an answer — but the distinction itself
> ("ratchet fine in office, restricted in retail") is exam material.

### Percentage (turnover) rent

Retail only. Rent = base rent plus a percentage of the tenant's sales above
a **natural breakpoint** — the turnover at which percentage rent begins,
equal to base rent ÷ the percentage rate:

```
Base rent $80,000 · rate 5%
Natural breakpoint = 80,000 / 0.05        = $1,600,000
Turnover (MAT)                            = $2,100,000
Percentage rent    = 5% × (2,100,000 − 1,600,000) = $25,000
Total rent                                = $105,000
```

This is Zarraffa's at Richlands Plaza ($10,441 of percentage rent) — and it
is why a manager who grows centre trade grows income *automatically* on
turnover-rent leases but only *at the next review* on fixed ones.

## 4.5 Face Rent vs Effective Rent

The incentive machinery from Topic 2, now with the lease terminology
attached. Face (stated) rent is what the lease says; effective rent is what
the deal actually earns once incentives are netted off:

```
Effective rent (straight-line, ignoring discounting)
  = (total face rent over term − total incentives) / term

1,000 sqm · face $500/sqm net · 5-year term
Incentive: 6 months rent-free + $150,000 fitout contribution

Total face rent   500 × 1,000 × 5           = 2,500,000
Rent-free         0.5 × 500,000             =   250,000
Incentive total   250,000 + 150,000         =   400,000   = 16% incentive
Effective rent    (2,500,000 − 400,000) / 5 =   420,000 p.a.
                                            =   $420/sqm  vs  $500 face
```

> **Face rent survives on the tenancy schedule; the incentive usually
> doesn't.** Owners protect face rents because valuers capitalise them; the
> incentive is a one-off cost that vanishes from the passing-income story.
> Auburn Quarter's schedule shows the mechanism mid-flight: a live rent-free
> line sitting inside an otherwise fully-let schedule. When comparing two
> buildings' rents, always ask which one you are seeing.

## 4.6 Green Leases

A green lease embeds environmental performance in the tenancy contract: a
framework for **both** parties to achieve and maintain energy, water and
waste efficiencies and other sustainability goals through the term, with
each party recognising its obligations.

### What changes against a standard lease

1. **Green provisions** — in the lease itself or an attached schedule.
2. **Consequences** if either party fails to meet the agreed targets.
3. **Governance** — an Environmental Management Plan and a joint Building
   Management Committee.

That third item is the structural novelty: a standard lease is adversarial
by design; a green lease creates a standing owner–tenant body with shared
targets.

### Why each side signs one

| Tenant benefits | Landlord benefits |
|---|---|
| Direct and indirect cost savings — lower outgoings | Operating cost savings **+ higher rent = higher value building** (`CV = NI / CR` again) |
| Corporate social responsibility, employee satisfaction | Higher tenant retention → less vacancy |
| Safer, healthier workplace — air quality | CSR and tenant calibre |

### The government layer

Named in the lecture (refer to the QUT readings for the detail):

- National Green Leasing Policy
- Qld Government Sustainable Office Building Rating Policy
- Qld Government Green Lease Policy — the slide cites a **90% target for
  leases over 2,000 sqm**
- Qld Government Strategic Asset Management Framework

Government is the tenant that moves markets: when the state mandates green
leases for its own occupancies above a size threshold, owners of stock that
can't meet the ratings lose an entire tenant class.

### Where it's heading

- Environmental and social goalposts keep shifting **upward**.
- **Secondary-grade stock must be refurbished to stay competitive** — the
  same flight-to-quality pressure as the PCA grade benchmarks in Topic 2,
  arriving through the lease rather than the grading guide.
- **WELL certification is the next evolution**: Green Star and NABERS rate
  the *building*; WELL rates the experience of the *people inside it*.

> **Green leases are the contractual arm of the ESG story.** Topic 2 gave
> the ratings (NABERS, Green Star) that set a building's grade; the green
> lease is how those ratings survive contact with an actual tenant — the
> building can't hold a NABERS Energy rating if the tenancies run against
> it. In Part B terms: a green-lease programme is a concrete SAM action that
> defends grade, rent and cap rate at once.

## 4.7 Summary

- A lease grants **exclusive possession**; a licence doesn't — and the
  distinction decides WALE treatment.
- The lease characteristics list is a **due-diligence checklist**: term and
  options feed the WALE; outgoings clauses decide recoverability; make-good,
  default, demolition and registration clauses allocate the risks.
- When the building sells, the leases survive: the **notice of attornment**
  tells each tenant to pay the new owner under the existing lease, and the
  bank guarantees must be reissued because they don't follow automatically.
- Rent reviews are the income growth engine: fixed/CPI for certainty, market
  for repricing, modified by ratchets, floors, caps and collars — each
  modifier shifts risk between owner and tenant.
- **Face ≠ effective**: incentives convert headline rent into a lower real
  rent; 16% incentive turned $500 into $420 in the worked example.
- Percentage rent pays the manager for growing trade; the natural breakpoint
  is base rent ÷ rate.
- Green leases contractualise sustainability — provisions plus consequences
  plus joint governance — pushed by government occupancy policy, heading
  toward WELL.

6. Why does a market review with a ratchet behave differently in a Brisbane
