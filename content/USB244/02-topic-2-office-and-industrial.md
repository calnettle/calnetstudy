Week 2. Risk management, then the two asset classes: commercial (office)
and industrial. The PCA measurement rules in here are the most examinable

## 2.1 Risk and Risk Management

> Risk is the chance of unforeseen events or outcomes, or those different
> from predictions or established plans or strategies.

Two things follow from that definition, and both are examinable:

- **Variance may have a positive or negative effect** on overall outcomes.
  Risk is not the same as loss. Upside variance is still risk.
- Risk may be generated by forces **internal** to the property
  (**non-systemic**) or **external** to the property (**systemic**).

> **Get the pairing the right way round.** Internal → non-systemic.
> External → systemic. Systemic risk is the market-wide kind you cannot
> diversify away by picking a better building; non-systemic risk is
> property-specific and *is* manageable. That is why the manager's effort
> concentrates on the non-systemic side.

## 2.2 The Risk Management Matrix

Likelihood (probability) on one axis, consequences (impact) on the other.

| | **Low impact** | **High impact** |
|---|---|---|
| **High probability** | Adjust systems / activities to lower probability (particularly in maintenance and tenancy agreements) · benchmark · look for low-cost systems solutions | **Specific risk management plan** · major strategy changes, if necessary |
| **Low probability** | Maintenance management / response · systems / records · looking for trends | **Insurance** · lower risk profile in specific areas |

The slide asks: *where should we concentrate efforts?*

> **The answer is the high-probability / high-impact quadrant — and the reason
> matters.** The other three quadrants have cheap generic answers: systems and
> benchmarking for frequent-but-minor, records and trend-watching for
> rare-and-minor, and **insurance** for rare-but-severe. Only the top-right
> cannot be insured away or systematised away, because it will probably
> happen *and* it will hurt. That is where a specific plan and, if needed, a
> change of strategy is required.
>
> Note where insurance sits: **low probability, high impact**. Insuring a
> high-probability event is either unavailable or priced at the cost of the
> event. If a question offers "insure it" for a top-right risk, that is the
> distractor.

## 2.3 Evolution of the Office Building

Technology has driven office form throughout its history. Worth knowing as
context for why B and C grade stock struggles.

| Period | Development |
|---|---|
| 1860s–1870s | Steel frame office structure; use of the elevator (invented 1854) |
| 1873/74 | Typewriter |
| 1876 | Telephone |
| 1930s | Fluorescent lighting; window air conditioner |
| 1950s | Centralised a/c and suspended ceiling |
| 1970s | Energy crisis → insulation technology, sealed buildings, lower artificial light levels, building automation to cut energy costs |
| 1980s | PCs, one per person; networks, cable management; office automation standard |
| 1990s | Portable technology — mobile phones, laptops, flexible work |
| 2000s | Green buildings, virtual offices |

The pattern: each wave of technology raised the servicing standard, and
buildings that could not absorb it fell down the grade ladder.

## 2.4 Contemporary Issues

- **Non-conforming building products** (Queensland Building Safety, QBCC) —
  cladding fire risk, exemplified by the Melbourne Lacrosse building (2014)
  and the Neo200 fire.
- **Mandatory fire safety systems** in commercial and accommodation buildings
  — fire alarms, sprinkler systems, evacuation requirements.
- **Climate change** — coastal erosion; building cracks and foundation
  failure (Opal Tower, Mascot Tower, Zetland).
- **COVID-19** — worker safety and health, physical distancing, hygiene,
  health checks; and questions about building design itself.
- **Miami building collapse (24 June 2021)** — a 2018 structural survey had
  found abundant cracking of columns, beams and walls in the below-ground
  parking area; the collapse began at the base through column failure.

> **The Miami case is the one to cite for "why inspection reports matter."**
> The defects were documented three years before the collapse. It is a
> failure of acting on information, not of obtaining it — which maps directly
> onto the guest lecture's "problems don't shrink by waiting."

|---|---|

### Size benchmarks — new buildings

New buildings get their own, tighter table, and **Brisbane and Perth are
named explicitly** rather than lumped into "other CBDs":

| Code | Parameter | Premium | Grade A | Grade B |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| B1 | Building size (sqm NLA) | Syd/Melb > 30,000; **Bris/Perth > 25,000**; other > 20,000 | Syd/Melb > 10,000; **Bris/Perth > 10,000**; other > 5,000 | Syd/Melb > 5,000; **Bris/Perth > 5,000**; other > 2,500 |
| B2 | Floor plate (sqm NLA) | Syd/Melb/**Bris/Perth** > 1,500; other > 1,200 | Syd/Melb/**Bris/Perth** > 1,000; other > 800 | Syd/Melb/**Bris/Perth** > 700; other > 600 |

> **Existing and new buildings use different thresholds — and Brisbane moves.**
> Under the *existing* table Brisbane falls in "other CBDs", so Premium needs
> only >20,000 sqm. Under the *new* table Brisbane is named separately and
> Premium needs >25,000 sqm, with a 1,500 sqm floor plate rather than 1,000.
> A new Brisbane building is held to a materially higher standard than an
> existing one of the same grade. Using the wrong table is an easy way to
> mis-grade a building in Part A.

### Management personnel (H1)

| Premium | Grade A | Grade B |
|---|---|---|
| Full management and operational on-site team, regular building management committee meetings, online tenant service request system, active environmental education, building user guides and risk management systems | Same, but on-site team only for buildings greater than 30,000 sqm | Remote monitoring with daily site attendance |

Note that **management itself is a graded parameter**. The quality of
property management is part of the building's grade — which is the Week 1
thesis ("good management = good quality asset") written into the PCA
standard.

## 2.7 PCA Method of Measurement

From *Method of Measurement: Commercial*, covering office, industrial,
retail and mixed-use premises. Three methods matter:

1. **Gross Lettable Area (GLA)**
2. **Gross Lettable Area – Retail (GLAR)**
3. **Nett Lettable Area, Office Building (NLA)**

### The dominant portion rule

Everything starts here.

> The **dominant portion** is the portion which occupies the most surface area
> of a vertical wall.

| If… | Then… |
|---|---|
| Windows are the dominant portion (window area **more than 50%** of wall area) | Measure to the **inside face of glass**. The frame is included as a window element. |
| The wall is the dominant portion (window area **less than 50%** of wall area) | Measure to the **finished surface of the wall**. |

> **The dominant portion decides *where you measure to*, not what you include.**
> It is a per-wall test, so one tenancy can have a glass-dominant wall on one
> elevation and a solid-dominant wall on another, measured to different
> planes. Students routinely apply one answer to the whole floor.

### NLA — Net Lettable Area (office)

The NLA is the aggregate of floor lettable space at each level, measured in
square metres:

- **from** the internal surface — dominant portion of external walls,
  firewalls, service areas
- **to** the centreline of inter-tenancy walls or partitions

| Include | Exclude |
|---|---|
| Window mullions | Areas set aside for the provision of services |
| Window frames | Areas dedicated as public spaces not for the exclusive use of tenants (foyer and atrium) |
| Structural columns or piers | Lift lobbies, lift shafts, stairs and escalators |
| Engaged perimeter columns | |
| Fire hose reels attached to a wall | |
| Additional facilities for individual tenants | |

On a floor plan: measure to the inside surface of the dominant portion,
**engaged column is included**, and **exclude the electrical duct and common
areas**.

### GLA — Gross Lettable Area (industrial and large-format retail)

**Applications:** warehouses, industrial buildings, free-standing
supermarkets, showrooms.

The GLA for each tenancy is measured in square metres:

- **External building walls** — **outside faces** of the dominant portion of
  the external walls
- **Inter-tenancy or common walls** — **centre line** of the walls (i.e. for
  multiple tenancy)

| Include | Exclude (in a multiple-tenanted building) |
|---|---|
| Window mullions | Areas set aside for the provision of services |
| Window frames | Areas dedicated as public spaces not for the exclusive use of occupiers of the floor or building (foyers, atria and access ways) |
| Structural columns | Lift lobbies, lift shafts, stairs and toilets |
| Engaged perimeter columns or piers | |

On a floor plan: measure to the **external** face of the dominant portion of
the wall; **includes internal structural columns**; **protruding columns on
the external face are not included** in the GLA. In a multiple tenancy,
measure to the external face on external walls and the centre line on the
inter-tenancy wall.

### NLA vs GLA — the one comparison to memorise

| | **NLA** | **GLA** |
|---|---|---|
| Used for | Office buildings | Warehouses, industrial, free-standing supermarkets, showrooms |
| External walls | Measure to the **internal** surface of the dominant portion | Measure to the **outside** face of the dominant portion |
| Inter-tenancy walls | Centreline | Centreline |
| Structural columns | **Included** | **Included** |
| Toilets | Excluded (service area) | **Explicitly excluded** in the multiple-tenanted list |
| Lift lobbies, shafts, stairs | Excluded | Excluded |
| Relative size, same building | Smaller | Larger |

> **The single distinguishing fact: NLA measures to the *inside* of external
> walls, GLA to the *outside*.** Everything else is nearly identical. That one
> difference is why GLA always exceeds NLA for the same envelope, and why you
> cannot compare a $/sqm rent struck on NLA with one struck on GLA. If an
> assignment gives you rates on different bases, say so rather than averaging
> them.
>
> Second trap: **structural columns are included in both.** It feels wrong —
> you cannot use the space a column occupies — but you pay for it under both
> methods. What is *not* included is a column protruding beyond the external
> face under GLA.

## 2.8 Commercial Property Incentives

- **Rent free period**
- **Rent abatement**
- **Fit-out contribution**
- Other forms — furnishings and equipment; relocation costs and pay-out of
  other lease commitments; interest-free loan; cash payments, cars, boats,
  holiday packages
- **Tax implications** apply

> **Incentives are why face rent ≠ effective rent.** A landlord will almost
> always prefer to give a large incentive than to cut the face rent, because
> value is `NI / CR` and the face rent is what capitalises. The incentive is a
> one-off hit; a lower face rent is a permanent reduction in capitalised
> value. When Part A asks what the manager did to protect value, generous
> incentives with a held face rent is a defensible answer — and the Brisbane
> industrial figure below (13% average incentive) is the benchmark to compare
> against.

## 2.9 Commercial Property Risks

- Commercial properties are exposed to **economic changes** — higher return
  and higher risk compared to residential.
- When the economy slows and unemployment rises, **consumer spending falls**.
  *Which property sector is affected first?* — retail, because discretionary
  spend contracts immediately, ahead of office (which is protected by lease
  terms) and industrial (which lags through the supply chain).
- **Location is essential** — accessibility and infrastructure; zoning.
- **Market condition** — competitors and supply.
- **Capital expenditure** — repair, value added.

Office (Colliers) · Brisbane CBD Office Market (Knight Frank) · CBRE, JLL
etc. · Urban Developer news (daily subscription) · PCA, UDIA, API members
updates · newspapers.

**Q1. A lift in a B-grade office building has a 1-in-20-year chance of a catastrophic failure that would close the building for a month. Which quadrant, and what is the response?**

Low probability, high impact → **insurance**, and lowering the risk profile in
that specific area (for example, a service contract and condition
monitoring). It is not the concentrate-effort quadrant.

**Q2. Same building, but the roof leaks in most heavy storms and damages one tenancy's ceiling tiles each time. Which quadrant?**

High probability, low impact → adjust systems and activities to lower the
probability, particularly through maintenance and the tenancy agreement;
benchmark; find low-cost systems solutions. Insurance is the wrong answer —
a recurring, expected event.

**Q3. A new Brisbane CBD office building has 22,000 sqm NLA and 1,100 sqm floor plates. Can it be Premium grade?**

No. Under the **new buildings** table, Brisbane Premium requires >25,000 sqm
NLA *and* a floor plate >1,500 sqm. It fails both. On size it comfortably
exceeds the Grade A threshold (>10,000 sqm, floor plate >1,000 sqm).

Note that if it were an *existing* building it would fall under "other CBDs"
(>20,000 sqm, floor plate >1,000) and would meet the Premium size test — the
grading tables differ.

**Q4. A warehouse is 40m × 25m measured to the outside face of the external walls, with four internal structural columns each 0.4m × 0.4m, and a 0.5m-deep pilaster protruding from the external face along 10m of one wall. What is the GLA?**

```
Envelope to outside face   = 40 × 25          = 1,000.0 sqm
Internal structural columns: INCLUDED         → no deduction
Protruding external columns: NOT included     → no addition
GLA                                           = 1,000.0 sqm
```

The distractors are the columns. Internal structural columns are included
(so you do not subtract 4 × 0.16 = 0.64 sqm), and the protruding pilaster on
the external face is excluded (so you do not add 10 × 0.5 = 5 sqm).

**Q5. Why can't you compare a $/sqm rent quoted on GLA with one quoted on NLA?**

Because GLA measures to the outside face of external walls and NLA to the
inside face, so GLA is the larger area for the same building. The same total
rent spread over a larger area gives a lower $/sqm. The rates are on
different denominators and are not comparable without restating one.

## 2.10 Introduction to Industrial Property

### The industrial market

| Sectors | Participants | What is being leased |
|---|---|---|
| Location | Owner occupiers | Office to factory space |
| Property use | Institutional investors | Hard stand |
| Size | Private investors | Landscaping |
| Construction | Government (owned and leased) | Car parking |
| | Lessees | Building size |
| | Financiers | |

> **"Hard stand" is the industrial-specific one.** It is the sealed external
> area used for truck movement, container storage and parking. In industrial
> leases it is leased and priced separately from the building, often at a
> much lower rate per sqm — so a headline "$/sqm" for an industrial property
> is meaningless unless you know whether hard stand is in the numerator, the
> denominator, or neither.

### Brisbane industrial and logistics market

- **Occupier demand:** transport, postal and warehousing **44%**; retail trade
  **19%**
- **Vacancy:** stable through 1H22 and 2H22, now **0.6%**
- **High supply**
- **Net face rental growth of 12.5%**, to **$145/m²** (at 2Q23)
- **Average incentives 13%**

Source: CBRE (2023), *Australia Industrial and Logistics vacancy 1H23*.

> **0.6% vacancy against 12.5% rental growth is the whole story.** With
> effectively no vacancy, occupiers have no alternatives and rents run. Note
> also that these figures are 2023 vintage while the office data is 2025 — do
> not present them as contemporaneous in an assignment.
>
> And note **net face** rental growth: 12.5% growth on a *face* basis with
> average incentives of 13% means effective growth is materially lower. Face
> and effective rent is the same distinction as §2.8.

### Safe premises — industrial property

Industrial property owners are **legally required to provide safe premises**.
A prospective tenant should also perform their own due diligence, being
particularly mindful of:

- Access
- Fire safety
- Security
- Surfaces
- Hazardous materials and toxins
- Installed plant and equipment
- Lighting
- Air quality

## 2.11 Commercial Property Management in Practice

From the Week 2 industry seminar — Phil Levesque, Managing Director and
Founder, FAL Property Group (in the industry since 2002; founded FAL in
2007; manages in excess of **$1.2 billion** in assets across shopping
centres, multi-storey office, industrial warehouses and strata-titled
units).

### What students think vs reality

Students think property management is mainly rent collection and
maintenance. In reality managers oversee cash flow, risk, compliance,
leasing strategies, tenant relations and investment returns, and handle
disputes, budgets, emergencies and complex lease administration requiring
strong judgment.

### The three things property owners care about

1. **Income** — rental revenue, occupancy, cash flow; managed through
   efficient rent collection and minimising vacancies
2. **Risk** — tenant defaults, compliance, safety, market uncertainty;
   managed by proactive monitoring and control
3. **Value** — income stability, lease quality, tenant retention and
   operational performance affecting asset valuation

That maps exactly onto `CV = NI / CR`: income is NI, risk is CR, value is CV.

### Why leases are so valuable

Leases determine rental income, expense recoveries and tenant rights, and
form the foundation of property cash flow. Buildings without tenants
generate little revenue. Strong leases and reliable tenants reduce risk,
improve financing terms and increase property value.

### Critical lease events

Timely tracking of **rent reviews, option exercises and lease expiries**
prevents costly errors. Rent reviews drive cash-flow growth; option notices
influence tenant retention and occupancy. **Make-good obligations** require
careful negotiation at expiry to avoid disputes and additional costs.

### Risk management in practice

Work health and safety · fire and essential services compliance (legally
mandated, and often invisible until an audit or incident exposes a gap) ·
environmental risk (contamination and waste liabilities that surface long
after they appear) · insurance and hidden costs.

The weirdest problems: floods and storm damage, fire and power incidents,
dangerous goods and contamination, vehicle strikes and structural damage.

### Technology and the future

Platforms such as Cirrus8, MRI and PropertyMe handle trust accounting, lease
administration and reporting at scale. Building management systems monitor
energy, access and equipment in real time. Analytics turn raw data into
insight on income, arrears and expenses.

**AI will** accelerate reporting and assist communication. **AI won't**
replace relationships or commercial judgement.

### Career lessons

Relationships matter most · **problems don't shrink by waiting** · every
lease clause matters · reputation compounds over time. To add value quickly
as a graduate: learn the leases, master Excel and cash flow, ask questions,
follow through.

> "Commercial property management is not just about buildings. It's about
> protecting and growing income-producing businesses housed within those
> buildings."

## 2.12 Topic 2 Summary

- **Risk** is variance from expectation, positive or negative, from internal
  (non-systemic) or external (systemic) sources. Effort concentrates on
  **high probability / high impact**; insurance belongs to **low probability /
  high impact**.
- The **PCA Office Market Report** (Jan and Jul) is the benchmark source.
  National vacancy rose from **14.7% (Jan 25) to 15.2% (Jul 25)**. Brisbane CBD
  went 9.5% → 10.2% → 10.7%.
- **PCA grades** run Premium / A / B / C, with separate benchmark tables for
  existing and new buildings — and Brisbane is treated differently between
  them. Management quality is itself a graded parameter.
- **NLA measures to the inside of external walls; GLA to the outside.** Both
  include structural columns and use the centreline of inter-tenancy walls.
  GLA is used for warehouses, industrial, free-standing supermarkets and
  showrooms; NLA for offices.
- **Incentives** preserve face rent — and therefore capitalised value — at the
  cost of a one-off payment.
- **Brisbane industrial** at 2Q23: 0.6% vacancy, 12.5% net face rental growth
  to $145/m², average incentives 13%, demand led by transport/postal/
  warehousing at 44%.
