# TOPIC 1 — Property and Asset Management

Week 1. What property and asset management actually is, who the property
manager answers to, and the single equation the whole unit hangs off.

## 1.1 The Nature of Real Property

Real property is held by private and public sectors alike — including
corporatised government departments and statutory authorities. Two forces
push every holder toward active management:

- **Accounting and taxation requirements** — the value of assets sits on the
  balance sheet, so it gets scrutinised.
- **Requirements for return on all assets** — no owner gets to hold property
  passively any more.

That second point is the reason this unit exists. Property is no longer
just occupied; it is expected to *perform*.

## 1.2 Characteristics of Real Property Assets

Five headline characteristics, then a longer list. Learn the five — they
are the most quotable thing in Week 1.

| Characteristic | What it means in practice |
|---|---|
| **Fixed** | It cannot be moved. Location risk is permanent and undiversifiable at the asset level. |
| **Unique** | No two properties are identical, so valuation is comparative, not exact. |
| **Sub-segment** | Markets split into office / retail / industrial and further by grade and location. A "property market" barely exists as one thing. |
| **Income / value from use** | Value is derived from the use to which the asset is put — not from the bricks. |
| **Large capital** | Entry is expensive, which drives long-term decisions, capital investment, and pooled vehicles like unit trusts and syndication. |

Extending that list:

- Considerable and variable **legal** dimension
- **Evolves over time** — a "dynamic asset"
- **Diversity of stakeholders**
- **Conservative** as an asset class
- **Complex cash flows**
- **Large upfront capital**
- **Income and operating expenses** both matter
- **Lag of supply** — you cannot build a tower in response to this quarter's demand

> **The supply lag is the exam-friendly one.** Because supply cannot respond
> quickly, property markets overshoot in both directions. The Brisbane CBD
> development pipeline in Topic 2 is this characteristic made concrete:
> towers approved in one cycle deliver into a completely different one.

## 1.3 Forces and Influences

Society, business, community and individuals **invest** in real property
assets and hold **expectations / returns** of them. Four things mediate
between the investment and the return:

1. **The type of asset** — its characteristics, internal and intrinsic drivers
2. **The external environment** — drivers, demand, change
3. **Management strategies / principles** — risk, income
4. **Time** — emerging trends

Only the third is under your control as a manager. That is the whole job.

## 1.4 Property Asset Management vs Property Management

The lecture draws a deliberate split.

**Property asset management** operates on two levels at once:

| Level | Content |
|---|---|
| Day-to-day aspects of management | Technical knowledge, financial knowledge, legal knowledge, customer care |
| Long-term prospects and opportunities | Maximising rental, maximising capital growth |

**Property management** is the operational layer:

- **Traditionally** — starts when the building is finished and handed over
  from the construction company to the property manager.
- **Ideally** — the manager is involved from the beginning, in the design
  period.
- Day-to-day operation and maintenance (e.g. cleaning)
- Building core — for example, the number of lifts
- Future adaptation

> **Why "ideally involved at design" is worth marks.** Decisions locked in at
> design — lift count, floor plate, service risers — determine operating cost
> and lettability for the building's whole life. A manager brought in at
> handover inherits constraints they can never fix, only pay for. If a
> question asks how a property manager adds value early, this is the answer.

## 1.5 The Evolution of Property Management

Traditional property management was a task list:

- Accounting and reporting
- Leasing
- Maintenance and repair
- Paying taxes
- Provision of utilities and insurance
- Remodelling
- Rent rate setting and collection

Modern property management is better understood as a set of **interface
points** — the manager coordinates between:

- Site supervisor (owner's representative)
- Legal representative
- Asset manager
- Property manager
- Accountant / tax agent
- Facilities manager
- Real estate agent

The shift is from *doing the tasks* to *coordinating the specialists*.

## 1.6 The Role of the Property Manager

> "A property manager is pivotal to the asset achieving the required outcome
> for all stakeholders."

That sentence is the unit's thesis. The operative words are **all
stakeholders** — the manager is not the owner's agent alone in practice,
even though they are legally the owner's agent. Which sets up the tension
in the next section.

Beyond producing a **property management plan**, the role covers:

- Maintenance and outgoings collections
- Leasing
- Service contracts
- Taxation provisions
- Facilities management and outsourcing
- Dispute and conflict resolution

## 1.7 Stakeholder Analysis

This table is highly examinable. Learn the *required outcome* column — one
word each — and you can reconstruct the rest.

| Stakeholder | Required outcome | Measurement criteria / issues |
|---|---|---|
| **Owner** | Marketable / competitive | Value / net capital growth · risk control and security (investment and income) · protection from claim · versatility / utility · special requirements of an owner-occupier |
| **Manager** | Controllable / predictable | Financial (income and outgoings) · tenancy management systems · operational systems |
| **Tenant / Lessee** | Best platform for business | Business performance · lease issues (security of tenure, cost competitive) · client satisfaction · staff satisfaction |
| **Occupant** | Habitable | Logical · accessible · controllable · safe · available services and amenities · social and other links |
| **Community** (users and wider community) | Compatible | Accessible · logical · safe · available services · aesthetics · environmental issues · environmental health · legislative compliance |

> **Tenant ≠ occupant, and the exam knows it.** The *tenant* is the entity on
> the lease and wants a platform for its business. The *occupant* is whoever
> is physically in the building — staff, customers, visitors — and wants
> somewhere habitable and safe. They are frequently different people with
> different interests. Writing "tenant" where the answer is "occupant" costs
> the mark.

## 1.8 Agency — Responsibility

Accepting the role of agent brings contractual and/or legislative
responsibilities. **Some are explicit; some are implicit** — that
distinction is the point of the slide.

| Explicit | Implicit |
|---|---|
| Contractual relations | Client expectations |
| Legislative regulations | Community values |
| Codes of conduct | Ethics and morals |
| Rules | Cultural issues |

## 1.9 Legal Responsibilities of a Property Manager

Nine duties. These are classic agency-law duties and they examine well
because they are crisp:

1. Duty to **perform**
2. Duties with **professional skill** required and with **due care**
3. Offer **professional advice**
4. **Not to delegate** authority
5. To act in the interests of the principal with the **utmost good faith**
6. Duty **not to act on his own behalf**
7. Duty not to take **secret commissions or profit**
8. Duty to keep the principal's business **confidential**
9. Duty to **account and keep informed**

> **Duties 5, 6 and 7 are one idea in three forms: no conflicts of interest.**
> Utmost good faith is the positive statement; not acting on your own behalf
> and not taking secret commissions are the two specific prohibitions that
> follow from it. If a scenario question hands you a manager who quietly
> takes a supplier rebate, that is duty 7, and duties 5 and 6 as well.

## 1.10 Establishing an Agency Agreement

**In writing.** Then the contract provisions:

| Group | Provisions |
|---|---|
| Who and what | Principal and agent · nominated personnel / representatives · the property to which the agreement refers |
| Scope | Duration · specific identification of duties · provisions for securing services · access to, use and return of records |
| Money | Payment · financial delegations |
| Operating | Special or emergency provisions · confidentiality · reporting / recommendations · administrative processes |
| Ending it | Termination of agreement / provisions for variation · clarification of issues / dispute resolution |

### Types of contracts

The manager sits at the centre of four contract families:

1. **Property** — sales, lease, licences / concessions
2. **Capital works** — construction, acquisitions
3. **Professional services** — agency, property management
4. **Services contracts** — maintenance, equipment services, cleaning
   (internal / external), security, waste

**Q1. A property manager accepts a 5% rebate from the cleaning contractor she appoints, and does not tell the owner. Which legal duties are breached?**

Duty not to take secret commissions or profit (7), the duty to act in the
interests of the principal with utmost good faith (5), and the duty not to
act on her own behalf (6). Arguably also the duty to account and keep
informed (9), since the rebate is money arising from the agency that has not
been disclosed.

**Q2. Give the "required outcome" for each of the five stakeholders in one word or phrase each.**

Owner — marketable / competitive. Manager — controllable / predictable.
Tenant — best platform for business. Occupant — habitable. Community —
compatible.

**Q3. Why does the lecture say a property manager should ideally be involved during the design period rather than at handover?**

Because design decisions — building core, lift numbers, floor plate,
services — determine operating costs, lettability and future adaptability
for the life of the asset. A manager engaged at handover inherits those
constraints and can only manage around them, never remove them.

**Q4. Which characteristic of real property explains why a boom produces oversupply two or three years later?**

Lag of supply. Development cannot respond quickly to demand, so approvals
made at the top of a cycle complete into a weaker market.

## 1.11 Strategic Asset Management (SAM)

### The RICS definition

> "The process which aligns business and property asset strategies, ensuring
> the optimisation of an organisation's property assets in a way which best
> supports its key business goals and objectives."

Note what that definition does: it makes property **subordinate to the
business**. Property exists to support business goals, not the other way
round.

SAM sits underneath **strategic resource management (SRM)**:

| Layer | Concerns |
|---|---|
| Strategic resource management (SRM) | Planning and prioritisation of investment · deployment of investment · monitoring against KPI · effective and efficient direction and utilisation of assets |
| Strategic asset management (SAM) | Performance of property assets · strategic and operational component |

### What SAM is trying to do

- **Maximise returns to stakeholders** — value of portfolio (owner), business
  performance (tenant)
- **Minimise risk**
- **Manage the certainty of cash flow**
- **Meet investment objectives**
- **Value adding**

Applied to a single property or a full portfolio: CBD buildings, shopping
centres, industrial estates, and not-for-profits such as universities.

### Two components, two time frames

| Component | Content | Time frame |
|---|---|---|
| **Operational** | Ongoing management of property assets within an allocated budgetary framework | Three to five years (short to medium) |
| **Strategic** | Decisions on appropriate investment in property assets to meet customer / end-user needs and service delivery requirements | Three to five years (medium), up to ten years and beyond (long) |

> **Both components start at "three to five years."** That overlap is in the
> source and is easy to mis-copy. The difference is not where they start but
> where they *end* — the strategic component extends to ten years and beyond;
> the operational one does not.

### Basic principles of SAM

Different environments and buildings can change · service function · short,
medium and long term · evolution over time · **function over form** ·
simplicity and functionality · people and place · management systems ·
property-by-property strategies / plans · stakeholder analysis.

## 1.12 Maximising Value — `CV = NI / CR`

This is the equation the unit is built on.

```
CV = NI / CR

CV = Capital value
NI = Net income      (Gross income less outgoings)
CR = Cap rate        (a risk weighting)
```

To manage property assets strategically and operationally you therefore
need a sound understanding of:

- **how net income is derived and managed**, and
- **the nature and management of risk**

Because those are the only two inputs. Everything else in this unit is a
strategy for moving one of them.

### Worked example — the two levers

A neighbourhood centre produces gross income of $2,791,905 and outgoings of
$603,781, and comparable centres trade on a 6.05% cap rate.

```
NI = 2,791,905 − 603,781
   = 2,188,124

CV = 2,188,124 / 0.0605
   = $36,167,339
```

Now run the two levers separately.

**Lever 1 — lift net income by $100,000** (say, by leasing the vacancy and
tightening recoveries), cap rate unchanged:

```
CV = 2,288,124 / 0.0605
   = $37,820,231
   → +$1,652,893 of capital value from +$100,000 of income
```

**Lever 2 — leave income alone, but tighten the cap rate by 50 basis
points** to 5.55% by improving the quality of the income (longer WALE,
better covenants, no arrears):

```
CV = 2,188,124 / 0.0555
   = $39,425,657
   → +$3,258,318 of capital value from zero extra rent
```

> **The cap rate is the bigger lever, and it is the one students forget.**
> Halving a percentage point off the cap rate created roughly twice the value
> that $100k of new rent did. Cap rate compression comes from the *quality* of
> income, not the amount: WALE, covenant strength, arrears history, dispute
> history, maintenance standard. "Good management = good quality asset" is
> the slide's own summary, and it is a capital-value statement, not a
> housekeeping one.

Note also the arithmetic direction: **CR is in the denominator**, so a
*lower* cap rate means a *higher* value. Writing "the cap rate improved to
7%" when you mean values rose is backwards and it is a common slip.

## 1.13 The Four Strategy Families

Because `CV = NI / CR` and `NI = gross income − outgoings`, there are
exactly four things to have a strategy for: **gross rent, outgoings, the
capitalisation rate, and capital value.**

### Gross income strategies

- Expenditure to maximise rent increases
- Tenant mix to maximise patronage and turnover
- Promotional activities to increase patronage
- Diversify income sources — signage, licences, car parking and kiosk
- Timely market reviews
- Minimise vacancy periods
- Manage delinquencies
- Active lease renewal strategies

### Outgoings strategies

- Employ new technologies to reduce utilities and maintenance costs
- Installation of timers and meters
- Individual metering for user pays
- Structure lease terms for maximum recoverable
- Negotiate long-term service contracts
- Tender service contracts
- Lobby government for reduced statutory costs
- Preventive maintenance regime

### Capitalisation rate strategies

The cap rate *range* is market driven, but the quality of the asset is
manageable:

- Income and outgoings strategies
- **Attractive WALE**
- Frequency and terms of market reviews
- Good quality tenants
- Historical low vacancies
- Limited arrears
- Few disputes
- Well maintained

### Capital value / portfolio strategies

A function of all the other factors, plus:

- Protecting the property rights
- Monitoring the local environment
- Adapting property to changing needs
- Acquisition and sales
- Conducting regular valuations
- Funding considerations

### Formulating strategies

The inputs are the property characteristics, the institutional arrangements
of the markets, organisational objectives, the property users'
characteristics, and the external world. Those feed a loop:

**formulate strategies → test strategies → selection of strategies →
implementation → performance evaluation → review strategies → revise the
property or its use → (back to formulate)**

> **SAM summary, in the lecture's own words:** *maximising returns to owners by
> proactively minimising both costs and risks — and it requires you to have a
> strategy, reviewed regularly.* Note "proactively". Reactive management is
> the thing being argued against throughout Week 1.

## 1.14 Building Repurposing — Quay Quarter Tower

The Week 1 case for adaptive reuse:

- Adaptive reuse of an existing Sydney tower
- Billed as the world's tallest upcycled building
- Repurposed **70% of its own materials** and surpassed sustainability targets
- Earned a **6-star** rating for sustainability from the Green Building
  Council of Australia

This is the "evolves over time / dynamic asset" characteristic and the
"adapting property to changing needs" capital-value strategy, in one
example. Useful to cite in an exam answer about extending economic life.

## 1.15 Topic 1 Summary

- Real property is **fixed, unique, sub-segmented, valued from use, and
  capital hungry** — and supply lags demand.
- **Property management** is operational; **property asset management** adds
  the long-term value layer. The modern manager coordinates interfaces
  rather than performing every task.
- The manager is **pivotal to the asset achieving the required outcome for
  all stakeholders** — owner, manager, tenant, occupant, community — each of
  whom wants something different.
- Agency brings **explicit and implicit** obligations and **nine legal
  duties**, three of which are really one rule: no conflicts of interest.
- **SAM** aligns business and property strategy (RICS), has an operational
  and a strategic component, and aims to maximise return while minimising
  risk and managing cash-flow certainty.
- Everything reduces to **`CV = NI / CR`**. Manage net income, manage risk.
  The cap rate is the larger and more neglected lever, and it responds to
  income *quality*.

<summary>✅ Checkpoint 2 — SAM and capital value</summary>

**Q1. A centre has gross income of $1,800,000 and outgoings of $520,000, and comparable sales imply a 6.25% cap rate. What is the capital value?**

```
NI = 1,800,000 − 520,000 = 1,280,000
CV = 1,280,000 / 0.0625 = $20,480,000
```

**Q2. Using Q1's figures, which adds more capital value — recovering an extra $60,000 of outgoings from tenants, or compressing the cap rate to 6.0%?**

Extra recovery: NI rises to $1,340,000, so CV = 1,340,000 / 0.0625 =
$21,440,000, a gain of **$960,000**.

Cap rate compression: CV = 1,280,000 / 0.060 = $21,333,333, a gain of
**$853,333**.

Here the income lever wins, because $60,000 is a large 4.7% lift on a small
income base while 25 basis points is a modest compression. The general
lesson is not "cap rate always wins" — it is that you must *compute both*
rather than assume. Compare with §1.12, where a larger compression on a
larger income base flipped the answer.

**Q3. Name three things a manager can do that compress the cap rate without increasing rent at all.**

Any three of: lengthen WALE, improve tenant covenant quality, keep vacancy
history low, keep arrears limited, avoid disputes, maintain the building
well, set favourable market-review frequency and terms.

**Q4. Why does the RICS definition of SAM put business strategy first?**

Because property is a means to the organisation's key business goals, not an
end in itself. SAM *aligns* business and property asset strategies and
optimises the property in the way that best supports the business — so the
business objective determines the property decision.
