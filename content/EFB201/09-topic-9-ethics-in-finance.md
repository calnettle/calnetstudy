# Topic 9 — Ethics in Finance

**Reference:** CFA 2020 Level II curriculum Readings 1–3; Stanford case
*Barclays and LIBOR: Anatomy of a Scandal* (ETH-03).

Two halves. The first is the **CFA Standards of Professional Conduct** — seven
headings, and you need to be able to name which one a described behaviour
breaches. The second is two cases: **Hong Kong Minibonds** (a suitability and
disclosure failure) and the **LIBOR scandal** (market manipulation, with
arithmetic attached). The LIBOR arithmetic is the only calculation in the
topic and it is very likely to appear.

---

## 1. The seven Standards

| | Standard |
|---|---|
| **I** | Professionalism |
| **II** | Integrity of Capital Markets |
| **III** | Duties to Clients |
| **IV** | Duties to Employers |
| **V** | Investment Analysis, Recommendations, and Actions |
| **VI** | Conflicts of Interest |
| **VII** | Responsibilities as a CFA Institute Member or Candidate |

The deck details II, III and IV — the three the cases turn on.

### II — Integrity of Capital Markets

| | |
|---|---|
| **II(A) Material Nonpublic Information** | Members and candidates who possess material nonpublic information that could affect the value of an investment **must not act or cause others to act** on the information |
| **II(B) Market Manipulation** | Members and candidates **must not engage in practices that distort prices or artificially inflate trading volume** with the intent to mislead market participants |

### III — Duties to Clients

| | |
|---|---|
| **III(A) Loyalty, Prudence, and Care** | Duty of loyalty to clients; act with reasonable care and prudent judgement; **place clients' interests before the employer's or their own** |
| **III(B) Fair Dealing** | Deal **fairly and objectively with all clients** when providing analysis, making recommendations, taking action, or engaging in other professional activities |
| **III(C) Suitability** | (1) In an advisory relationship: make a reasonable inquiry into the client's investment experience, risk and return objectives and financial constraints **before** any recommendation, and reassess regularly; determine the investment is suitable to the client's financial situation and consistent with their written objectives, mandates and constraints; judge suitability **in the context of the client's total portfolio**. (2) When managing to a specific mandate, strategy or style: only recommend or act consistently with the portfolio's stated objectives and constraints |
| **III(D) Performance Presentation** | Communicated performance information must be **fair, accurate and complete** |
| **III(E) Preservation of Confidentiality** | Keep information about current, former and prospective clients confidential **unless**: (1) it concerns **illegal activities** by the client, (2) disclosure is **required by law**, or (3) the client **permits** disclosure |

### IV — Duties to Employers

| | |
|---|---|
| **IV(A) Loyalty** | Act for the benefit of the employer; do not deprive them of your skills and abilities, divulge confidential information, or otherwise cause harm |
| **IV(B) Additional Compensation Arrangements** | Do not accept gifts, benefits, compensation or consideration that competes with or might reasonably create a conflict with the employer's interest, **unless written consent is obtained from all parties** |
| **IV(C) Responsibilities of Supervisors** | Make reasonable efforts to ensure anyone under your supervision or authority complies with applicable laws, rules, regulations, and the Code and Standards |

> **The three confidentiality exceptions are exhaustive and examinable.**
> Illegal activity, legal requirement, client permission. "My employer asked
> me to" is not on the list — and III(A) puts the client's interests **above
> the employer's**, so it cannot be added.

> **III(C) Suitability requires the inquiry to happen *before* the
> recommendation, and to be judged against the *total* portfolio.** Both
> qualifiers are load-bearing. Recommending a suitable product to someone whose
> risk profile you never assessed still breaches the Standard, and a risky
> holding that is suitable inside a diversified portfolio is not made unsuitable
> by being risky in isolation.

## 2. Material nonpublic information — the four tutorial questions

### What is "material" information?

> Information is material if its disclosure **would probably have an impact on
> the price of a security**, or if **reasonable investors would want to know
> it before making an investment decision**. In other words, information is
> material if it would **significantly alter the total mix of information**
> currently available about a security in such a way that the price would be
> affected.

### What is "nonpublic"?

> Information is nonpublic **until it has been disseminated or is available to
> the marketplace in general** — as opposed to a select group of investors.
> "Disseminated" means "made known". A company report of profits posted on the
> internet and distributed widely through a press release, or accompanied by a
> filing, has been effectively disseminated.

### Case — the SmartTown chain

Frank Barnes, president and controlling shareholder of SmartTown, decides to
accept a tender offer at almost double the market price. He tells his sister
(the treasurer), who tells her daughter (who owns no stock), who tells her
husband **Staple**. Staple tells his stockbroker **Alex Halsey**, who
immediately buys SmartTown stock for himself.

**Analysis.** The information about the pending sale is **both material and
nonpublic**. Two violations of II(A):

- **Staple** violated the Standard by **communicating** the inside information
  to his broker.
- **Halsey** violated the Standard by **buying the shares on the basis of**
  material nonpublic information.

> **II(A) prohibits acting *or causing others to act*. Passing the tip on is
> itself the breach.** Staple never traded, and is still in violation. Note who
> is *not* named in the model answer: Barnes, his sister and the daughter. A
> full answer explains that the Standard binds CFA members and candidates, and
> that the chain becomes a breach at the point where a market professional
> either passes it on or trades on it.

### Case — Elizabeth Levenson

Levenson is based in Hanoi, covering the Vietnamese market for a
Singapore-based firm. She is invited, with the ten largest shareholders, to
meet a manufacturing company's finance director. He states the company expects
its workforce to strike next Friday, crippling productivity and distribution.
Can she use this to change her rating from "buy" to "sell"?

**Analysis.** She must **first determine whether the material information is
public**. Under II(A), **a small group forum does not qualify as a method of
public dissemination**. If the company has not made the information public,
**she cannot use it**.

> **A meeting with the eleven largest shareholders is a "select group", not
> the marketplace.** Size of the audience is irrelevant; what matters is
> whether the information was made generally available. Her correct course is
> to encourage the company to disclose publicly, and to make no
> recommendation change until it does.

## 3. Case — the Hong Kong Minibond

A structured product mis-sold through bank branches. The deck's contrast table
is the answer to "which Standards were violated".

| True feature | Marketed feature |
|---|---|
| **5% return carrying credit risk, market value risk, interest rate risk, exchange rate risk and swap counterparty risk** | **High return, low risk, or even risk-free** |
| A 5% coupon **CDO insured with a CDS** | Issuer used the word **"bond"** to build confidence |
| If the underlying company goes bankrupt: coupon payments stop | Front-line bank staff **proactively induced clients to convert matured fixed deposits into Minibonds** for higher return and free shopping coupons |
| The issuer can **withhold CDS compensation until the Minibond's maturity, 3–6 years away** | Staff **failed to consider clients' risk profiles** — especially retired, elderly, less educated and risk-averse clients |
| The issuer can **call the Minibonds prematurely** — e.g. if the CDS insurer goes bankrupt, the issuer can fire-sell, and investors get only the recovered value | Staff **neither provided product information** (term sheets, prospectuses) **nor explained product features and risks** |
| | Staff **only highlighted the well-known reference entities** and their associated credit risks |

The complainant testimony the deck reproduces makes the suitability point
vividly: an illiterate depositor with decades of manual-work savings was told
the Minibonds were "the most suitable financial product for retirement,
promising higher interest rate and low risk", and invested HK$500,000.

**Which Standards were breached?**

| Standard | How |
|---|---|
| **III(C) Suitability** | No inquiry into the clients' experience, objectives or constraints; a leveraged credit derivative sold to retired, elderly and risk-averse depositors; no assessment against the total portfolio |
| **III(A) Loyalty, Prudence and Care** | Staff placed the bank's sales interest ahead of the clients' interests |
| **III(B) Fair Dealing** | Vulnerable clients were targeted rather than dealt with fairly and objectively |
| **III(D) Performance Presentation** | Return information was presented as high-return/low-risk — not fair, accurate or complete |
| **I Professionalism** | Misrepresentation in the sales process |
| **IV(C) Responsibilities of Supervisors** | Branch management ran an incentive structure (shopping coupons, conversion targets) that produced systematic breaches |

> **The word "bond" is the mis-selling.** A Minibond was a **credit-linked
> note** — a CDO wrapped in a CDS — with no principal guarantee. Calling it a
> bond imported the connotations of Topic 2's fixed income: fixed maturity,
> guaranteed obligation to repay, top of the capital structure. None of those
> applied. If asked why the product name mattered, that is the answer.

> **Suitability is about the *client*, not the *product*.** A Minibond sold to
> a sophisticated institution that understood the CDS counterparty risk would
> breach nothing. The same product sold to a retiree who was never asked about
> their risk tolerance breaches III(C) regardless of how the product later
> performed. Do not argue that the product was inherently unethical.

## 4. Case — the LIBOR scandal

### Review: what LIBOR was

From Topic 2: every business day a selected group of banks submitted the rate
at which they believed they could borrow from other banks in the London
interbank market. The submissions were ranked, the **upper four and lower four
discarded**, and the **arithmetic mean of the remaining rates** became LIBOR.

### How submitters could manipulate it

**Overstate or understate the rate to be submitted**, so as to affect the set
of rates used in the calculation and move LIBOR upward or downward.

### How traders benefited — the interest rate swap

The deck's structure. Barclays (Borrower 1) and Borrower 2 have a swap:

```
                     Floating: LIBOR + 1%
  BARCLAYS  ──────────────────────────────────────►  BORROWER 2
            ◄──────────────────────────────────────
                     Fixed: 7%

  Barclays also pays Lender 1 a fixed 8%
  Borrower 2 also pays Lender 2 LIBOR + 1%
```

Net position per period:

| | Period 1 | Period 2 | Period 3 |
|---|---|---|---|
| **LIBOR** | 6% | 5% | 4% |
| **BARCLAYS** | | | |
| Receive from Borrower 2 (fixed) | +7% | +7% | +7% |
| Pay Borrower 2 (LIBOR + 1%) | −7% | −6% | −5% |
| Pay Lender 1 | −8% | −8% | −8% |
| **Net payment** | **−8%** | **−7%** | **−6%** |
| **BORROWER 2** | | | |
| Pay Barclays (fixed) | −7% | −7% | −7% |
| Receive from Barclays (LIBOR + 1%) | +7% | +6% | +5% |
| Pay Lender 2 (LIBOR + 1%) | −7% | −6% | −5% |
| **Net payment** | **−7%** | **−7%** | **−7%** |

Reading the table:

```
BARCLAYS net  = +7 − (LIBOR + 1) − 8  =  −2 − LIBOR
  LIBOR 6% → −8%      LIBOR 5% → −7%      LIBOR 4% → −6%

  ⇒ THE LOWER THE LIBOR, THE LESS BARCLAYS PAYS
```

Borrower 2's net is a flat −7% whatever LIBOR does — it has converted floating
to fixed, which is the legitimate purpose of the swap. Barclays has done the
opposite: it has converted fixed to floating and now has a **direct financial
interest in the level of the rate it helps set**.

> **Barclays is the floating-rate PAYER, so it profits when LIBOR *falls*.
> That direction is the whole scandal.** Any question that asks whether the
> bank wants to over- or under-state its submission is asking you to identify
> which side of the swap it is on. A floating-rate **receiver** would want
> LIBOR pushed **up**. Draw the two legs before you answer.

> **The conflict of interest is structural, not incidental.** A bank that both
> submits to a benchmark and holds positions priced off that benchmark is
> conflicted by design. That is Standard **VI Conflicts of Interest** in
> addition to **II(B) Market Manipulation**, and naming both is what a full
> answer looks like.

### Worked example — the deck's version

Barclays is one of sixteen submitting banks. The other fifteen submit (%):

```
3.86  3.86  3.86  3.86
3.865 3.865 3.865 3.865 3.865
3.87  3.87  3.87  3.87  3.87
3.875
```

Barclays believes the accurate rate is **3.87**.

**If Barclays submits the honest 3.87** — sixteen values, sorted:

```
3.86 3.86 3.86 3.86 | 3.865 3.865 3.865 3.865 3.865 3.87 3.87 | 3.87 3.87 3.87 3.875
└── drop lowest 4 ──┘                                          └──── drop highest 4 ────┘

Remaining eight: 3.865 3.865 3.865 3.865 3.865 3.87 3.87 3.87
Sum   = (3.865 × 5) + (3.87 × 3) = 19.325 + 11.610 = 30.935
LIBOR = 30.935 / 8 = 3.866875%
```

The deck displays **3.8668%** (truncated rather than rounded — 3.8669 would be
the correctly rounded four-decimal figure).

**If Barclays submits a dishonest 3.86** — sixteen values, sorted:

```
3.86 3.86 3.86 3.86 | 3.86 3.865 3.865 3.865 3.865 3.865 3.87 | 3.87 3.87 3.87 3.875

Remaining eight: 3.86 3.865 3.865 3.865 3.865 3.865 3.87 3.87
Sum   = 3.86 + (3.865 × 5) + (3.87 × 2) = 3.860 + 19.325 + 7.740 = 30.925
LIBOR = 30.925 / 8 = 3.865625%
```

The deck displays **3.8656%** ✓.

```
Impact on LIBOR = 3.866875% − 3.865625% = 0.00125 percentage points
On a $1 billion loan: $1,000,000,000 × 0.0000125 = $12,500 per annum
```

> **The deck says $100,000 per annum. The correct figure from its own numbers
> is $12,500 — an eight-fold overstatement.** The $100,000 comes from applying
> the **0.01% change in Barclays' own submission** (3.87 → 3.86) to the $1bn
> notional. But Barclays' submission is only one of eight surviving values in
> a trimmed mean, so the **LIBOR** moves by only 0.00125%, which the deck's own
> very next slide computes correctly. `$1bn × 0.00125% = $12,500`. Use $12,500,
> show the two LIBOR values, and flag the discrepancy with your tutor — the
> mechanism the slide is teaching is right; the multiplication is not.

> **This is exactly why the deck's follow-up point matters: "to pay even less,
> Barclays can collude with other banks."** One bank moving a trimmed mean by
> 0.00125% is a small effect. The design of the trimmed mean is what forces
> collusion, and collusion is what turned a submission error into a
> multi-billion-dollar scandal. The next section quantifies the difference.

### Worked example — the tutorial (Q5 and Q6)

Set-up. A Barclays trader and a counterparty agree an interest rate swap on
**USD 4 billion** of loans:

```
Barclays pays counterparty:      $4bn × (LIBOR + 0.2%)
Counterparty pays Barclays:      $4bn × 5%
Benchmark:                       3-month US dollar LIBOR
```

Barclays believes the accurate — the ethical — rate to submit is **4.820%**.
The other fifteen banks submit (%):

```
4.810  4.810  4.810  4.810
4.815  4.815  4.815  4.815  4.815
4.820  4.820  4.820  4.820  4.820
4.830
```

**The ethical baseline.** Barclays submits 4.820, making sixteen values:

```
4.810 ×4 | 4.815 ×5 | 4.820 ×6 | 4.830

Drop lowest four:   the four 4.810s
Drop highest four:  4.830 and three of the six 4.820s

Remaining eight:    4.815 4.815 4.815 4.815 4.815 4.820 4.820 4.820
Sum   = (4.815 × 5) + (4.820 × 3) = 24.075 + 14.460 = 38.535
LIBOR = 38.535 / 8 = 4.816875%
```

**Q5 — Barclays submits 4.810% instead.**

```
4.810 ×5 | 4.815 ×5 | 4.820 ×5 | 4.830

Drop lowest four:   four of the five 4.810s
Drop highest four:  4.830 and three of the five 4.820s

Remaining eight:    4.810 4.815 4.815 4.815 4.815 4.815 4.820 4.820
Sum   = 4.810 + (4.815 × 5) + (4.820 × 2) = 4.810 + 24.075 + 9.640 = 38.525
LIBOR = 38.525 / 8 = 4.815625%

Reduction  = 4.816875% − 4.815625% = 0.001250 percentage points
Selfish gain = $4,000,000,000 × 0.0000125 = $50,000
```

**Barclays pays $50,000 less.** ✓ Matches the solution.

**Q6 — Barclays also colludes.** It submits 4.810 itself, convinces **two
banks** to submit 4.810 rather than 4.815, and a **third bank** to submit
4.810 rather than 4.820. All other submissions are unchanged.

```
Counting the new distribution:
  4.810:  4 original + 2 moved down from 4.815 + 1 moved down from 4.820
          + Barclays' own 4.810                                     = 8
  4.815:  5 − 2                                                     = 3
  4.820:  5 − 1                                                     = 4
  4.830:                                                            = 1
                                                                    ───
                                                                     16 ✓

Drop lowest four:   four of the eight 4.810s
Drop highest four:  4.830 and three of the four 4.820s

Remaining eight:    4.810 4.810 4.810 4.810 4.815 4.815 4.815 4.820
Sum   = (4.810 × 4) + (4.815 × 3) + 4.820 = 19.240 + 14.445 + 4.820 = 38.505
LIBOR = 38.505 / 8 = 4.813125%

Reduction  = 4.816875% − 4.813125% = 0.003750 percentage points
Selfish gain = $4,000,000,000 × 0.0000375 = $150,000
```

**Barclays pays $150,000 less** — **three times** the solo gain, for moving
three other banks. ✓ Matches the solution.

> **Both final answers are right, but the solution's intermediate `4.817%` is
> a rounded figure and using it gives the wrong result.** `4.817% − 4.81563% =
> 0.00137%`, and `$4bn × 0.00137% = $54,800`, not $50,000. The exact baseline
> is **4.816875%**. Carry six decimal places through the subtraction and round
> only at the dollar figure. (The same rounding produces $154,800 instead of
> $150,000 on Q6.)

> **A second housekeeping note: the tutorial solution numbers both answers
> "5".** The second "5. Pay $150,000 less" is the solution to **Q6**. Do not
> read it as an alternative answer to Q5.

> **Count trimmed values by *position*, not by *distinct value*.** In the
> ethical baseline six banks submit 4.820, and you drop **three** of them
> along with the 4.830 — leaving three 4.820s inside the average. Dropping all
> six because "4.820 is one of the high values" is the most common error, and
> it changes the answer.

### Why 0.01% matters

The deck's closing point: **more than $4 trillion of interest-rate swap
contracts occur every day.** A one-basis-point distortion applied across that
notional is enormous even though it is invisible on any single contract.

### Which Standards were violated?

The deck poses the question and leaves it open. A complete answer:

| Standard | How |
|---|---|
| **II(B) Market Manipulation** | Submitting rates the bank did not believe accurate, with intent to distort a benchmark price. This is the central breach |
| **I Professionalism** | Knowingly participating in conduct involving dishonesty, fraud and deceit |
| **VI Conflicts of Interest** | Submitting to a benchmark while holding positions priced off that benchmark, without adequate separation |
| **IV(C) Responsibilities of Supervisors** | Supervisors permitted traders and submitters to communicate about desired rates |
| **III(A) Loyalty, Prudence and Care** | Counterparties on the wrong side of manipulated contracts were harmed by the bank's pursuit of its own interest |

> **Do not lead with II(A) Material Nonpublic Information here.** LIBOR is a
> **manipulation** case, not an insider-trading case: the wrongdoing is
> distorting a published price, not trading on a secret. Reserve II(A) for the
> SmartTown and Levenson cases and lead with **II(B)** for LIBOR. Mixing them
> up is the fastest way to look like you have not read the case.

## Summary

- Seven Standards. **II** Integrity of Capital Markets, **III** Duties to
  Clients and **IV** Duties to Employers carry the cases.
- **II(A)** prohibits acting **or causing others to act** on material nonpublic
  information. **II(B)** prohibits distorting prices or inflating volume.
- **Material** = would probably affect the price, or reasonable investors would
  want to know. **Nonpublic** = not yet disseminated to the marketplace in
  general; **a select group forum is not dissemination**.
- **III(C) Suitability** requires inquiry **before** the recommendation and
  judgement **against the total portfolio**. **III(E)** has exactly three
  exceptions: illegal activity, required by law, client permission.
- **Minibond** = a suitability and disclosure failure. A CDO-plus-CDS sold as a
  "bond" to retirees whose risk profiles were never assessed. Breaches III(C),
  III(A), III(B), III(D), I and IV(C).
- **LIBOR** = a manipulation case. Barclays **paid floating**, so it wanted
  LIBOR **lower**. Breaches II(B) primarily, plus I, VI and IV(C).
- LIBOR arithmetic: **trim four from each end, average the middle eight.**
  Ethical baseline **4.816875%**; solo manipulation **4.815625%** → **$50,000**
  on $4bn; with three colluding banks **4.813125%** → **$150,000**.
- The deck's claim that a 0.01% understatement saves **$100,000 on $1bn is
  wrong** — the LIBOR only moves 0.00125%, so the saving is **$12,500**.
- Carry full precision. Rounding 4.816875% to 4.817% turns $50,000 into
  $54,800.
