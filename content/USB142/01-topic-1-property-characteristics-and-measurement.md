# TOPIC 1 — Property Characteristics and Measurement

Weeks 1 and 2. Why property behaves differently to every other asset, how
to describe it without sounding like a real estate agent, and how to
measure it so the number survives scrutiny.

## 1.1 Property Economics

Economics is a social science studying the production, distribution and
consumption of goods and services. Robbins (1998) narrows it to "the
science that studies human behaviour as a relationship between ends and
scarce means, which have alternative uses."

**Property economics** is one branch of it. The lecture's point is that
property markets deviate from pure economic reasoning more than most,
because demographic profile, perceived risk, time preferences and
community sentiment all push buyers and sellers away from the
mathematical model (Häring & Storbeck 2009). That deviation matters more
in real estate than elsewhere because transactions are large-scale and
the financial consequences are significant.

| | Micro economics | Macro economics |
|---|---|---|
| Studies | Individuals, households, firms, single markets | Aggregates — government, regions, industry sectors |
| Property relevance | How one buyer chooses one house | Cash rate, interest and exchange rates, employment, inflation, national income and output, global markets, international trade |
| Decision level | Where most property decisions actually sit | Sets the water level everyone swims in |

Maslow's hierarchy is used to make the micro point: shelter sits at the
physiological base, but a *particular* house serves safety, belonging,
esteem and self-actualisation simultaneously. Behaviour is motivated by
several needs at once, which is why buyers pay for a view.

> **Consumers can walk away; producers cannot.** A buyer can withdraw from
> the property market in an afternoon. A developer who has committed to a
> site cannot. That asymmetry — instant demand-side exit against a
> multi-year supply-side commitment — is the engine behind the price
> spikes and gluts you will describe in Topic 5.

### Property's place in the economy

- Real property is a critical input to practically all economic activity,
  alongside capital, labour and management skill
- It is a basic form of holding and aggregating wealth
- Development, financing, management and operations generate employment
  across the economy
- It provides the **collateral** most financial sector dealings are based on
- In economic theory, "land" includes buildings and other fixed improvements

Australian residential stock, ABS 2024:

```
Total value of dwelling stock      $11,093.8 billion
Total residential dwellings            11,252,600
Mean price of residential dwellings      $985,900

Check:  11,093,800,000,000 ÷ 11,252,600 = $985,888  →  $985,900  ✓
```

Most people hold property directly (own, lease, work in it) and indirectly
(investments, superannuation, insurance).

### The business cycle

| Expansion | Contraction |
|---|---|
| Production/output ↑ | Production/output ↓ |
| Unemployment ↓ | Unemployment ↑ |
| Wages ↑ | Wages ↓ |
| Consumer spending ↑ | Consumer spending ↓ |

The property sector is closely linked to and largely integrated with the
financial sector and the development/construction sectors. How much
investment flows into property depends on the direction of the financial
sector, the attractiveness of competing options (shares, interest rates,
bond rates), and prevailing interest and exchange rates.

## 1.2 The Three Characteristic Sets

Everything in Week 1 hangs off one division. If you learn one structure
from this topic, learn this one — it is the spine of the "describe the
property" sections of both assessments.

```
PHYSICAL          →  Land + Improvements
INSTITUTIONAL     →  Rights derived from law
ECONOMIC          →  Immobility, large units, durability, scarcity,
                     land unproductive alone
```

### Physical — land

Shape, size, topography, views, exposure (sunlight, wind), soil quality,
bearing capacity. The *effect* of any of these depends on three things:
the cost of making the site productive, whether doing so is profitable,
and what the proposed use actually requires. A steep block is only a
problem relative to what you want to build.

### Physical — improvements

The distinction the lecturer keeps returning to:

| | Examples |
|---|---|
| Improvements **TO** the land | Drainage, filling, trace elements, clearing, levelling |
| Improvements **ON** the land | Structures, fencing, other physical attachments |

Improvements TO the land can help or hurt the improvements ON it. For
improvements on the land, consider conformity with surrounding uses,
amenity provided, placement relative to natural features, external space
relationships, and whether it is the **optimal improvement** — the one
that maximises site value subject to site constraints.

### Institutional

1. **Property rights derived from sources of law**
   - **Tenure system** — powers of ownership to *use*, *alienate* (transfer
     ownership or rights to another party), *assimilate*, *pass by
     succession*, *claim title*
   - The **Crown retains a prior right to resume or acquire**
   - **Laws restricting rights** — health, town planning, building, heritage
2. **Rights available to be traded** = the tenurial system, *less*
   restrictions imposed at law

That subtraction is the whole institutional story. Topic 2 unpacks it.

### Economic — the five

| # | Characteristic | Consequences |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Immobility** | Users come from a fixed area → localised market, few transactions to act as a price indicator. Income derived from a fixed location → cannot avoid externalities. Inhomogeneity, unlike the stock market → no two properties identical, trends hard to study, long transaction times, opaque market and sale terms |
| 2 | **Large economic units** | Large capital required to invest; special financing techniques required to own |
| 3 | **Durability** | Land virtually indestructible → major investment vehicle. Structures designed for long life but may be economically "destroyed" through obsolescence. HABU may change over time. Cost of improvements may not be recouped for a long time. Ownership and planning decisions are long-term |
| 4 | **Scarcity** | Physical supply of land is limited; economically, use cannot be sustained beyond its extensive margin; uses have locational requirements. Scarcity creates competition for uses; **the market sets the price and the valuer attempts to predict it** |
| 5 | **Land is unproductive on its own** | Requires labour, capital and management. Optimum productivity requires the power to act plus institutional factors (titling, legislation). If unbalanced → **functional obsolescence**. Labour, capital and management are mobile and will move to sites offering higher rewards. **Land value is the residual after rewards are paid to the other factors** |

> **Never confuse physical supply with economic supply.** Physically, land
> is finite. Economically, supply is far tighter than that, because a use
> with locational requirements cannot occupy a site that lacks them. There
> is plenty of land in Queensland; there is almost no land in Kalinga that
> a Kalinga buyer would consider. This is the sentence that earns the mark
> on any "why is property scarce" question.

Durability plus scarcity together explain short-run price behaviour:
construction is slow, improvements are durable, stock cannot meet an
upsurge in demand — therefore **demand is the more important determinant
of short-term price.**

### Hefferan's longer list

The Week 1 deck also runs a fuller set of economic characteristics worth
having as talking points: property is held in small parcels (so use
decisions are microeconomic); land is a finite resource; property is a
physical asset subject to deterioration, decay, erosion and obsolescence;
it is fixed in space; it is heterogeneous; it exists in sectors with
different determinants of supply and demand; it requires management; it
requires investment (and the other production factors merge irretrievably
into the land); it has long supply lags producing short-term price spikes
and windfall profits for existing holders; it has a high entry price
creating barriers to entry; it is under legislative control; and it is a
changeable asset that evolves physically, legally and economically.

## 1.3 Sectors and Participants

Three headline sectors, each subdividing:

| Residential | Commercial | Infrastructure |
|---|---|---|
| Single lot dwelling, townhouses, units/apartments, aged care, build-to-rent, student accommodation | Offices (premium, A–C grade, suburban), industrial (logistics, manufacturing, warehousing), retail (super regional, neighbourhood, convenience), hotels/motels/resorts/pubs, rural (hobby farms, grazing, cultivation), specialised (childcare, storage units) | Hospitals, schools, stadiums, libraries, toll roads, dams |

The more sophisticated the sector, the greater the variation of property
*within* that market.

### The property life cycle

```
Planning → Acquisition → Utilisation → Asset Management → Dispose/Redevelop
                    ↑                                            │
                    └────────────────────────────────────────────┘
```

| Direct participants | Indirect participants |
|---|---|
| Property developers, investors, real estate agents, **valuers**, property managers, facility managers, asset managers, property owners, property researchers | Financiers/banks, solicitors, town planners, builders, government, architects, tenants, service providers |

### Graaskamp's three stakeholder groups

From Whipple (2006, p.48) — a cleaner way to think about who is in the
market than a flat list:

- **Space users** — occupy space to buy or rent; decide on cost vs location
  vs amenity; space providers compete for them
- **Space producers** — skills/trades/professions (supply constrained by
  availability), materials (supply chain), capital (debt and equity, short
  term for the developer and long term for the investor)
- **Public infrastructure** — roads, water, sewer, drainage, power,
  telephone, data; hospitals, schools, fire, police, libraries

> **Why characteristics analysis exists at all.** Understanding a property's
> characteristics lets you predict its *saleability*, *potential buyers*,
> *investment potential*, *rates and taxes*, *market demand* and *property
> enjoyment*. Those six are all factors of value. If an exam question asks
> "why does the valuer bother describing the roof material," that is the
> chain to walk back along.

## 1.4 Location Analysis

Week 2's opening rule: **all analysis starts at the macro and moves to the
micro.** Area → suburb → street → views → services → transport.

Socio-economic factors — educational attainment, occupation status, income
levels — influence quality-of-life attributes and the opportunities
afforded to households in a geography. The deck sorts communities into a
three-level hierarchy driven by location, affordability, function,
amenities, infrastructure, gentrification, employment levels and crime.

The Brisbane examples, as given:

| Tier | Suburb | Sale 1 | Sale 2 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Low socio-economic | Inala | 34 Delphinus St — $480,000 (13 Jan 2023) | 3 Clipper St — $612,000 (4 Jan 2023) |
| Middle socio-economic | Bardon | 21 Leslie St — $1,625,000 (18 Feb 2023) | 85 Hebe St — $3,050,000 (20 Feb 2023) |
| High socio-economic | Hamilton | 49 Oxford St — $1,225,000 (10 Dec 2022) | 70 Parkside Circuit — $4,005,000 (12 Oct 2022) |

> **The tier labels and the prices do not line up, and that is the lesson.**
> Bardon is labelled "middle" and Hamilton "high," yet Bardon's second sale
> ($3,050,000) beats Hamilton's first ($1,225,000) by $1.8m. The spread
> *within* each suburb — $1.4m in Bardon, $2.8m in Hamilton — dwarfs the
> gap between suburb medians. A report that quotes the suburb median and
> stops has done no analysis. (The deck also files both Bardon and Hamilton
> under "Brisbane North West," which is geographically loose for Hamilton;
> don't repeat that in your own work.)

### Location within location — the street

- How does the street compare to the subject, and how does it affect demand?
- Street appeal; services connected or available
- Issues in the street that could affect value, positive and negative
- Access/egress, noise, aspect, views
- Side of the street; proximity to advantageous and detrimental factors
- Flood and bushfire risk
- On top of a hill vs lowest house in the street
- Busy road / road noise

The prompt the lecturer repeats: **think about why people live here — those
are the value drivers.**

## 1.5 Describing the Improvements

Three groups, always in this order: **external, internal, ground.**

### Exterior

Sub-classification (vacant land, single unit dwelling, townhouse,
unit/apartment, mobile home, tiny house) → building design → building size
→ age → construction type → quality.

Construction type breaks down as: number of levels, foundations, façade /
external wall, frame, roof materials, doors and windows, style/design/era.

| Element | Options named in the deck |
|---|---|
| Foundations | Concrete slab, timber stumps, basement, piers |
| External wall | Timber, brick, rendered, concrete, stone, or a combination (brick + render, brick + timber, render + brick + timber) — note construction material, whether structural or clad, condition, age |
| Balcony | Faux/Juliet (ornament), false/French, true |
| Other outdoor | Porch, veranda, patio, deck |

### Age by architectural period

Age generally indicates style and construction. **Age is not an indicator
of condition.**

| Period | Style | Markers |
|---|---|---|
| Late Colonial 1840s–1900s | **Queenslander** | Single storey, high set on timber stumps, timber construction, corrugated iron roof, timber-framed windows, wrap-around veranda |
| Late Colonial 1870s–1880s | Victorian terrace | Terrace form (Carlton, VIC example) |
| Federation 1890s–1910s | Federation / Queen Anne | Freestanding or semi-detached, red brick, tiled roof, detailed wooden fretwork in roof gables and windows |
| Inter-war 1920s–1930s | Porch-and-gable | Double-gable and multi-gable variants |
| Inter-war 1920s–1930s | California Bungalow | Low, shady, solid; freestanding; thick columns to front veranda; low-pitched tiled roof; wood panelling inside |
| Post-war 1940s | Timber frame | Single level, weatherboard, double-hung timber windows, tiled roof, chimney |
| Post-war 1950s | Renovated post-war | Split level, weatherboard cladding, timber casement windows, concrete stumps, underfloor enclosed with horizontal timber slats |
| Post-war 1960s | Timber frame | Single level, fibrous cement sheet cladding, timber casement windows, tiled roof |
| 1980s | Brick veneer | Single level, slab on ground, face brick, tiled double-gable roof, double lock-up garage, aluminium sliding windows, exposed aggregate driveway |
| 2010s | Modern brick veneer | Two storey, rendered brick veneer, tiled roof, covered portico, second-level balcony, DLUG, aluminium sliders, stencil concrete driveway |

Design/type also drives ownership structure. Generally: low density =
freehold; medium density (townhouses, terraces) = community title; medium
and high density (apartments) = strata title. Different models apply in
different states — see Topic 2.

### Interior

Design and layout (bedroom and bathroom counts, open plan vs traditional,
second living areas, eat-in kitchen, sustainable features, indoor/outdoor
living), wall finishes (VJ board, timber panels, tile, cladding, render),
flooring (polished concrete, timber, tile), ceilings (regular, vaulted,
cathedral, coffered, tall, shed, beamed, tray).

Then equipment and mechanical systems: plumbing (piping, fixtures, hot
water), HVAC, fire protection, electrical, and miscellaneous equipment
such as alarms, call systems and CCTV — **noting that CCTV may be a
fitting rather than a fixture.** Not all apply to residential.

### Ground improvements

Swimming pool, tennis court, basketball court, deck, pergola, detached
garage, carport, garden tool shed, storage shed, pavements (driveway,
garden pavement, walkways), landscaping, major trees, gardens, fencing.

For every improvement, record three things: **materials used**,
**condition** (poor / fair / good / new; unrenovated or newly renovated),
**quality** (poor / average / good).

## 1.6 Objective Writing

Academic and professional reports use **objective** language: impartial,
not personal, judgmental or emotive; avoids exaggeration and bias; shows
respect for others' views; fair and accurate. **Subjective** language
expresses opinions based on personal values, beliefs or preferences rather
than evidence, and can be disputed because it lacks complete proof.

The deck's paired example:

| Subjective | Objective |
|---|---|
| "A four-bedroom house in the cavity brick cottage style, featuring a detached single garage, a **huge** rear pergola, and **nice** gardens. Buyers looking for a **peaceful** outside environment may find the house **appealing** because it has **lovely** gardens, a **huge** rear pergola, and a **cosy** cottage-style atmosphere." | "A cavity brick dwelling with four bedrooms, detached single garage, rear pergola and established landscaping." |

And the location version, Chelmer:

| Subjective | Objective |
|---|---|
| "Encased by the Brisbane river on three sides, Chelmer is a suburb known for its **grand** homes and **picturesque**, Laurel tree lined streets. Presenting an **idyllic** family lifestyle … the **pinnacle** of easy suburban living." | "Chelmer is a riverside suburb in Brisbane's western suburbs. It consists of mostly low-density housing. Chelmer is located 7 kilometres South-West of the Brisbane CBD. In the 2016 census, 65% of residents were couples with children … 90% of people spoke only English at home." Then a list of amenities with **distance and direction** to each. |

> **The objective example contains a sentence you should not copy.** It
> reads: "The average household income is $180,000 above the Brisbane
> average of $121,000." That parses two ways — either the average *is*
> $180,000 (a 48.8% premium over $121,000), or it is $180,000 *above*
> $121,000, i.e. $301,000. On a slide that is literally teaching precision,
> that is an own goal. Write "the average household income is $180,000,
> compared with the Brisbane average of $121,000." Ambiguity is a
> subjectivity failure even when every word is factual.

Two prompts worth memorising, because they are how the lecturer wants you
to structure the description:

- **Exterior:** close your eyes and describe the house as if you're standing
  on the street looking at it
- **Interior:** close your eyes and describe the house as if you're standing
  at the front door

And the line that summarises the whole section: **a valuation is not a
sales brochure.**

## 1.7 Measurement

### Why property is measured

| Purpose | Why it matters |
|---|---|
| Transactional | Sale, leasing, and any related purpose including valuation |
| Property/facilities management | Calculating operating costs including repair and maintenance |
| Cost estimation | Construction costs for insurance, replacement, depreciation |
| Planning, design and development | Identifying development potential; controlling height and size |
| Taxation | Infrastructure charges and other government charges |
| Other | Benchmarking, construction cost rates, cost allocation, insurance, research, sustainability and energy efficiency |

"Size does matter. But bigger is not always better. You need to know how to
measure it and analyse it."

Different property types use different standards — office and retail are
very specific about where lease measurements are taken from. Because rent
and cost are negotiated per m² or per m² of GFA, **small measurement errors
compound into big money.**

### The standards

The **International Property Measurement Standards Coalition (IPMSC)**
convened its Standards Setting Committee in July 2013 to develop global
measurement standards. In Australia the **Property Council of Australia
Method of Measurement** is also very widely used, especially in lease
negotiations. (PCA measurement — NLA, GLA, GLAR — belongs to the commercial
units; residential uses GBA and GFA.)

**The underlying principle: physical measurement is a matter of fact, not
an opinion.** That single line separates measurement from everything else
a valuer does.

The seven IPMS measurement practice fundamentals:

1. Use the unit of measurement commonly adopted in the relevant jurisdiction
2. All measurements except height are taken **horizontally**
3. Support measurement with computer-generated drawings if available; where
   other drawings are used, prefer **annotated dimensions** over scaling alone
4. Where possible, verify measurements independently on site
5. Measurement and computing processes must be accurate enough for the
   purpose
6. Buildings or selected areas are measured individually, **level by level**
7. Where IPMS does not explicitly address a situation, extrapolate the
   principles logically and consistently, **and explain what you did**

Before measuring anything, settle five questions: what is the purpose of
the exercise; what premises are being measured; what is the building use;
what is the layout at the time of measurement; and what unit of measurement
is appropriate.

### GBA and GFA

```
GROSS BUILDING AREA (GBA)   ≡ IPMS External Floor Area
  The whole area of the main building footprint, measured from the
  OUTSIDE of all external walls.
  An external horizontal structure at any floor level with a balustrade
  to the open sides, INCLUDING generally accessible balconies and
  verandahs, but EXCLUDING patios and terraces not integral to the
  structural construction of the building.
  Permanent yard improvements (e.g. a shed) can be included separately:
  GBA = external measurement of Building A + Building B

GROSS FLOOR AREA (GFA)      ≡ IPMS Floor Area
  The sum of the floor area of all spaces within a normally horizontal,
  permanent, load-bearing structure, measured to the INTERNAL face of
  the wall.
  INCLUDES areas occupied by walls, columns, stairs, staircase openings,
  lift shafts, atria, corridors, entrance halls, cupboards and wardrobes,
  skirting boards, mouldings and cornices, for all or part of each level.
  EXCLUDES decks, verandahs, carports.
```

> **GBA is measured to the outside; GFA to the inside.** GBA is a footprint
> and is single-level unless you add buildings; GFA is summed across every
> level. For a two-storey house, GFA will usually exceed GBA. Getting the
> two the wrong way round inverts your LBR and FSR simultaneously.

Worked GBA example from the deck — four rectangles:

```
Area A:  3 m × 1 m  =   3 m²
Area B:  5 m × 6 m  =  30 m²
Area C:  8 m × 10 m =  80 m²
Area D:  4 m × 1 m  =   4 m²
                      -------
GBA               =  117 m²      ✓ (3 + 30 + 80 + 4 = 117)
```

### Scale drawing

Assessment 1 requires a hand-drawn floor plan **at 1:100** (1:200
acceptable for very large dwellings). Drawing to scale means every unit of
measurement is drawn in similar proportion. Photographs of your plan must
keep the scale too — **always indicate the scale on the drawing**, and use
a scale rule so the drawing survives being resized.

Australia is metric. Residential valuation expresses length and width in
**millimetres**.

```
metres → millimetres :  × 1000        12 m       = 12,000 mm
millimetres → metres :  ÷ 1000         3,500 mm  = 3.5 m

Then convert by scale:
  1:100  →  ÷ 100
  1:200  →  ÷ 200

Worked: a room 6 m × 3 m at 1:100
  Length:  6 m × 1000 = 6,000 mm  ÷ 100 = 60 mm
  Width:   3 m × 1000 = 3,000 mm  ÷ 100 = 30 mm
  Drawn:   60 mm × 30 mm
```

Sanity check with a real dimension from the 80 Bertha Street construction
drawings — the site is 14.821 m to the wall on one boundary:

```
14.821 m × 1000 = 14,821 mm  ÷ 100 = 148.2 mm at 1:100
```

That is 148 mm on an A3 sheet (297 × 420 mm), which fits comfortably. The
architect's own site plan for the same property is drawn at **1:200** on
A3 — which is exactly the deck's advice about large drawings.

Cal's A1 measured QUT's **E Block** rather than the subject dwelling:
total footprint **1,246.08 m²**, drawn by hand. At 1:100 a 1,246 m²
footprint would not fit on A3 — this is precisely the case the 1:200
allowance exists for.

## 1.8 Ratio Analysis

Three ratios. All are measures of **development intensity** — how hard the
site is being worked. All are expressed as `1 : x`, which is the same
number as a percentage.

```
                    Land Area        Gross Building Area
Land to Building  = ───────────  :  ─────────────────────      LBR ≤ 1 always
                    Land Area            Land Area

                    Land Area          Gross Floor Area
Floor Space       = ───────────  :  ─────────────────────      FSR CAN exceed 1
                    Land Area            Land Area

                    Land Area     Gross Building Area + Other Improvements
Site Coverage     = ───────────  : ────────────────────────────────────────  SCR ≤ 1
                    Land Area                  Land Area
```

| | Numerator | Can it exceed 1? | Functional component |
|---|---|---|---|
| **LBR** | Gross Building Area (footprint) | **No** — a footprint cannot be bigger than its site | Dwelling |
| **FSR** | Gross Floor Area (all levels) | **Yes** — routinely, for multi-level buildings | Dwelling |
| **SCR** | GBA + other improvements: awnings, pools, enclosed or unenclosed balconies, decks, patios, pergolas, terraces, verandas, carports | **No** | Site + ground improvements |

Valuers may devise other ratios — usable land where the site is steep, or
building efficiency by deducting circulation space from GFA to give net
floor area.

### The deck's own worked examples, checked

```
LBR   dwelling 120 m², land 521 m²
      = (521 ÷ 521) : (120 ÷ 521) = 1 : 0.2303  →  23.03%   deck: 23%   ✓

LBR   building 80 m², land 100 m²
      = 1 : 0.8  →  80%                          deck: 80%   ✓

FSR   floor area 105 m², land 321 m²
      = (321 ÷ 321) : (105 ÷ 321) = 1 : 0.3271  →  32.71%   deck: 33%   ✓

FSR   GFA 120 m², land 100 m²
      = 1 : 1.2  →  120%                         deck: 120%  ✓  (exceeds 1)

SCR   GBA 250 m², land 521 m²
      = 1 : 0.4798  →  47.98%                    deck: 48%   ✓

SCR   GBA = A + B + C = 122 + 24 + 30 = 176 m², land 521 m²
      = 1 : 0.3378  →  33.78%                    deck: 34%   ✓
```

Every deck example reconciles. The rounding is to the nearest whole
percent throughout.

### Worked example — 80 Bertha Street, Kalinga

The measured areas from Cal's A1 inspection:

| Component | Area |
|---|---|
| Site | 448 m² |
| Ground floor | 118.819 m² |
| First floor | 96.160 m² |
| **Dwelling GFA** | **214.979 m²** |
| Alfresco | 25.655 m² |
| Carport | 33.750 m² |
| Gardens | 14.380 m² |
| Fencing | 19.920 m² |
| **Ground improvements** | **93.705 m²** |

```
GFA check   118.819 + 96.160                     = 214.979 m²   ✓

LBR         118.819 ÷ 448  = 0.265221            → 26.52%
FSR         214.979 ÷ 448  = 0.479864            → 47.99%
```

LBR uses the **footprint** (ground floor, 118.819 m²), not the GFA. FSR
uses the GFA across both levels. Because this house is two storeys, FSR is
almost double LBR — which is exactly what those two ratios are for.

> **Site coverage for this property has three different published answers.**
> This is worth working through, because it is the single most likely thing
> to be marked wrong in an assessment.
>
> ```
> (a) Cal's A1:  (118.819 + 25.655 + 33.75 + 14.38 + 19.92) ÷ 448
>                = 212.524 ÷ 448 = 47.44%
> (b) Buildings + alfresco + carport only:
>                (118.819 + 25.655 + 33.75) ÷ 448
>                = 178.224 ÷ 448 = 39.78%
> (c) Architect's BA drawing (MR Design + Drafting, May 2021):
>                190 m² ÷ 448 = 42.41%   — stated on the plan as "42.4%"
> ```
>

**Q1.** A single-storey house has GBA 180 m² and GFA 168 m² on a 600 m²
lot. Calculate LBR and FSR, and explain why GFA is the smaller number.

**A1.** LBR = 180 ÷ 600 = 1 : 0.30 = **30%**. FSR = 168 ÷ 600 = 1 : 0.28 =
**28%**. GFA is smaller because it is measured to the *internal* face of the
walls while GBA is measured to the *outside* — the difference is the wall
thickness around the perimeter, plus any covered-but-excluded areas such as
verandahs.

**Q2.** Can FSR exceed 100%? Can SCR? Explain both.

**A2.** FSR **can** — it sums floor area across every level, so a
three-storey building on a fully covered site would approach 300%. SCR
**cannot** — improvements physically cannot cover more than the site they
sit on. Neither can LBR, for the same reason.

**Q3.** You are given: site 448 m², ground floor 118.819 m², first floor
96.160 m², alfresco 25.655 m², carport 33.750 m², gardens 14.380 m²,
fencing 19.920 m². Calculate SCR and justify what you included.

**A3.** SCR = (118.819 + 25.655 + 33.750) ÷ 448 = 178.224 ÷ 448 = **39.78%**.
Include the dwelling footprint, alfresco and carport — all are structures
covering the site. Exclude gardens and fencing: landscaping does not cover
the site and a fence line has negligible footprint. State your inclusions
explicitly in the report so a marker can follow the arithmetic.

**Q4.** Your tape measure reads 4,850 mm. What is that in metres, and how
long is the line at 1:100?

**A4.** 4,850 ÷ 1000 = **4.85 m**. At 1:100, 4,850 ÷ 100 = **48.5 mm**.

**Q5.** IPMS fundamental 3 says to prefer annotated dimensions on drawings
over scaling. Why, and what does the 80 Bertha Street construction plan
print in its title block?

**A5.** Because a drawing can be reproduced or resized and the scale
silently breaks, while a written dimension cannot. The plan prints "USE
DIMENSIONS FOR CONSTRUCTION — DO NOT SCALE FROM PLAN."

## Summary

- Property economics sits between micro (individual decisions) and macro
  (cash rate, employment, inflation); both drive value
- Three characteristic sets: **physical** (land + improvements TO and ON),
  **institutional** (tenure less legal restriction), **economic**
  (immobility, large units, durability, scarcity, land unproductive alone)
- Never confuse physical supply with economic supply; demand is the more
  important determinant of short-term price because supply lags
- Land value is the residual after labour, capital and management are paid
- Analysis runs macro → micro: area, suburb, street, then the property
- Describe external, internal and ground improvements in that order, and
  record materials, condition and quality for each
- Objective language only. A valuation is not a sales brochure
- **GBA** = footprint measured to the *outside*; **GFA** = all levels
  measured to the *inside*. Physical measurement is fact, not opinion
- **LBR = GBA/land** (≤1), **FSR = GFA/land** (can exceed 1),
  **SCR = (GBA + covering improvements)/land** (≤1)
- Say what you counted in the SCR numerator. Three published answers exist
  for the same house because nobody did
