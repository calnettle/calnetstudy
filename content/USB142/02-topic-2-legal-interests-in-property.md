# TOPIC 2 — Legal Interests in Property

Weeks 3 and 4. The institutional characteristic set from Topic 1, opened
up: how a parcel is identified, what the register says the owner has, what

## 2.1 How Land Is Identified

```
THE OLD HIERARCHY (pre-1965)
  Parish → Portion → Subdivision ('sub') → Resubdivision ('resub')
  e.g. 'Resub 3 of Sub 2 of Portion 1 in the Parish of Enoggera,
        County of Stanley'
  Town land sometimes: 'subs of allotments of Section …, Town of …'

THE MODERN SYSTEM
  1965  Freehold land = a LOT on a SURVEY PLAN, e.g. 'Lot 3 on RP 102396'
  1985  Lot on Plan extended to land administered by the State
  All land except unsurveyed unallocated State land now has a Lot on
  Plan description — the REAL PROPERTY DESCRIPTION (RPD).
```

Every parcel gets a unique description; no two share one. The old system
has not vanished — it is buried in the encumbrance schedule of modern
titles. The Crown reservation on the 80 Bertha Street title reads "Deed of
Grant No. 19515042 (**POR** 202)" — POR is Portion — and the architect's
drawings for the same house still print `PARISH: ENOGGERA`. One property,
two identification systems 50 years apart, both live on paper today.

| Prefix | Plan type | What it implies |
|---|---|---|
| **SP** | Survey Plan | Current default since the automated titling system |
| **RP** | Registered Plan | Older freehold subdivision |
| **CP** | Crown Plan | Plans from Crown grants |
| **BUP** | Building Units Plan | Strata, typically high-rise — *Building Units and Group Titles Act 1980* |
| **GTP** | Group Titles Plan | Cluster/townhouse development, same Act |
| **GRP** | Group Titles Plan of Resubdivision | A derivation of GTP |

> **The prefix answers the ownership-structure question before you read the
> description.** "Lot 4 on BUP 12345" is a strata lot with a body corporate,
> common property and lot entitlements under the *Body Corporate and
> Community Management Act 1997*, and the plan's site area is not the
> owner's alone. Write "freehold" for a BUP and everything downstream —
> comparables, ratios, outgoings — is wrong.

## 2.2 Survey Plans and the Real Property Description

Boundaries are defined by **survey plans**, also called a cadastral map,
maintained by the State. A survey plan **shows** the lot description,
bearings (angles measured in relation to true North), distances, area, and
location relative to other boundaries. It **may also show** easements,
natural monuments such as a lake, stream or cliff, and artificial monuments
such as a survey peg, fence or building.

```
Real Property Description   Lot 172 on RP19535     ← identifies the LAND
Title Reference             15171099               ← identifies the TITLE
```

> **The title search does not tell you the area.** Read the 80 Bertha Street
> search line by line — estate, lot on plan, local government, owners,
> encumbrances — and there are no square metres anywhere. The 448 m² comes
> from the SmartMap DCDB extract and Council's report, not the register.
> "448 m² as per the current title search" attributes a figure to a document
> that does not contain it.

The four documents that *do* state the area all agree, which is unusual
enough to record:

| Source | Date | Area |
|---|---|---|
| SmartMap / DCDB subject parcel extract | 08/04/2025 | 448 m² |
| BCC Property Lot Report (City Plan v32) | 27/03/2025 | 448 m² |
| MR Design + Drafting construction plan | May 2021 | 448 m² |
| WBP PropertyPRO valuation, site area field | 21/06/2021 | 448.00 m² |

That is the denominator Topic 1's LBR, FSR and SCR rest on, so those ratios
are safe. Council still qualifies it — "areas shown in this Report are
approximate only" — so the survey plan governs any boundary dispute.

## 2.3 Torrens Title and Indefeasibility

Robert Richard Torrens, born in Cork in 1814, became Registrar General of
South Australia in 1852 and, against bitter opposition, got the **Real
Property Act 1858 (SA)** proclaimed. Queensland followed first, by the
proclamation of the **Real Property Act 1861**.

```
The system is TITLE BY REGISTRATION, not REGISTRATION OF TITLE.
  You must register your interest to HAVE legal title.
  Registration does not record a right you had — it CREATES it.
```

Under the **Land Title Act 1994** the Registrar must record every lot and
every interest in a lot in the Freehold Land Register (ss 27, 28), and on
recording a lot an **indefeasible title** is created (s 37).

| Registration required for | LTA | | LTA |
|---|---|---|---|
| Alienation of State land | s 47 | Title by adverse possession | s 99 |
| Transfers of a lot or interest | s 60(1) | Writs of execution | s 116 |
| Leases of a lot or part of a lot | s 64 | Caveats | s 122 |
| Mortgages | s 72 | Power of attorney | s 133 |
| Easements | s 82 | Carbon abatement interests | — |

**The four elements.** A registered title is *conclusive* despite a defect
in the transaction or the registration process; a registered interest has
*priority against all other interests* subject to exceptions (s 184 LTA); a
proprietor who registers *without notice* of prior unregistered interests is
not bound by them; and the title is nonetheless *subject to exceptions*,
s 184(3)(b) and s 185 LTA.

**Exceptions to indefeasibility:** fraud · short leases · wrong description
of boundaries · earlier existing title · omitted easements · adverse
possession · the *in personam* exception · overriding statutes.

> **"Indefeasible" does not mean "nothing can touch it."** Eight named
> exceptions sit in the same deck as the word, and the two that bite in
> residential practice — **short leases** and **omitted easements** — are
> both invisible on the search and still bind the new owner. An answer that
> stops at "the title is indefeasible, so the purchaser takes clear" has
> skipped half the lecture.

Unregistered interests are protected by **caveats and settlement notices**,
which freeze dealings until the parties reach court.

> **What the certificate of title does *not* tell you** is the most
> examinable question in Week 3. Not the area; not zoning or overlays; not

> is a statement about **rights**, not about **land**.

### The real trap, from the real dossier

The WBP Property Group PropertyPRO valuation of 21 June 2021 on this exact
property, prepared for Macquarie Bank, records:

```

                           Title Not Supplied

```

and qualifies itself: "An up-to-date Title search has not been sighted or

unaffected by any adverse encumbrance."

> **The valuer's client already held the encumbrance the valuer could not
> see.** The Macquarie mortgage was registered 27/08/2020 — **298 days
> before** the valuation Macquarie instructed. The report also names one
> borrower where the register names two joint tenants. Neither is
> negligence: the assumption was disclosed in writing, as ANZPGP-201
> requires, and a mortgagee's own security does not reduce market value. The
> lesson is the mechanism — **a qualification transfers the risk, it does not
> remove the fact.** Assume clear title in the assumptions section, in those
> words, and value the unencumbered fee simple; do not quietly value
> "whatever is left."

## 2.5 The Bundle of Rights

```
THE STICKS                       WHAT OWNERSHIP MEANS (Week 3's list)
  Right to use                     Right to exclusive possession
  Power to alienate                Right to alienate
  Power to assimilate              Right to devise
  Pass by succession               Right to create a lesser interest (lease)
  Claim title to                   Right to share
                                   Right to receive income from the land
```

Institutional characteristics affect how land is bought, sold, used, taxed,
resumed, inherited and leased. Exclusive possession has physical limits.
**Above** the land the owner has exclusive use of the airspace *at least to
the extent necessary for the reasonable use and enjoyment of their land* —
not infinitely upward. **Below**, at common law the owner has rights to the
soil, but sub-surface minerals are vested in the Crown, *Mineral Resources
Act 1989* s 8; with very few exceptions all minerals and petroleum are
reserved to the State, extended to **quarry material from 31 December 1991**.

What you buy: the physical limits of the land, restrictions from external
sources, and improvements ON and TO the land — including all **fixtures**.
The test for whether an object has become part of the land has two limbs:
**degree of annexation** and **purpose of annexation**.

> **Two governments, two compensation rules.** The Commonwealth must acquire
> property on **just terms** — s 51(xxxi) of the *Commonwealth of Australia
> Constitution Act*. **There is no such provision in the State
> constitutions.** One Week 3 slide writes this as "the *Crown* can acquire
> land compulsorily … s 51(xxxi)" and the next corrects it to "the
> Commonwealth". Almost every residential resumption you meet — road
> widening, busway, park acquisition — is a *State* or council resumption
> compensated under Queensland statute, not the Constitution.

**Common law** is judge-made: where no statute covers an issue the verdict
becomes precedent. **Statute law** is passed through Parliament. A
**regulation** is delegated law, changeable without parliamentary debate.

```
ADMINISTERED IN THE TITLING FUNCTION
  Land Title Act 1994 · Land Act 1994 · Building Units and Group Titles
  Act 1980 · Body Corporate and Community Management Act 1997 · Foreign
  Ownership of Land Register Act 1988 · Aboriginal Land Act 1991 ·
  Torres Strait Islander Land Act 1991
OTHER ACTS THAT IMPACT TITLING

  Trustee Act 1978 · Coastal Protection and Management Act 1995 · Survey
  and Mapping Infrastructure Act 2003 · Planning Act 2016 · Water Act 2000
```

## 2.6 Estates in Land

**Land tenure** identifies who has the right to use and occupy land in
varying degrees of ownership. **Security of tenure** distinguishes their
value — freehold is more secure, and more valuable, than State leasehold.

| Estate | What the holder has | Sell / gift / bequeath? |
|---|---|---|
| **Fee simple (freehold)** | Land alienated from the State; the State has no claim and must acquire by negotiation or resumption with compensation | Yes — the full bundle |
| **Life estate** | Use for the duration of a person's life ("life tenant"); on death the asset goes to the **remaindermen** named in the will or deed | **No** |
| **Leasehold** | Exclusive possession for a definite term for rent. Lessee holds the **leasehold estate**, lessor the **reversion** | The leasehold estate is the lessee's asset and may be assigned in life or on death |
| **Crown / State leasehold** | Held from the State under the *Land Act 1994* | Subject to statutory conditions and Ministerial control |

A fee simple is acquired by **contract**; by **will**, or absent a will the
rules of succession in the *Succession Act 1981*; by **transfer** on an
Instrument of Transfer (s 61 LTA); or by **adverse possession** (§2.12).

> **A life estate is not a discounted fee simple.** They are used as wealth
> protection in blended families — partner X may enjoy partner Y's home for
> X's lifetime, then it is sold and passes to Y's children. If a title
> carries one, the fee simple you are valuing is a **reversionary** interest
> in a property somebody else may occupy rent-free indefinitely. Valuing it
> as vacant-possession fee simple overstates it by the whole value of the
> occupation right, and the term of that right is a life expectancy, not a
> date.

**Crown leases.** 62% of Queensland is held by the State in one form or
another and may be leased to companies or individuals; most of it falls
under Crown leasehold, which the *Land Act* calls **unallocated State
land**, and all of it could potentially be subject to a native title claim.
There is no equivalent in the UK. The **Land Act 1994** replaced the Land
Act 1962 and rationalised Crown leases into main tenure streams:

| s | Provision |
|---|---|
| 14, 15(1) | Governor in Council may grant unallocated State land in fee simple, or lease it for a term of years or in perpetuity; reserve land for a term of years only |
| 15(4), 153, 154 | A lease may state its purpose and the land must be used for it; the Minister may approve other uses |
| **155** | A term cannot exceed **50 years** — unless for a significant development or timber plantation, then **100 years** |
| 16(1), 28 | Land must be evaluated for the most appropriate tenure; dealings must be consistent with the *Native Title Act 1993* (Cth) |
| 158, 159 | Renewal on application unless prohibited; the Minister first considers public interest, environmental need, land degradation and lessee compliance |
| 199–203, 206 | Care for the land, control noxious plants, supply information, sustainability conditions; pastoral leases, no development within 2 years of expiry without approval; the lessee may be required to live there for seven years |

## 2.7 Co-ownership

**Co-ownership** is an estate or interest in land held by two or more
persons concurrently, governed by **Part V of the Property Law Act 1974,
ss 33–43**, with limited provisions in the **Land Title Act Part 5,
ss 56–57**. Section 33(1) PLA provides that any interest in any property may
be held by two or more persons as **joint tenants** or **tenants in
common** — and those are the only two. One person or corporate body holds a
**sole tenancy**.

| | Joint tenancy | Tenancy in common |
|---|---|---|
| Shares | No separate or distinct title or share | Determined by the tenants; need **not** be equal |
| Unities | All four | **Unity of possession only** |
| Survivorship | **Yes** — the deceased's interest vests in the surviving joint tenant/s | **No** — passes under the will, or by intestacy |
| Part share transfer | Not while the joint tenancy subsists | **Possible** |
| Corporations | May hold as joint tenants; on dissolution the other joint tenant remains in ownership of that share | Yes |

```
THE FOUR UNITIES — all must be present for a joint tenancy
1. POSSESSION  each party may occupy the whole of the land, and none can
               exclude the others from any part of it
2. INTEREST    each must hold the same interest in the property
3. TITLE       each must acquire through the same transaction
4. TIME        the interests must have been acquired at the same time
```

Break any of the last three and the joint tenancy is severed; a tenancy in
common survives on unity of possession alone.

> **The statutory default is tenancy in common.** Where land is clearly
> disposed of to co-owners as "joint tenants" or "tenants in common," effect
> is given to that intention — but **if there is no express intention the
> land passes as tenants in common**, s 35 PLA. Students routinely assume the
> opposite. The 80 Bertha Street title says **JOINT TENANTS** in capitals
> precisely because it has to be spelled out.

Two registered proprietors, joint tenants, on a property assessed at
$1,150,000 in June 2021:

```
AS JOINT TENANTS
  Neither has a distinct share. If one dies the survivor holds the whole:
  1 × $1,150,000. The deceased's will has NO effect on the land.

SEVERED INTO A TENANCY IN COMMON, EQUAL SHARES
  Each holds a distinct 1/2:  $1,150,000 ÷ 2 = $575,000
  On death that $575,000 passes under the will or on intestacy.

TENANTS IN COMMON IN UNEQUAL SHARES, e.g. 65/35
  65% × $1,150,000 = $747,500      35% × $1,150,000 = $402,500
  Check: 747,500 + 402,500 = 1,150,000  ✓
```

> **Survivorship beats the will, and the arithmetic is the proof.** A joint
> tenant can leave their interest to anybody in their will and it changes
> nothing — the interest vests in the survivor at the instant of death,
> before the will can operate, so the estate has nothing to distribute.
> Sever first and there is a $575,000 asset in the estate. In a
> blended-family question, one word on the title search decides who inherits.

**Rights and obligations of co-owners**, either form: rights to occupy and
the obligation to pay for sole occupancy; rights to rents or profits from a
third party; obligations to pay for improvements, maintenance and day-to-day
outgoings, and to contribute to mortgage repayments. A joint tenancy
terminates by conversion to a tenancy in severalty; a tenancy in common by
that or by statute under s 41 PLA.

> **A partnership cannot own land.** It is a legal entity but is not able to
> hold property — the *individuals* hold it, as joint tenants or tenants in
> common (s 35(2)(b) PLA). Joint tenancy is more convenient on a partner's
> death, but s 35(1) means that unless a contrary intention appears it is
> held as **tenants in common**, so the convenient outcome is the one you
> have to ask for.

## 2.8 Community Title and the Body Corporate

**Community title schemes** let buildings be subdivided into individual
lots with multiple titles issued over one parcel of land — also called
strata or group title. The history matters because old schemes are still
registered under old Acts:

```
COMPANY TITLE     A company owns the land; shareholders who buy shares are
                  entitled to exclusive occupation of a flat/unit. The buyer
                  does NOT own the property — they own a share in the company
                  that owns it. Directors can approve or deny buyers.
                  e.g. Glenfalloch, New Farm — 98 apartments, heritage listed.
                  Rare in Qld; more common in NSW and Victoria.
      ↓
Building Units Titles Act 1965   buildings into lots + common property (strata)
Group Titles Act 1973            cluster housing — townhouses
Building Units and Group Titles  buildings OR land into building lots
  Act 1980 (BUGT Act)            + common property
Mixed Use Development Act 1993   some resorts and mixed-use developments
Body Corporate and Community     the current regime
  Management Act 1997 (BCCM Act)
```

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Lot** | The individual unit — can be land |
| **Common property** | Freehold land that is part of the scheme but not part of any lot |
| **Lot entitlement** | Entitlement to share in the capital, and the formula for entitlement and levies |
| **Community management statement (CMS)** | Details the by-laws, lot entitlements and which regulation module applies |
| **Body corporate** | A **separate legal entity** responsible for the CMS |

The **BCCM Act 1997** covers creation, management, termination and
amalgamation of schemes; the body corporate's powers, functions, financial
and property matters and record-keeping; by-laws including exclusive-use
by-laws; sale of lots; and dispute resolution. Body corporate
responsibilities, run by a committee of owners: **maintain common property ·
insurance · financial management · records and information · conduct of
occupiers.**

> **Two identical units, two different levy bills.** Levies follow the **lot
> entitlement** in the CMS, not floor area — the original developer set them
> and they are only sometimes rational. Read the CMS for the entitlement
> schedule and the exclusive-use by-laws, because an "exclusive use" car
> space or courtyard is a by-law right over **common property**, not part of
> the lot on the plan. Measure it into the lot area and you have valued
> something the owner does not own.

All the forms together: **sole tenancy · joint tenants · tenants in common ·
NOT by partnership · community title / company title.** Indirect forms in
later units include property trusts, companies, A-REITs, superannuation and
joint ventures.

## 2.9 Encumbrances on Title — Easements

An **encumbrance** is an interest registered against the title that
qualifies what the owner can do: easements, mortgages, caveats, leases and
other. There is **no statutory definition** of an easement; the working
definition is "a right annexed to land to use some other person's land in a
particular manner, or to prevent owners of another land from using their
land in a particular manner."

Typical rights, not a closed list: right of way for access; support of
buildings; party (shared) walls; drainage or sewerage reticulation; water
storage or supply; retention of light or air; electricity transmission;
encroachment; eavesdrop.

```
THE FOUR ELEMENTS — Re Ellenborough Park [1956] Ch 131
1. There must be a DOMINANT and a SERVIENT tenement.
2. The easement must ACCOMMODATE the dominant tenement.
3. The dominant and servient tenement must not be held and occupied
   by the same person.
4. The right must be capable of forming THE SUBJECT MATTER OF A GRANT.
```

**Element 2** — the easement must benefit the dominant **land**, not the
dominant **owner**, making it a better or more convenient property
(*Ellenborough Park*; *Clos Farming Estates Pty Ltd v Easton* [2001] NSWSC
525). A right that is merely a commercial advantage to a business conducted
on the land is a **personal right** — *Hill v Tupper* (1863) 159 ER 51.

**Element 3** — note "**owned and occupied**". An easement is exercisable
over the land *of another* and is extinguished when dominant and servient
come into the same ownership *and* occupation. **Queensland modifies this**:
under s 86 *Land Title Act 1994* an instrument of easement may be registered
even where the lot benefited and the lot burdened have, or are to have, the
same registered owner. (The slide prints the year as "199"; it is 1994.)

**Element 4** — interests too wide or vague are not easements.

| Capable of being an easement | Too wide |
|---|---|
| A right to enjoy a park | Protection from the weather |
| A right to a general air flow | Privacy |
| Right to light, usually by **express grant** | A view; a mere right of recreation |

> **There is no easement for a view.** Nothing in the bundle of rights and
> nothing in *Ellenborough Park* protects an outlook, privacy or sunlight
> from a neighbour's lawful development. If a view contributes to value — and
> in Kalinga it does — you are valuing an amenity **contingent on what the
> neighbour has not yet built**. Say so, and check the neighbour's zoning and
> height limits before you price the view.

| | Legal easement | Equitable easement |
|---|---|---|
| Created by | In writing (s 11(1) PLA) and **registered** (s 181 LTA; s 82 LTA) | A contract for an easement before registration, or **failure** to register — *Chan v Cresdon* (1990) 64 ALJR 111 |
| Binds | The world, on registration | The parties, and the current registered proprietor via the *in personam* exception |

Under Torrens **registration is paramount**; it is unlikely a *subsequent*
registered proprietor will be bound by an unregistered easement, though see
*Pryce v McGuinness* [1966] Qd R 591. Section 180 PLA lets the court
**create** an easement on the landowner's application where it is consistent
with the public interest, the servient owner can be adequately recompensed
in money, and either the servient tenant is unreasonable or no person with
capacity to agree can be found.

```
REMEDIES for interference    abatement (minimum and positively necessary
                             only) · damages for nuisance · injunction or
                             declaration · damages in addition to an
                             injunction where damage is substantial
EXTINGUISHMENT               agreement (deed or registration) · abandonment
                             (more than non-use — intention must be shown) ·
                             consolidation of dominant and servient ·
                             alteration in use of the dominant land ·
                             s 181 PLA court order (obsolete or contrary to
                             the public interest, with compensation)
```

> **A service crossing the land is not an easement over the land.** The
> 80 Bertha Street site plan marks an "EXISTING SEWER LINE SCALED FROM DBYD
> RECORDS" running through the property, and the title search shows **no
> easement at all**. Report both consequences separately: no registered right
> burdens the title, so nothing reduces the estate; but there is a physical
> constraint on where a building can go, which is a **highest-and-best-use**
> issue. And *scaled from DBYD records* means the location is indicative, not
> surveyed.

## 2.10 Encumbrances on Title — Mortgages

A mortgage is a **secured loan**. The *Land Title Act* s 4 definition is
that a mortgage "includes a charge on a lot for securing money or money's
worth."

```
MORTGAGOR   the BORROWER  —  GIVES the mortgage
MORTGAGEE   the LENDER    —  TAKES the mortgage (e.g. the bank)
```

| Secured loan | Unsecured loan |
|---|---|
| Promise to repay **plus security** over a particular asset | Personal promise to repay only |
| Creditor **can repossess** and enforce by sale of the secured asset | Creditor **cannot** repossess; must sue, and if unsatisfied may initiate bankruptcy proceedings |

A **legal** mortgage is a charge registered over the land — generally a
first registered mortgage, though second registered mortgages exist. An
**equitable** mortgage is an agreement to grant one, an unregistered
mortgage, or a deposit of title deeds (s 74 LTA). Indefeasibility applies
once registered — s 184 LTA, *Tessman v Costello* (1987) 1 Qd R 285.

The lecture's own priority example, with the consequence worked out:

```
Frank's house                            $600,000
  1st mortgage  CBA      (purchase)      $400,000
  2nd mortgage  Westpac  (alterations)   $ 50,000
                                         ---------
  Total secured debt                     $450,000
  Frank's equity        600,000 − 450,000 = $150,000
  LVR, first mortgage   400,000 ÷ 600,000 = 66.67%
  Combined LVR          450,000 ÷ 600,000 = 75.00%

ON DEFAULT the FIRST mortgagee takes priority. Net proceeds:
  Sale $600,000   CBA $400,000 | Westpac $50,000 | Frank $150,000
  Sale $500,000   CBA $400,000 | Westpac $50,000 | Frank  $50,000
  Sale $420,000   CBA $400,000 | Westpac $20,000 | Frank       $0
                                 ↑ Westpac short $30,000
  Sale $380,000   CBA $380,000 | Westpac      $0 | Frank       $0
                                 ↑ Westpac short $50,000, CBA short $20,000
```

> **The second mortgagee absorbs the entire first slice of any value fall.**
> A 30% fall here ($600,000 → $420,000) costs the first mortgagee nothing and
> the second 60% of its loan. That asymmetry is why a second mortgage prices
> at a higher rate, and why a mortgage valuation carries a **Land (inc
> planning title)** risk rating — WBP rate 80 Bertha Street a 2 out of 5 on
> that line. The order of dealings on a title search is the distribution
> waterfall, not administrative trivia.

**Rights of the mortgagor.** The **equitable right of redemption**, enlivened
by **unconscionable conduct** in the negotiation or in the terms themselves
(*Commercial Bank of Australia v Amadio* (1983) 151 CLR 447), or by **undue
influence** — "the improper use of the ascendancy acquired by one person over
another … so that the acts of the person influenced are not in the fullest
sense of the word his free, voluntary acts" (*Union Bank of Australia v
Whitelaw* 1906). Undue influence is **actual** or **presumed**; presumed
arises from a relationship of trust and confidence (parent/child,
solicitor/client, trustee/beneficiary), and there **the onus is on the
mortgagee to rebut** it. The mortgagor also has rights to possession, to a
second mortgage, to lease, and to seek equity to redeem after the contractual
date — equity will not permit a **clog on the equity of redemption**.

**Rights of the mortgagee.** Action for recovery of the debt · take
possession (s 78 LTA) · appoint a receiver · foreclosure (s 78(2) LTA) ·
exercise the power of sale.

```
PRECONDITIONS TO THE POWER OF SALE
  s 84 PLA default → notice specifying the default and requiring its
  remedy, served on the mortgagor → default continued for 30 DAYS
  e.g. notice served 2 March 2026 → the 30 days expire 1 April 2026
```

The **duty on sale**, s 85 PLA, is reasonable care to ensure the property is
sold **at market value**; damages under s 85(3) are the difference between
market value established through evidence and the sale amount. The *Property
Law (Mortgagor Protection) Amendment Act 2008 (Qld)* added s 85(1A):
adequately advertise; obtain reliable evidence of value; maintain the
property including reasonable repairs; sell by **auction** unless another way
is appropriate; and anything else prescribed by regulation.

> **Section 85 is where the valuer gets subpoenaed.** "Reasonable care to
> ensure the property is sold at market value" is enforced by comparing the
> sale price against a market value **established through evidence** — and
> that evidence is a valuation. Damages equal the gap. A mortgagee-in-
> possession sale generates two valuations that must be reconciled: the one
> relied on before selling, and the one the mortgagor's lawyer commissions
> afterwards.

A mortgagor may seek an **injunction** to restrain the power of sale
(*Allfox Building v Bank of Melbourne* — the mortgagor succeeded on the
ability to refinance despite not paying the amount into court); intervening
after contract but before settlement requires proof of **bad faith or
impropriety**. After conveyance in exercise of the power, the purchaser's
title is not impeachable if the power was properly exercised (s 87 PLA), and
the purchaser is not to enquire whether it was (s 79, s 184 LTA).

## 2.11 Leases, Licences, Caveats and Covenants

A lease is a contract by which the lessor, as registered proprietor, grants
the lessee an estate or interest in land for a fixed term in consideration
of rent. The lessee holds the **leasehold estate**; the lessor holds the
**reversion**. The leasehold estate is the lessee's asset and may be
assigned in life or on death. **The lessor must be the registered owner of
the fee simple.** Two elements: the **right to exclusive possession**
(*Radaich v Smith* (1959)) and a **fixed or determinable term**.

| Lease | Licence |
|---|---|
| **Exclusive possession** — the tenant can control and restrict access, including from the landlord | Permission to use without exclusive possession; the owner retains control and can enter at any time |
| Fixed or determinable term | Flexible; short-term or specific-use |
| An **interest in land** | A **personal right**, not an interest in land |
| Registrable | Not registrable |

In *Radaich v Smith*, Radaich occupied a shop for five years to run a milk
bar under an agreement **labelled a licence**. The High Court held it was a
**lease**: the substance of the agreement, not its label, determines its
nature, and Radaich had exclusive possession.

> **The deck cites *Radaich v Smith* two different ways and they cannot both
> be right** — "(1959) 101 CLR 209" on two slides and "(1959) 101 CLR 109" on
> two others, once spelled "Raddaich". One of those page references will not
> take a marker to the case. Check it before it goes in an assessment; the
> *principle* (substance over label) earns the mark, not the digits.

Types: **fixed term**; **periodic tenancies** (yearly, weekly, monthly or
other); **tenancies at will**; **tenancies at sufferance**; **tenancies by
estoppel**.

```
COMMERCIAL LEASES — the registration threshold
  LONG LEASE   term EXCEEDING 3 years     → MUST be registered on title
  SHORT LEASE  term NOT EXCEEDING 3 years → registration not required

  Worked: a lease commencing 1 July 2025
    to 30 June 2028  = exactly 3 years (1,095 days)  → SHORT lease
    to  1 July 2028  = 3 years + 1 day (1,096 days)  → LONG lease, register
    to 30 June 2030  = 5 years                       → LONG lease, register
```

> **The unregistered short lease is the encumbrance you cannot search for.**
> A three-year lease need not be registered, and "short leases" is one of the
> eight **exceptions to indefeasibility** in §2.3 — so a purchaser can take a
> title reading NIL encumbrances and still be bound by a tenant with two
> years to run. This is why the inspection asks who is in occupation and on
> what terms, and why "Fee Simple **Vacant Possession**" on the WBP report is
> a stated basis of value, not a description of the world.

Two documents make a lease. The **lease document** — no proforma for
commercial leases; residential uses the **General Tenancy Agreement (Form
18a)**, retail a Retail Shop Lease. And the **lease plan**: none needed for a
whole lot, but a lease of part of a lot must include a plan identifying that
part, certified by a **licensed surveyor** if the lease is of high value or
long term. Lease-specific legislation: *Residential Tenancies and Rooming
Accommodation Act 2008* and its Regulation; *Retail Shop Leases Act 1994*
(5 or more premises used wholly or predominantly for retail); *Building
Energy Efficiency Disclosure Act 2010* (office space of 2,000 m² or more).

The 80 Bertha Street valuation assesses unfurnished market rent at $800.00
per week:

```
Annual gross rent   $800 × 52          =  $41,600
Gross yield         41,600 ÷ 1,150,000 =  3.62%
```

Leased, the owners would hold the **reversion** — and it is the reversion,
not vacant possession, that would then be the interest valued.

**Profits à prendre** are a right to remove soil, produce or animals — sand,
gravel, trees. Unlike an easement they **may be granted to any person, not
just another property owner**: there is no dominant tenement requirement.

**Caveats.** A caveat prohibits registration of an instrument affecting the
interest claimed by the **caveator** (the person in whose favour it is
lodged) against the **caveatee** (the registered proprietor), until it is
withdrawn, removed, lapses or is cancelled. Its purpose is to **allow time**
for parties to apply to the court. Caveats protect an **equitable** interest
— a purchaser under a valid unconditional contract of sale, a chargee under
an agreement specifically charging the land, an unregistered (equitable)
mortgagee, a beneficiary of a constructive, resulting or implied trust, or a
trustee in bankruptcy following vesting.

> **A caveat is a freeze, not a claim.** It does not create an interest,
> prove one, or resolve anything — it buys time to get to court. On a title
> search it is a signal to stop and ask what the underlying dispute is,
> because the *dispute* is the risk, not the caveat.

**Covenants.** A written agreement whereby one or both parties agree to do,
or refrain from doing, specific things — **positive** or **negative
(restrictive)**. Most common in large-scale developments of uniform
appearance: restricting multiple dwellings, restricting building styles and
designs, restricting fences or pools. Enforcement requires notice, and if
the covenant was on the title when the purchaser acquired the land they are
deemed to have had **constructive notice**.

> **Constructive notice means "you should have looked."** A purchaser who
> never ordered a title search is bound by the covenant as if they had read
> it. A highest-and-best-use conclusion that ignores a no-dual-occupancy
> covenant is wrong even where the zoning permits the duplex. Zoning says
> what the *council* will allow; a covenant says what the *title* will allow.

## 2.12 Encroachment and Adverse Possession

The *Property Law Act 1974* defines **encroachment** as "encroachment by a
building, including encroachment by overhang of any part as well as
encroachment by intrusion of any part in or upon the soil," and "building"
as "a substantial building of a permanent character, and includes a wall."

Remedies under **s 185 PLA**: compensation to the adjoining owner;
conveyance, transfer or lease of the subject land to the encroaching owner,
or the grant of any estate, interest, easement, right or privilege in
relation to it (*Kostis v Devitt* (1979) 1 BPR 9231); or removal of the
encroachment. Where improvements were created under a mistake, relief may be
granted under **s 197 PLA**. In exercising its discretion the court may
consider (s 185(2)) which owner applies; the situation and value of the
subject land and the nature and extent of the encroachment; the character of
the encroaching building and its purposes; the loss and damage to each owner
respectively; and the circumstances in which the encroachment was made.

> **Not every intrusion is an "encroachment" in the statutory sense.** The
> definition needs a *building* — substantial, permanent. *Ex parte Van
> Achterberg* [1984] 1 Qd R 160 concerned a fence set in concrete
> foundations, and whether that qualified was the question. A neighbour's
> eave over the boundary is an encroachment; their vegetation and their
> unfooted paling fence are a different problem with different remedies.

A squatter can acquire an estate in fee simple enforceable against the owner.

```
1. FACTUAL POSSESSION — 'open, not secret; peaceful, not by force; and
   adverse, not by the consent of the true owner'
   — Mulcahy v Curramore Pty Ltd [1974] 2 NSWLR 464
2. THE INTENTION TO POSSESS
3. OVER A PERIOD OF TIME
   Queensland: 12 YEARS — s 15 Limitation of Actions Act 1974
   e.g. possession from 1 March 2013 → expires 1 March 2025 (4,383 days)
```

| Requirement | What it means | Authority |
|---|---|---|
| **Exclusive** | Using the property as their own, not sharing possession | — |
| **Adverse** | Without consent; not under a lease or licence, nor at the same time as the owner | *Clement v Jones* |
| **Open** | Open enough to give the owner the opportunity to challenge | *Lord Advocate v Lord Lovat* |
| **Peaceful** | Undisturbed | *Shaw v Garbutt* (1996) |
| **Physical control** | Depends on the value of the land, the natural manner of using it, and the circumstances. Issues considered include **fencing** and **payment of rates** | — |

Time questions: has there been abandonment? Is there a series of possessors?
Are there relative claims? Has the documentary owner regained possession?
**Time stops running if the documentary owner regains possession** — by
commencing court proceedings *or* resuming physical/legal possession.
Registration is under **s 99 LTA**, and the Registrar has some discretionary
powers to issue a certificate of title.

> **Twelve years of use is not twelve years of possession.** The clock runs
> only while possession is adverse, so a neighbour who has mown the strip for
> twenty years *with the owner's blessing* has no claim — consent destroys
> the adversity. A fence in the wrong place plus paid rates is the pattern
> that succeeds. When an inspection finds a fence that does not match the
> survey plan, the two readings — encroachment, or a running adverse
> possession claim — have opposite consequences for who owns the land.

## 2.13 Native Title

Native title was first recognised through **Mabo (No 2)**. The framing most
often got wrong: **the common law did not create native title — it
recognised the existence of Indigenous customary law.**

The position it displaced was stated in *New South Wales v Commonwealth*
(1923): the wastelands in the colonies were owned by the British Crown,
whether by prerogative right or by the feudal principle extended to the land
on first settlement — either way "the lands of Australia became the property
of the King of England." The justifying doctrine was **terra nullius**,
"nobody's land."

*Mabo v Queensland (No 2)* [1992] HCA 23; (1992) 175 CLR 1. On 20 May 1982
Eddie Koiki Mabo, Sam Passi, David Passi, Celuia Mapo Salee and James Rice
began their claim to their lands on the island of Mer in the Torres Strait.
It was decided on 3 June 1992 — ten years later, by which time both Celuia
Mapo Salee and Eddie Mabo had died. Parliament then passed the **Native
Title Act 1993** (Cth).

```
Q  Can native title be recognised by the common law consistently with
   the feudal doctrine of tenure?
Q  If the Crown acquired full beneficial ownership of all land, could
   native title be recognised, given it came from no Crown grant?

A  The High Court CONFIRMED the reception of the Doctrine of Tenure into
   Australia — BUT the Crown did not acquire full ownership of all land.
   It acquired RADICAL TITLE: underlying title that does NOT automatically
   confer beneficial ownership, allowing the Crown to grant interests
   while recognising pre-existing rights.
```

```
NATURE       may be over land OR sea · sui generis, "of its own kind",
             in a class by itself · inalienable
EXTINGUISHED by failure to continue connection · valid exercise of
             sovereign power · permanent extinguishment
FRAMEWORK    Indigenous customary law · common law · Native Title Act 1993
             (Cth) · Racial Discrimination Act 1975 (Cth)
s 223 CLAIM  (a) rights/interests possessed under traditional law or custom
             (b) a connection with the claimed land or waters
             (c) the rights/interests recognised by the common law
```

```
LEGISLATIVE JOURNEY
  1788  Colonisation — the principle of terra nullius
  1992  Mabo v Queensland (No 2) (1992) 175 CLR 1
  1993  Native Title Act 1993 (Cth)
  1996  Wik Peoples v Queensland (1996) 187 CLR 1
  1998  Native Title Amendment Act 1998 (Cth)
  2002  WA v Ward (2002) 191 ALR 1 · Yorta Yorta v Victoria (2002) 194 ALR 538
  2003  Daniel v Western Australia [2003] FCA 666 · De Rose v SA (2003)
```

Two claims failed on **connection**. In *Yorta Yorta* the High Court held
the "tide of history" had washed away continuous observance of traditional
laws and customs, though the Victorian Government signed a co-operative
management agreement in 2004. In *De Rose* the Federal Court initially found
no continuous connection in 2002, but the Full Court overturned that on
appeal in 2003. Recent Queensland determinations include **North Keppel
Island (2021)** and **Stradbroke Island (2011)**, and **Indigenous Land Use
Agreements** are increasingly common as an alternative to litigation.

> **A Crown lease does not automatically extinguish native title.** *Wik
> Peoples v Queensland* held native title rights can **coexist** with the
> rights of pastoral leaseholders. That matters because the general principle
> of leases (§2.11) is that the lessee has **exclusive possession**, which
> would be inconsistent with anyone else's continuing rights — and the Week 3
> deck poses exactly that question. Post-*Wik* it does not follow
> automatically, which is why *Land Act 1994* s 28 requires all dealings
> under that Act to be consistent with the *Native Title Act 1993* (Cth).

## Summary

- Land is **Lot on Plan** (the RPD); the plan **prefix** tells you the
  ownership structure before you read anything else
- The **survey plan** gives boundaries, bearings, distances and **area**; the
  **title search does not state area**. For Lot 172 on RP19535 the 448 m²
  comes from four other documents, all agreeing
- Torrens is **title by registration, not registration of title** (*Real
  Property Act 1861*; ss 27, 28, 37 LTA). Indefeasibility has **eight
  exceptions** — short leases and omitted easements bind a purchaser who
  searched the title
- A title search states **rights**, not land. Nothing about area, zoning,
  condition, lawfulness or value
- Minerals stay with the Crown (*Mineral Resources Act 1989* s 8), and
  **s 51(xxxi) just terms binds the Commonwealth only**
- **Fee simple** is the full bundle; a **life estate** cannot be sold, gifted
  or bequeathed; the lessee holds the leasehold estate and the lessor the
  reversion
- **Joint tenancy** needs all four unities and carries **survivorship**,
  which beats the will. **Tenancy in common** needs only unity of possession
  and is the **statutory default** under s 35 PLA. A **partnership cannot
  hold land**
- Under the BCCM Act 1997, strata levies follow the **lot entitlement**, not
  floor area
- An **easement** must accommodate the **land** not the owner and be capable
  of forming the subject matter of a grant — so none for a view, privacy or
  weather. Queensland modifies the same-owner rule at s 86 LTA
- **Mortgagor gives, mortgagee takes.** First registered mortgage takes
  priority; the second absorbs the first slice of any value fall. The s 85
  PLA duty is reasonable care to sell at **market value**, and damages are
  the gap
- Leases **over** 3 years must be registered, which is why the unregistered
  short lease is the encumbrance you cannot search for. **Caveats** freeze
  dealings; **covenants** bind on constructive notice and can defeat a use
  the zoning allows
- **Encroachment** needs a substantial, permanent **building**. **Adverse
  possession** needs open, peaceful, adverse possession, intention to possess
  and **12 years** (s 15 *Limitation of Actions Act 1974*), registered under
  s 99 LTA
- **Native title** was **recognised**, not created, by *Mabo (No 2)*; the
  Crown holds **radical title**; and after *Wik* it can **coexist** with a
  pastoral lease
