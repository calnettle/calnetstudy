# TOPIC 5 — Economic Analysis and the Valuation Report

Weeks 8 and 11. The two halves of this topic look unrelated and are not:
Week 8 gives you the economy and the market that set the water level under
your value, and Week 11 gives you the document that has to carry that
reasoning to a reader who was not there. Both are graded on the same
thing — whether a stranger can follow your evidence to your number.

## 5.1 Where This Sits in the Valuation Process

Both decks open with the same ten-step flow, in this order:

```
1  Valuation instruction — from the client
2  Property verification
3  Property inspection            ← Week 11
4  Data sources & collection
5  Sales and market evidence      ← Topic 4
6  Economic analysis              ← Week 8
7  Market analysis                ← Week 8
8  Valuation calculations
9  Valuation report               ← Week 11
10 Valuation fee                  ("your grade for the unit")
```

The report headings **follow the inspection process**. That is the Week 11
line worth internalising: the document is not a separate creative act, it
is the field notes made legible in the order you gathered them.

Every market study, whatever the asset, answers three questions:

1. Will there be users to rent or buy the subject property?
2. How quickly, and at what rent or price, will the property be absorbed?
3. How might the property be planned or marketed to be more competitive?

And the two analyses are not the same analysis:

| | Economic analysis | Market analysis |
|---|---|---|
| Focus | Broader economic conditions | Demand and supply of a **local market segment** |
| Indicators | Economic growth (GDP), inflation, interest rates, wages growth, employment, consumer confidence, retail spending | Available stock, vacancy rate, absorption rate, days on market, average sale price |
| In the report | Section 10 | Section 11 |

> **Relevant indicators differ by property type and sector.** A vacancy
> rate is the headline number for an office tower and close to noise for a
> Kalinga freestanding house that will be owner-occupied. Copying a generic
> indicator list into a residential report is one of the Week 11 pitfalls
> ("including information that did not relate to the property"). Choose the
> four or five indicators that actually move *this* property's price, and
> say why each one matters.

## 5.2 The Capital Market and the User Market

Valuers consider the property market at two levels.

| | Capital market | User / occupier market |
|---|---|---|
| Also called | Seller market — **who's got the money** | Space market — **who's got the space** |
| What it is | Markets for buying and selling equity and debt instruments to finance long-term investment | The market in occupation of physical space |
| In residential | Equity = deposit/cash; Debt = loan/mortgage | Owners supply, tenants and buyers demand |
| Driven by | Monetary policy (interest/cash rates, money supply), fiscal policy (expenditure, tax rates), rate of return vs risk profile | Current and proposed developments, zoning, location and accessibility, construction feasibility, market capture and absorption rate, availability of mortgage |
| Output | Funds flow into or out of real estate | Occupancy and rents |

The **real estate system** links them:

```
space market rents & occupancy → cash flow
   → capital market prices it against required return given risk
   → PROPERTY VALUE
   → is value > land + construction + other costs + profit margin?
        yes → new construction → back into the space market as SUPPLY
```

The construction industry is therefore the **feedback loop** from the
capital and property markets back to the space market.

The Week 8 COVID example, as monetary vs fiscal:

```
Official cash rate slashed to 0.10%          RBA      — monetary policy
$66bn Coronavirus 'safety net'               Govt     — fiscal policy
$130bn wage subsidy payment                  Govt     — fiscal policy
Early super access up to $10,000/person      Govt     — economic policy
```

> **The feedback loop is why a strong market softens itself.** The deck's
> exact words: if the real estate market is strong, values rise *in
> anticipation of eventual new supply*, followed by market softening. The
> value rise is what triggers the construction that ends the value rise.
> Any answer that says "high demand → high prices" and stops has described
> one arrow of a loop with four.

## 5.3 Economic Indicators

Four indicators are named in the report structure itself (section 10), so
these four are non-negotiable in A3: **GDP/GSP, cash rate and interest
rates, CPI and inflation, unemployment rate.**

**GDP.** A measure of economic growth — the total production of goods and
services. Measurable in nominal terms (the dollar value of production,
mixing volume *and* price changes) or **real** terms (volume only).
Economists normally talk about real growth because it better reflects how
much a country produces at one time compared with another. **GSP** is the
state equivalent.

**Cash rate.** The interest rate on unsecured overnight loans between
banks, set by the RBA, an instrument of monetary policy. The transmission
chain the deck wants:

```
cash rate ↑ → interest rates ↑ → (a) cost of servicing a given loan ↑
                               → (b) maximum loan size ↓
                               → financing constraint tightens
                               → effective demand ↓ → prices ↓
```

**CPI and inflation.** Inflation is the percentage change in the CPI.
Australia's target is annual consumer price inflation of **2 to 3 per
cent**.

Worked from the ABS series in the unit folder (all groups, Brisbane, index
2011–12 = 100):

```
Brisbane index    Mar-24  139.2      Dec-24  140.2      Mar-25  142.9
Eight capitals    Mar-24  137.4      Dec-24  139.4      Mar-25  140.7

Brisbane annual inflation, Mar-25 on Mar-24 (base = Mar-24 index):
    142.9 ÷ 139.2 − 1 = 0.026580  →  2.66%     ABS prints 2.7   ✓
Brisbane quarterly, Mar-25 on Dec-24 (base = Dec-24 index):
    142.9 ÷ 140.2 − 1 = 0.019258  →  1.93%     ABS prints 1.9   ✓
Eight capitals annual, Mar-25 on Mar-24:
    140.7 ÷ 137.4 − 1 = 0.024017  →  2.40%     ABS prints 2.4   ✓
```

Both sit inside the 2–3% target band, and **Brisbane ran hotter than the
eight-capital average by 0.26 percentage points** — not "0.26 per cent",
and not a Brisbane inflation rate that is 10.7% higher, which is the same
fact stated in a way nobody wants. Quote the gap in percentage points.

The same series shows Brisbane prices *falling* in one quarter:

```
Brisbane, Sep-24 on Jun-24:  139.4 ÷ 140.6 − 1 = −0.0085  →  −0.85%
Only three quarterly falls in Brisbane since Mar-2015:
    Mar-20  −0.09%     Jun-20  −2.24%     Sep-24  −0.85%
```

Why the Sep-24 fall happened is not in any source in this folder, so don't
assert a cause in your report — report the movement and cite the ABS.

Now the reason a valuer cares, applied to the running case study. WBP
valued 80 Bertha Street at **$1,150,000 on 21 June 2021**:

```
CPI Brisbane   Jun-21  119.2        Mar-25  142.9
Cumulative CPI:  142.9 ÷ 119.2 − 1 = 19.88%  over 15 quarters (3.75 yrs)
Annualised:      (142.9 ÷ 119.2)^(4/15) − 1  = 4.95% p.a.

$1,150,000 × 142.9 ÷ 119.2 = $1,378,649
```

So a March-2025 sale price of **$1,378,649** would be *zero real growth* on
the 2021 valuation. Anything below that is a real-terms loss even though
the nominal number went up.

> **Nominal growth is not performance.** If you write "the property has
> appreciated 20% since 2021" without deflating, you have reported the CPI
> and called it capital growth. State whichever you mean, name the base
> period, and if you deflate, name the index and the two quarters. The deck
> makes exactly this distinction for GDP and then never applies it to
> property — apply it.

Why the level matters, per the deck. **Too high:** purchasing power falls;
workers seek larger wage rises, raising firms' costs, which may push prices
further or cut employment; spending and investment decisions are distorted;
real returns fall, and a lender who under-forecast inflation receives less
than planned; international competitiveness falls unless the currency
depreciates. **Too low:** consumers delay purchases expecting falls
(deflation), and firms that cannot cut real wages by letting nominal wages
lag inflation lay workers off instead.

**Unemployment.** The ABS definition is a three-part test — people aged 15
and over who, in the reference week, were **not employed**, *and* either
actively looked for work at any time in the four weeks up to the end of the
reference week **and** were available for work in the reference week, or
were waiting to start a job within four weeks and could have started in the
reference week.

March 2025: unemployment rate 4.0%; unemployed people up 1,500 to 614,600;
youth unemployment 9.0%. The deck states the federal government and RBA
have ascribed to a target unemployment rate of **4.5–5%**, on the belief
that anything lower would set off accelerating wage and price inflation.

> **Note the direction of that target before you write it down.** A 4.0%
> actual against a 4.5–5% target means unemployment is *below* target — an
> inflation risk in the deck's own framing, not a good-news line. Students
> routinely write "unemployment is strong at 4.0%, supporting housing
> demand" and miss that the same figure is the argument for rates staying
> higher for longer, which cuts borrowing capacity.

**Government policy** is the fifth indicator in practice. Tax structure and
administration costs; whether assessment is on improved or unimproved land;
property taxes (stamp duty, land tax, council rates); special assessment
taxes for public services; and grants and incentive schemes.

The Queensland examples in the deck, with the arithmetic reconstructed:

```
First home owner grant  $30,000  for contracts 20 Nov 2023 – 30 Jun 2025
                        reverts to $15,000 after 30 Jun 2025      = −50%

Home concession   "saves you up to $7,175"
  Concession = $1.00 per $100 on the first $350,000, general rates above
  General duty on $350,000 = $1,050 + 3.5% × ($350,000 − $75,000) = $10,675
  Concessional on $350,000 = $350,000 ÷ 100 × $1              =  $3,500
  Saving                                                       =  $7,175  ✓

First home concession  "applies to a home valued under $800,000
                        and can save you up to $24,525"

                           = $17,325 + $7,200 = $24,525                ✓
```

> **The $24,525 is the duty on a $700,000 home, not an $800,000 one.** The
> maximum saving and the value cap are different numbers on the same slide
> and the deck reads as though they are the same one. $24,525 is exactly the
> full general transfer duty on $700,000, reproduced to the dollar — so
> $700,000 is where the concession still zeroes the duty and $800,000 is
> where it has phased out entirely. Note too that the home concession saving
> is a flat $7,175 for *any* home over $350,000, because the concession only
> touches the first $350,000. The duty schedule is not printed on the slide;
> it is reverse-engineered here from the two savings figures, both of which
> it reproduces exactly. Confirm current rates on the QRO site before
> quoting them in A3.

Two more policies in the folder: the temporary ban on foreign purchases of
established dwellings announced 16 February 2025, running **1 April 2025 to
31 March 2027**; and the one-off **20% reduction to all HELP and other
student loan debts** applied before indexation on 1 June 2025 ($27,600
average balance × 0.20 = **$5,520**). The second is a housing indicator
because HELP debt reduces assessable borrowing capacity.

## 5.4 Leading and Lagging Indicators

> **Neither deck uses the words "leading" or "lagging".** The classification
> below is standard economics applied to the deck's own indicator list, not
> lecturer content. It is useful for structuring section 10 of the report
> and for an exam question that asks *why* you weight one indicator over
> another — but do not cite it as though Dr Chia said it.

| Type | Indicator | Why |
|---|---|---|
| **Leading** | Building approvals, dwelling commencements | Signal supply 12–24 months out, before any of it exists |
| **Leading** | New loan commitments for dwellings | Finance is committed before settlement, so it front-runs sales volume |
| **Leading** | Consumer confidence | Sentiment moves before the transaction |
| **Leading** | Cash rate | Policy is set to act on the economy ahead of it |
| **Coincident** | Auction clearance rate, days on market, listings, vacancy rate | Measure conditions as they happen |
| **Coincident** | Median price, Home Value Index | Track the market it is measuring |
| **Lagging** | Unemployment rate | Employers cut and hire after conditions change |
| **Lagging** | CPI / inflation | Published quarterly, describing a quarter already finished |
| **Lagging** | GDP / GSP | Quarterly and revised; the newest print is already old |

The practical consequence: valuers conduct a **technical analysis**
describing *historical* data rather than future projection. Lean on lagging
and coincident indicators for what *is* happening and use leading
indicators only for what is *likely* — and make that distinction visible in
the wording.

## 5.5 Market Analysis — Demand

Market analysis is the study of the demand and supply of a property market,
founded on the analysis of **five factors that create value**:

1. Utility of improvement
2. Scarcity — estimated absorption rate
3. Desirability of improvement by end users
4. Effective purchasing power
5. Highest and best use

And it is done at four geographic levels, in this order, which is also the
required structure of report section 11: **Australia → Queensland → local
government area → suburb.**

Housing demand drivers, as listed: income levels; demographics (an ageing
population, more single-person households); consumer confidence; location
(desirable neighbourhoods, good schools); housing supply (a shortage lifts
demand-side pressure); affordability (prices, taxes, interest rates).

Demographic analysis answers "who is your target market?" — resident
population (size, density, distribution), number of households (people per
dwelling), household composition (lone-person, couple, group, nuclear
family), population structure (sex and age distribution), race and
ethnicity trends. **Include only those indicators that will influence the
value of the property.**

Affordability, from CoreLogic and ANZ (November 2024):

```
Median dwelling value to income ratio          8.0
20-year average                                6.7
   8.0 ÷ 6.7 − 1 = 19.40% above the 20-year average (base = 6.7)
   — and equal to the record highs set in early 2022

Years to save a 20% deposit, saving 15% of income p.a.:
   deposit needed = 8.0 × 0.20 = 1.60 years of gross income
   1.60 ÷ 0.15 = 10.67 years          deck states 10.6   ✓ consistent
   (10.6 exactly implies a ratio of 7.95, i.e. the unrounded 8.0)

Share of median household income to service a new home loan,
at current average owner-occupier rates, 20% deposit, 25-year term: 50.6%
```

That is a rare case where two independently quoted statistics reconcile
against each other, and it is worth showing the working in a report for
exactly that reason.

> **A value-to-income ratio is not an affordability rate.** 8.0 is a
> multiple of annual gross household income, 50.6% is a share of income
> going to a mortgage, and 10.6 years is a savings duration. Three
> different denominators. If you write "affordability is at 8.0" the marker
> cannot tell which one you mean, and the Week 11 pitfall "not providing the
> source of data used" is one sentence away.

## 5.6 Market Analysis — Supply

Supply analysis counts what competes directly with the subject property:
existing dwellings; dwellings under construction; dwellings added or
removed through conversion (change of use); development projects in
planning or DA approval — **with care, because some will never be built**;
and dwellings demolished or lost to natural hazard such as fire and flood.

The comparative factors must meet four requirements: **reliability,
recentness, uniformity, specificity.**

In the short run, property markets show demand and supply lags producing
disequilibrium, often in response to the availability of finance. Supply is
relatively fixed and responds slowly; loss of existing stock can exceed the
rate of construction; prices respond to demand, rising *before* new
construction begins. In the long run, markets show successive expansion,
decline, recession and recovery responding to employment rates, population
growth, household income and consumer preferences.

## 5.7 The Property Cycle and the Property Clock

A **property cycle** is a sequence of recurrent events reflected in
demographic, economic and emotional factors that affect supply and demand
for property, subsequently influencing the property market. The **property
clock** is a method of tracking that cycle, based on the recognised
stages: a boom, a downswing in prices, a bust as the market hits bottom,
and a recovery period building towards the next boom.

The four quadrants as the deck names them, with its own supply and demand
readings:

| Phase | Supply | Demand | What you observe |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Boom** | Mid–High | **High** | Prices grow rapidly, demand peaks, clearance rates soar; popular time to sell |
| **Downturn** | **High** | Mid–Low | Demand drops while developer supply stays high; vacancy rates up, rents declining, prices commonly fall; developers face redundancies and financial vulnerability |
| **Stabilisation** | Mid | Mid | Market adjusts to excess supply and reduced demand; interest rates tend to fall; values begin a gradual recovery; opportune time to buy on lower prices and less competition |
| **Upturn** | Mid | **High** | Buyers return, competition increases, vacancy rates fall, rents and prices rise; developers start major projects aiming to finish as the boom hits |

Read that table as a loop and the mechanism is visible: supply is *highest*
when demand is lowest, because supply was commissioned in the boom and
arrives in the downturn. That lag is the cycle.

> **The deck gives two incompatible cycle lengths on the same slide.**
> "Conventionally, property cycles last 7 to 10 years," and then the Matusik
> chart showing the overall Australian property market "has peaked every 4
> years or so" since 1980. Over the 45 years from 1980 to 2025 those imply
> roughly 4.5–6.4 cycles or roughly 11 cycles — you cannot cite both. The
> defensible line is that 7–10 years is the textbook convention for a full
> peak-to-peak cycle, while the 4-year figure counts local peaks within it.
> Say which you are using and why; confirm with your tutor before an exam.

The deck's own three-phase Australian narrative:

| | What happened |
|---|---|
| **Boom 2020–21** | Pandemic years. Historically low interest rates, high demand, government incentives. A change in the "want" — home became the place to work, play and self-isolate, driving upgrades. Big price rises left most existing owners on substantial equity |
| **Downturn 2022** | The boom ended as prices soared and affordability deteriorated. Prices surged around 26% overall while wage growth rose 2.3%. Inflation soaring, deposits harder to save |
| **Reset 2023** | Market bottomed early in the year *despite rates still rising*. Demand booming on surging migration and returning international students, competing with locals in the rental market, driving rents higher and spilling into prices. Dwelling supply lagging, with building approvals for new homes at a decade low |

> **26% and 2.3% are not the same kind of number.** The 26% is a cumulative
> price rise across the boom; the 2.3% reads as an annual wage growth rate.
> Setting them side by side implies prices outran wages 11-fold, which is
> not what the data says. If you use this comparison, put both on the same
> basis — cumulative against cumulative, or annual against annual — and name
> the period. The deck also states the long-run average annual growth rate
> of the Australian median house price is **7.62%**, which doubles a price
> in ln(2) ÷ ln(1.0762) = **9.44 years**; that is the annual figure to
> compare a wage series against.

One more line worth holding: cyclical downturns often do **not** lead to
long-term improvements in housing affordability, because the declines
eventually attract new buyers, and in the absence of a material increase in
housing supply that pushes values higher.

## 5.8 Describing the Data

Valuers conduct a **technical analysis** for residential property, which
means describing *historical* data rather than projecting the future. Three
things to describe, and the vocabulary the deck wants:

```
PATTERNS   Sales volume, growth rate
TRENDS     Price increase or decrease — and the OVERALL trend, which may
           run opposite to the most recent segment
RANGE      Sideways trading ranges
```

The distinction between a trend and the overall trend is the whole point of
the slide: a six-month fall inside a five-year rise is a trend, not the
trend. Chart both, and say which window each statement describes.

## 5.9 The Inspection Procedure

The inspection and the field notes taken **form the basis for your
valuation report**. IVSC: sufficient evidence must be assembled by means
such as inspection, inquiry, computation and analysis to ensure the
valuation is properly supported. **Field notes may be called as evidence in
court.** You generally go to a property once, so develop a routine.

Valuation standards on inspections, four rules:

1. Inspections must always be carried out to the extent necessary to
   produce a valuation professionally adequate for its purpose
2. Take reasonable steps to verify the information relied on, and clarify
   any necessary assumptions with the client if not already agreed
3. **Any limitations or restrictions on the inspection must be identified
   and recorded in the report**
4. If relevant information is not available, any necessary assumptions made
   must be identified and recorded in the report

Before that, the instruction. Written instructions — letter of appointment,
or standing instructions under a panel appointment — must settle the basis
of valuation and **who the intended user is: who will be relying on the
report, and who you are legally responsible to.** Written confirmation
covers: interest valued; purpose; date of valuation; scope of work;
internal or external inspection; agreed fee or basis for it; information to
be provided by the client; the parties the valuation is prepared for;
timeframe; access arrangements; and where applicable the purchase price and
selling agent.

Competency is a separate gate: is the valuer qualified, experienced in this
type of work, covered by their level of registration, covered by their
level of indemnity insurance, and do they have access to the required data?

```
                       impact on demand, street appeal, services connected
                       or available, positive and negative street factors,
                       access/egress, noise, aspect, views.
                       THINK: why do people live here?

AT THE SUBJECT         1. Confirm land and site details
                       2. External building inspection
                       3. Internal building inspection
                       4. Ground and other improvements
                       Take lots of photos and notes. Be methodical.
```

Step 1 in full: confirm the **address** (that you have the correct
property); the current survey plan — real property description, frontage
and dimensions, easements and encumbrances; physical characteristics (size,
shape, topography, aspect, views, accessibility); utilities (electricity,
sewer, gas, water, telephone, NBN); whether the use is consistent with the
zoning; environmental factors including flooding, wildlife, proximity to
protected areas and other overlays. **Always include a location plan and
cadastral map with the subject identified.**

Steps 2–4 repeat Topic 1 exactly — external ("describe the house as if
you're standing on the street"), internal ("as if you're standing at the
front door"), then ground improvements — recording **materials**,
**condition** (poor / fair / good / new; unrenovated or newly renovated)
and **quality** (poor / average / good) for each.

The A1 external inspection checklist in the unit folder is the field
instrument: one row per element (foundations/sub-floor, structure,
façade/frame, roof, approximate age, design style, doors and screens,
windows, driveway, footpaths, garage/carport, outbuildings, ground
improvements, external stairs and balustrades, other features, utilities)
against three columns — **construction material/type**, **condition (good /
fair / poor)** and **notes**. The completed sample records a 1930s
Queenslander: concrete slab, double gable, weatherboard, tin roof,
double-hung windows, concrete driveway, paved tile footpaths.

> **Take detailed measurements at the inspection or you cannot finish the
> report.** The deck gives four reasons and the last two are graded: they
> verify your assumptions, add detail to file notes, are **needed for ratio
> analysis** (Topic 1 — LBR, FSR, SCR), and are **needed for the summation
> approach** if you use it. You do not get a second visit. A missing carport
> dimension in April is a missing secondary valuation method in June.

## 5.10 The 17-Section Valuation Report

A valuation report is a **legal document**. It must comply with
professional body and legislative requirements — Australian Property
Institute, RICS, and the **Valuers Registration Board Queensland**. It
must be accurate, complete, consistent and reliable; it is an analysis of
the subject property and the relevant market; and it is **an unbiased view,
not a sales pitch.**

Most residential valuations today are **short-form** — pro-forma reports
such as ValuePRO or in-house formats, "tick and flick", used extensively
for standard residential mortgage work. **Full** residential reports are
required for family law matters, resumption, litigation and non-standard
mortgage situations; full reports are standard for non-residential and
commercial work and can run to 100 pages. A3 is a full report.

The structure, exactly as both the Week 8 and Week 11 decks print it —
and they agree, which is worth knowing because much of this unit's
material does not:

| # | Section | Contents |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Valuation summary** | Property details, purpose, instructions, location, improvements, market commentary, valuation figure **in dollars and words**, valuation date, valuer name, qualifications and signature. Generally 1 page for residential |
| 2 | **Introduction / Instructions** | Instructing party; interest valued (freehold, leasehold); basis of valuation (market value, insurance valuation); report compliance with API and/or RICS standards; date of valuation. Define market value **with its reference** |
| 3 | **Title details** | Address, lot and plan, parish/county if on title, LGA, registered proprietors. Current Title Search always in annexures, often the registered plan and easement plans too. Note all easements, covenants, mortgages and other encumbrances visible on inspection or on title, **regardless of whether they impact value** — and discuss those that could |
| 4 | **Location analysis** | Street and suburb value drivers; distance **and direction** to CBD, transport, shops, schools, shopping centres, and services applicable to the land use; surrounding development with comment on size, construction, quality and condition |
| 5 | **Environmental issues** | Flooding (FloodWise map in annexures), bushfire risk (council overlay), potential asbestos, land contamination (contaminated land search), undue noise or air pollution, nature reserve / koala habitat / other planning constraints |
| 6 | **Town planning — highest and best use** | Planning scheme designation and summary of permissible development including overlays; alternative use potential (HABU *legally permissible*); heritage and cultural aspects; comment on zoning aspects with direct or indirect influence on value; zoning map where relevant |

| 8 | **Improvement descriptions** | External and internal. Photographs in text or annexures, **labelled**. Measurements summarised in text with analysis. Plan annexed |

## 5.11 Disclaimers and Qualification Statements — ANZPGP-201

API/PINZ/NZIV Guidance Paper **ANZPGP 201**, published 20 April 2023,
effective **1 July 2023**, replacing the version in effect from 1 July 2021.
A Guidance Paper is not a standard: it clarifies professional and industry
processes and serves as a guide and measure of acceptable professional
practice. **A court or tribunal may consider its contents in deciding
whether a Member acted to the standard required by law.**

Two different instruments, routinely confused:

| | **Third-party disclaimer** | **Qualification** |
|---|---|---|
| Purpose | Restricts **who may rely** on the report and **for what purpose**, and excludes everyone else | Alerts the reader to assumptions or issues that **may affect the conclusions** |
| Answers | "Who is this for?" | "What is this opinion contingent on?" |
| Comprises | One clause covering use, permitted parties, and exclusion of any other party | **Assumptions and special assumptions, conditions, limitations, warnings** |
| Where | Prominent position near the conclusions, and in the instruction recital | Immediately adjacent to the information it qualifies |

Defined terms worth memorising verbatim:

- **Client** — the entity or person for whom the report is undertaken and
  who is permitted to use it under the retainer agreement or instruction
- **Intended user(s)** — any entity or person, **including the client**,
  *identified by name* as permitted to use the report
- **Third party** — anyone other than the client or other intended users
- **Assumption** — an assumed fact that **is** (or could be) consistent with
  a fact existing at the date of valuation; used to alert the user that the
  conclusion relied on information the Member has not verified
- **Special assumption** — an assumed fact that **is not** consistent with,
  or differs from, a fact existing at the date of valuation; the conclusion
  is contingent on a change in circumstances, or reflects a different
  perspective than market participants would normally take
- **Condition** — the conclusion is based on something occurring or having
  occurred
- **Limitation** — the conclusion is restricted because information,
  investigations or procedures were unavailable or not undertaken
- **Warning** — alerts the user that caution is advised before using the
  report, so they can make an informed decision about what they need to do
  before relying on it

> **Assumption vs special assumption turns on one word: consistent.** An
> assumption is consistent with the facts at valuation date (you just did
> not verify it). A special assumption is *not* — it is counterfactual. "We
> have assumed the property is not contaminated" is an assumption. "We have
> valued as if complete" is a **special assumption**, because the building
> does not exist on the date of valuation. IVS requires all significant and
> material assumptions and special assumptions to be disclosed, and
> materiality is the valuer's professional judgement.

### What a disclaimer can and cannot do

A Member owes a duty of care to the client, **and can also owe one to a
third party who receives and relies on the report.** A disclaimer's
intention is to minimise exposure to third-party claims — to expressly
disclaim legal responsibility if an unauthorised party relies on the report
or it is used for an unauthorised purpose.

To be **effective** it must be brought to the attention of a third party
who comes into possession of the report, and make clear to a reasonable
person in that party's position that the report is not intended for them
and they should not rely on it at all.

Drafting principles — an effective third-party disclaimer:

1. **Specifically relates to the circumstances** of the particular advice,
   reflecting the instructions and purpose, and clearly articulating who the
   intended recipients are
2. Is in a **prominent position**, near the conclusions, under a heading
   like "Third-Party Disclaimer" or "Use of this Report", in the same font
   size as the rest of the report — or if smaller, bold or italicised and
   still reasonably legible. **Never in small print at the foot of a page**
3. Is included in the **instruction recital paragraph** and near the content
   it applies to
4. Uses clear, concise wording to avoid ambiguity

Qualification drafting principles are the mirror image: immediately
adjacent to what they qualify; appropriate to the circumstances of the
instruction; **containing nothing irrelevant** (customise, don't paste);
and clearly worded.

> **Ambiguity is construed against you.** ANZPGP-201 says it twice, once for
> disclaimers and once for qualifications: where there is any ambiguity in
> meaning, intention or interpretation, "a Court will almost invariably
> interpret such a clause in a manner which is **least helpful to the party
> seeking to rely on** that clause." That is the whole argument for the
> objective-writing rule from Topic 1, restated as a liability rule. A vague
> disclaimer is worse than none, because it cost you the drafting time and
> still fails.

Four more limits the guidance paper is explicit about:

- **A disclaimer may not survive consumer law.** Members may still face
  liability to clients, intended users and third parties under the
  Australian Consumer Law (Competition and Consumer Act 2010), state Fair
  Trading Acts, and NZ's Fair Trading Act 1986. **"It may not always be
  possible to limit one's liability under consumer law."**
- **Under Corporations Law, a disclaimer in an "Expert" report may be
  struck down**, and by its inclusion may itself be found misleading and
  deceptive. Seek legal advice for Expert reports.
- **A retainer clause binds the client, not the world.** A disclaimer in the
  retainer agreement is contractually binding only on the parties to it, but
  its *existence* — even if unknown to the third party — is a relevant
  factor for a court deciding whether it is "fair, just and reasonable" to
  impose a duty of care on the Member. Put it in **both** the retainer and
  the report; the report copy is what gives greater protection, by bringing
  the limits to the attention of a third party who obtains access.
- **A qualification is not a substitute for doing the work.** Qualification
  statements "should not be used as a substitute for a Member's own
  reasonable enquiries, investigations and procedures including
  verification of information."

The paper also notes that a third-party disclaimer **in the form
universally used by most Members**, and as may be required by some
insurers, may not always provide the intended protection — citing *BT v
Raine and Horne* [1983] 3 NSWLR 221 and *AFP Properties v Kestrel Holdings*
[2007] FCA 1561. The small-print point comes from *Butcher v Lachlan Elder
Realty* [2004] 212 ALR 357.

Seven situations where a qualification should be considered: source of
instructions; limitations on the scope of the Member's contract;
limitations on access; sources of information (tenants, solicitors, council
officers, agents, other experts); extent of independent verification; the
client's willingness to obtain information only they can get; and **reports
prepared by other experts** — where Members should never, except in
exceptional circumstances, adopt, verify or otherwise agree with the truth
or accuracy of another expert's report (*Yorke v Lucas* (1985) 158 CLR 661).

Sample qualifications given, all usable in a residential A3: site survey,
town planning, environmental/contamination, asbestos, **pest affectation
(termites)**, right to review, improvements, tenancy details, value as if
complete, and GST.

> **The improvements qualification is the one every residential report
> needs.** You inspected readily accessible parts. You did not sight an
> engineer's structural survey. You are not a structural expert and cannot
> certify soundness. You did not inspect unexposed or inaccessible portions,
> so you cannot comment on structural integrity, defect, rot, infestation,
> or the use of asbestos or other hazardous materials. Without that
> paragraph, a condition rating of "good" reads as a certification you are
> not qualified to give.

## 5.12 Common Pitfalls in A3

Straight from the deck's review of last year's cohort, grouped.

**Terminology and language**

- Radial kilometres quoted without the **direction**
- Distance to CBD and other location aspects without **actual distances**
- Subjective marketing language instead of objective factual terminology
  (listed twice, in two separate slides — read that as emphasis)

**Completeness**

- Valuation instructions incomplete
- Not covering all the required headings
- Not including the **reference** for the definition of market value
- Only one valuation method used
- Not providing the full sales analysis calculations, or the methodology
  for the secondary valuation
- Not providing the **source of data** used for valuation calculations
- Not including full field notes

**Accuracy and consistency**

- Incorrect date of inspection
- **Valuation figures not matching calculations and the final figure**
- Inaccurate description of the property, particularly construction and
  services
- Application of sales evidence without supporting rationale
- Sections written independently so the report is not seamless
- Page numbering missing, or not matching the table of contents
- Spelling, grammar, word choice and sentence structure

**Relevance**

- Including information that did not relate to the property or the
  valuation method adopted
- Location maps with **no indication of the subject property**
- Using assignments from previous years that do not reflect current market
  conditions

And the general instructions: read the marking rubric and allocate your
time and word count accordingly; address all requirements; include all
calculations in full detail in the annexures; ensure all assumptions are
supported; reference your data sources.

The seven Cs of a good report: **clear, concise, complete, compelling,
conforming, consistent** — and it leads the reader to the same conclusions
as the writer. Professional writing rules: avoid jargon, clichés and text
talk; use objective, non-emotive language; **avoid trade or brand names**;
understand your audience on technical matters; and remember you are valuing
the property, not selling it.

## Summary

- The valuation process runs instruction → verification → inspection →
  data → sales evidence → **economic analysis → market analysis** →
  calculations → report → fee, and the report headings follow it
- **Capital market** = seller market, who's got the money (equity + debt).
  **User/occupier market** = space market, who's got the space. Construction
  is the feedback loop between them — a strong market builds the supply that
  softens it
- Economic analysis = broad conditions (GDP/GSP, cash rate, CPI, unemployment
  — the four the report demands). Market analysis = local demand and supply
  (stock, vacancy, absorption, days on market, average sale price), at four
  levels: Australia → Queensland → LGA → suburb
- Inflation target **2–3%**. Brisbane's March-2025 annual rate was
  142.9 ÷ 139.2 − 1 = **2.66%** against 2.40% for the eight capitals. Deflate
  before you call a nominal rise "growth"
- Unemployment target **4.5–5%**; March 2025 actual 4.0%, youth 9.0%.
  Below target is an inflation argument, not only a demand argument
- **Property clock:** boom (supply mid–high, demand high) → downturn (supply
  high, demand mid–low) → stabilisation (mid/mid) → upturn (supply mid,
  demand high). Supply peaks when demand troughs, because of the build lag
- Supply analysis counts existing, under construction, converted, in DA
  (with care) and demolished/lost stock, and must be reliable, recent,
  uniform and specific
- The inspection is the report. Field notes may be called as evidence in
  court, you get one visit, and **every limitation on the inspection must be
  recorded in the report**
- Learn the **17 sections in order**. The Gladstone exemplar has 20 in a
  different order with no economic analysis section — use it for tone, not
  structure
- A **third-party disclaimer** controls *who may rely and for what*; a
  **qualification** (assumption, special assumption, condition, limitation,
  warning) controls *what the opinion is contingent on*. Neither can
  substitute for doing the work, and ambiguity in either is read against you
- The pitfall that recurs across every source, including a real Opteon
  report: **figures that do not match the calculations behind them.**
  Reconcile before you submit
