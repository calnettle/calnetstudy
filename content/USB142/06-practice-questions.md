## Part A — Property Characteristics and Measurement

**Q1.** Name the three characteristic sets used to analyse property, and
give the components of each.

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

- **Physical** — land (shape, size, topography, views, exposure, soil,
  bearing capacity) and improvements (TO the land: drainage, filling,
  clearing, levelling; ON the land: structures, fencing, attachments).
- **Institutional** — rights derived from sources of law. The tenure
  system (use, alienate, assimilate, pass by succession, claim title),
  the Crown's prior right to resume, and laws restricting rights (health,
  town planning, building, heritage). **Rights available to be traded =
  the tenurial system *less* restrictions imposed at law.**
- **Economic** — immobility, large economic units, durability, scarcity,
  land unproductive on its own.

</details>

**Q2.** "Property is scarce because there is only so much land." Criticise
that statement.

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

It confuses **physical supply** with **economic supply**. Physically, land
is finite. Economically, supply is far tighter, because uses have
locational requirements — a use cannot occupy a site that does not meet
them. There is plenty of land in Queensland and almost none in Kalinga
that a Kalinga buyer would consider. Scarcity in the economic sense
creates competition between uses; **the market sets the price and the
valuer attempts to predict it.**

</details>

**Q3.** Why is *demand* the more important determinant of price in the
short run?

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Durability plus scarcity. Improvements are durable and construction is
slow, so stock cannot respond to an upsurge in demand within the period
that matters. Supply is effectively fixed in the short run, so price moves
with demand. This is also the mechanism behind the long supply lags,
short-term price spikes and windfall profits for existing holders in
Hefferan's list.

</details>

**Q4.** A dwelling has a ground floor of 118.819 m², a first floor of
96.160 m², an alfresco of 25.655 m², a carport of 33.750 m², gardens of
14.380 m² and fencing of 19.920 m², on a site of 448 m². Calculate LBR,
FSR and SCR, and state what you included in each.

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

```
GFA  = 118.819 + 96.160                  = 214.979 m²

LBR  = GBA (footprint) ÷ land
     = 118.819 ÷ 448   = 0.265221        = 1 : 0.2652  →  26.52%

FSR  = GFA (all levels) ÷ land
     = 214.979 ÷ 448   = 0.479864        = 1 : 0.4799  →  47.99%

SCR  = (footprint + covering improvements) ÷ land
     = (118.819 + 25.655 + 33.750) ÷ 448
     = 178.224 ÷ 448   = 0.397821        = 1 : 0.3978  →  39.78%
```

**LBR** uses the *footprint* only — the ground floor, not the GFA.
**FSR** sums every level, which is why it is nearly double the LBR on a
two-storey house. **SCR** includes the dwelling footprint, alfresco and
carport because those are structures that cover the site; it excludes
gardens and fencing, which cover nothing.

Say what you counted. Three different published site-coverage figures
exist for this house (47.44%, 39.78%, 42.4%) precisely because nobody
stated their inclusions.

</details>

**Q5.** The unit's own mini-quiz asks: "What is the Floor Space Ratio if
the total floor area is 215 m² and the site is 448 m²? A. 26.5% B. 47.98%
C. 39.78% D. 61.24%". Answer it, and identify what each distractor is.

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**B**, but the arithmetic is 215 ÷ 448 = **47.99%**, not 47.98%. The quiz
key truncates rather than rounds. Every distractor is a real ratio for the
same house:

```
26.52%  = LBR                          118.819 ÷ 448
47.99%  = FSR  ← the answer            214.979 ÷ 448
39.78%  = SCR, structures only         178.224 ÷ 448
61.25%  = (GFA + alfresco + carport)   274.384 ÷ 448
```

The quiz prints 61.24% for what is actually 61.25%. Same truncation. In a
report, an inconsistent rounding convention is one of the Week 11 common
pitfalls — figures not matching calculations.

</details>

**Q6.** A single-storey dwelling has GBA 180 m² and GFA 168 m² on a 600 m²
lot. Calculate both ratios and explain why GFA is the smaller figure.

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

```
LBR = 180 ÷ 600 = 0.30   →  1 : 0.30  →  30%
FSR = 168 ÷ 600 = 0.28   →  1 : 0.28  →  28%
```

GBA is measured to the **outside** of the external walls; GFA is measured
to the **internal** face. On a single-storey building the difference is
the wall thickness around the perimeter, plus any covered area GFA
excludes (verandahs, decks, carports). On a *multi*-storey building GFA
would normally exceed GBA, because GFA sums every level while GBA is a
single footprint.

</details>

**Q7.** Convert to a 1:100 scale drawing: a room measuring 6.4 m by
3.75 m. Then state the one thing that must appear on the sheet.

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

```
6.40 m × 1000 = 6,400 mm  ÷ 100 = 64.0 mm
3.75 m × 1000 = 3,750 mm  ÷ 100 = 37.5 mm

Drawn: 64.0 mm × 37.5 mm
```

The **scale must be indicated on the drawing**. A plan can be photographed
or resized and the scale silently breaks; a written scale and annotated
dimensions survive it. IPMS fundamental 3 prefers annotated dimensions
over scaling, which is why the 80 Bertha Street construction plan prints
"USE DIMENSIONS FOR CONSTRUCTION — DO NOT SCALE FROM PLAN."

</details>

**Q8.** Rewrite objectively: "An immaculate family sanctuary boasting a
sun-filled open plan living zone and stunning entertainer's terrace."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

"A dwelling with open-plan living, dining and kitchen on the ground floor,
opening to a covered terrace of approximately [x] m² on the northern
elevation."

The markers being lost: *immaculate*, *sanctuary*, *boasting*, *sun-filled*,

</details>

## Part B — Legal Interests in Property

**Q9.** Two people buy a house together. Set out the differences between
holding it as joint tenants and as tenants in common, and state what
happens if the transfer document says nothing.

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

| | Joint tenancy | Tenancy in common |
|---|---|---|
| Shares | No separate or distinct share | Shares determined by the tenants, need not be equal |
| Unities required | All four — possession, interest, title, time | Unity of **possession** only |
| Death of a co-owner | **Right of survivorship** — the deceased's interest vests in the survivor(s) | No survivorship — the interest passes under the will or on intestacy |
| Dealing with part | Cannot transfer a distinct share | Transfers of part shares are possible |

If there is no express intention, the land passes as **tenants in common**
— s35 Property Law Act 1974. The default is the opposite of what most
students guess.

</details>

**Q10.** Name the four unities and say which single unity a tenancy in
common still requires.

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

1. **Unity of possession** — each party is entitled to occupy the whole of
   the land and none can exclude the others from any part.
2. **Unity of interest** — each party holds the same interest.
3. **Unity of title** — each acquired their interest through the same
   transaction.
4. **Unity of time** — the interests were acquired at the same time.

A tenancy in common requires **unity of possession** only. Break any of
the other three and a joint tenancy cannot exist.

</details>

**Q11.** Can a partnership hold real property? Explain.

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**No.** A partnership is a legal entity but is not able to hold property.
The individuals in the partnership hold it as joint tenants or tenants in
common (PLA s35(2)(b)). Holding as **joint tenants** is more convenient on
the death of a partner, because survivorship carries the interest across
without an estate administration. But under PLA s35(1), unless a contrary
intention appears, the property is held as **tenants in common** — so the
convenient outcome only happens if it is expressly stated.

</details>

**Q12.** State the four elements of an easement and the case they come
from. Then explain what Queensland changed.

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

From *Re Ellenborough Park* [1956] Ch 131:

1. There must be a **dominant and a servient tenement**.
2. The easement must **accommodate the dominant tenement** — it must make
   the dominant *land* (not its owner) better or more convenient.
3. The dominant and servient tenements **must not be held and occupied by
   the same person**.
4. The right must be **capable of forming the subject matter of a grant** —
   not too wide or vague.

Queensland modifies element 3. Under **s86 Land Title Act 1994**, an
instrument of easement may be registered even where the lot benefited and
the lot burdened have, or are to have, the **same registered owner**. That
is how a developer registers easements across a subdivision before selling
any lot.

</details>

**Q13.** Which of these can be an easement — protection from weather, a
right to a view, a right to enjoy a park, a right to general air flow, a
right to privacy?

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**A right to enjoy a park** and **a right to a general air flow** —
sufficient. Protection from weather, a view, privacy and a mere right of
recreation are **too wide or vague** to form the subject matter of a
grant. A right to light is possible but usually by express grant.

Compare *Hill v Tupper* (1863): a commercial advantage of a business
conducted on the land is a **personal right**, not an easement. The test
is whether the right connects with the land — the better enjoyment of the
parcel itself.

</details>

**Q14.** Frank owns a house worth $600,000. He has a first mortgage to CBA
of $400,000 and a second to Westpac of $50,000. The property sells under
power of sale for $430,000 net of costs. Who gets what, and what duty did
the selling mortgagee owe?

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

```
Net sale proceeds                       $430,000
Less first mortgagee (CBA)             −$400,000
                                        --------
Available to second mortgagee            $30,000
Westpac's debt                           $50,000
Westpac's shortfall                     −$20,000
Frank's equity                                $0
```

The **first mortgagee takes priority over the second in the event of
default**. Westpac recovers $30,000 of its $50,000 — a 60% recovery — and
pursues Frank personally for the $20,000 balance.

The selling mortgagee owed a duty under **s85 PLA** to take reasonable
care to ensure the property is sold **at market value**. Damages under
s85(3) are the difference between market value (established through
evidence) and the sale amount — so if market value was $600,000 and the
sale realised $430,000 through inadequate marketing, exposure is
$170,000. The **Property Law (Mortgagor Protection) Amendment Act 2008
(Qld)** added s85(1A): adequately advertise, obtain reliable evidence of
value, maintain the property including reasonable repairs, and **sell by
auction unless it is appropriate to sell another way**.

Note the connection back to your own work — "obtain reliable evidence of
the property's value" is a statutory reason a valuer gets engaged.

</details>

**Q15.** What are the statutory preconditions for a mortgagee exercising
power of sale in Queensland?

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Under **s84 PLA**:

1. **Default** by the mortgagor.
2. A **notice specifying the default and requiring it to be remedied**,
   served on the mortgagor.
3. The **default has continued for 30 days**.

A purchaser is protected once the conveyance is made — **s87 PLA** makes
the purchaser's title unimpeachable if the power was properly exercised,
and **s79 and s184 LTA** mean the purchaser need not enquire, before or
after conveyance, whether it was properly exercised.

</details>

**Q16.** Distinguish a lease from a licence, and state the registration
threshold for a commercial lease in Queensland.

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

A **lease** grants the **right to exclusive possession** (*Radaich v
Smith* (1959) 101 CLR 109) for a fixed or determinable term. A **licence**
is permission to act in a way that would otherwise be trespass or
nuisance; it is a **personal right, not an interest in land**, and falls
short of exclusive use.

Registration: a **long lease** exceeds **3 years** and must be registered
on title to secure tenure. A **short lease** of not more than 3 years is
not required to be registered.

The lessor must be the registered owner of the fee simple. The lessee
holds the leasehold estate; the lessor holds the **reversion**.

</details>

**Q17.** What is a caveat, who are the parties, and give three caveatable
interests.

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

A caveat **prohibits registration of an instrument affecting the interest
claimed by the caveator** until it is withdrawn, removed, lapses or is
cancelled. Its purpose is to buy time for the parties to apply to the
court to enforce or determine an interest in land — it is a freeze, not a
decision.

- **Caveator** — the person in whose favour the caveat is lodged.
- **Caveatee** — the registered proprietor of the lot caveated.

Caveatable interests include: a purchaser under a valid unconditional
contract of sale; a chargee under an agreement specifically charging the
land; an **unregistered (equitable) mortgagee**; a beneficiary of a
constructive, resulting or implied trust; a trustee in bankruptcy after
the property vests.

Caveats protect **equitable** interests — a registered interest does not
need one.

</details>

<details><summary>Checkpoint — self-mark Part B</summary>

The three answers that separate a pass from a distinction:

- **The default is tenancy in common**, not joint tenancy (s35 PLA).
- **Partnerships cannot hold property** — the partners do.
- Element 2 of an easement accommodates the **dominant land**, not the
  dominant owner. That is the whole of *Hill v Tupper*.

</details>

## Part C — Market Value and Highest and Best Use

**Q18.** State the market value definition in full, and identify the five
things it assumes.

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

API and IVSC: *"Market Value is the estimated amount for which a property
should exchange on the date of valuation between a willing buyer and a
willing seller in an arm's length transaction after proper marketing
wherein the parties had each acted knowledgeably, prudently, and without
compulsion."*

Learn it verbatim — the slide labels it "the most important definition in
your course."

The determinants:

1. **Estimated amount** — never "accurate." It is the valuer's *opinion*.
2. **As at a specific date.**
3. **Willing buyer, willing seller.**
4. **Arm's length transaction.**
5. Both parties acting **prudently, knowledgeably, without compulsion.**

</details>

**Q19.** State the *Spencer* test and explain why it is framed as a
hypothetical.

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

*Spencer v The Commonwealth of Australia* (1907) 5 CLR 418. The test is
**not** what price a seller could actually have obtained on a given day —
that asks whether there happened to be a willing buyer. It is: **what
would a person desiring to buy the land have had to pay for it on that
day to a vendor willing to sell it for a fair price but not desirous to
sell?**

The hypothesis matters because it strips out the accident of who was in
the market that day. The vendor is *willing but not anxious*; the parties
are *perfectly acquainted with the land and cognisant of all
circumstances* affecting its value, advantageously or prejudicially. That
is why an actual forced sale, a fire sale or a sale between relatives is
not evidence of market value — one or more of the *Spencer* assumptions
has failed.

</details>

**Q20.** Distinguish price, cost and value.

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

| | Definition | Nature |
|---|---|---|
| **Price** | What you pay for the goods or services acquired | An **historical fact** |
| **Cost** | The inputs incurred in producing the product — labour, capital, materials, bills, wages, marketing, distribution, shipping | A calculated fact |
| **Value** | What the goods or services pay *you* — worth | An **opinion**, not a fact |

Price and cost are calculated in numerical terms and are the same for all
customers. Value varies from customer to customer and can never be
"calculated" in the same sense.

The two rules to carry into the exam: **cost does not always equal value
and does not always equal price**, and **the asking price may or may not
be market value**. The clearest case is an **overcapitalised** property —
the owner has spent the money and insists it is worth more than the market
will pay. It is not. Value is determined by the market.

</details>

**Q21.** A 304 m² site at 42 Mollison Street, South Brisbane sits in the
South Brisbane Riverside Neighbourhood Plan, Boundary and Vulture precinct
(NPP-003). Development height is limited as follows:

```
Site < 1,000 m²                        3 storeys
Site 1,000 m² to < 2,500 m²            5 storeys
Site 2,500 m² to < 10,000 m²           8 storeys
Site ≥ 10,000 m²                      15 storeys
```

What is the permitted height, and which HABU test have you just applied?

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

304 m² < 1,000 m², so **3 storeys**. That is the **legally permissible**
test.

The wider legally-permissible checklist: certificate of title details,
zoning and land use planning, uses forbidden by government regulations,
uses prohibited by deed restrictions or covenants, and **easements**,
which may limit the use of the land. The underlying zone is only the start
— you must then check the **neighbourhood plan**, which is what actually
sets the height here.

> The site-area thresholds are a step function, not a rate. A 999 m² site
> gets 3 storeys and a 1,000 m² site gets 5. Buying the neighbouring 1 m²
> is worth two storeys of GFA. This is the sort of discontinuity that makes
> a site amalgamation financially feasible.

</details>

**Q22.** Why must legal permissibility and physical possibility be tested
*before* financial feasibility and maximum productivity?

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Because a use may be financially feasible and that is **irrelevant if it
is legally prohibited or physically impossible**. The first two tests are
screens; the last two are rankings. Running the tests out of order gets
you a beautifully modelled feasibility for a building that cannot be
approved or cannot stand up.

Note also the two definitions given differ slightly in ordering. The API
definition reads "physically possible, legally permissible and financially
feasible" and IVS 2025 reads "physically possible (where applicable),
financially feasible, legally allowed and result in the highest value."
**Neither wording is the sequence of application.** The sequence is legal
→ physical → financial → maximally productive.

</details>

**Q23.** Land costs $100,000. Three uses are legally permissible and
physically possible on a 1,000 m² building area:

| Use | Construction cost | End market value |
|---|---|---|
| Industrial warehouse | $750,000 | $910,000 |
| Retail neighbourhood centre | $1,500,000 | $1,840,000 |
| Residential apartments | $1,200,000 | $1,534,000 |

Complete the table and identify the maximally productive use.

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

```
Total cost = land + construction

Industrial    100,000 + 750,000   = 850,000
              910,000 − 850,000   =  60,000 profit
              60,000 ÷ 850,000    =   7.06%   → deck states 7%    ✓

Retail        100,000 + 1,500,000 = 1,600,000
              1,840,000 − 1,600,000 = 240,000 profit
              240,000 ÷ 1,600,000 =  15.00%   → deck states 15%   ✓

Residential   100,000 + 1,200,000 = 1,300,000
              1,534,000 − 1,300,000 = 234,000 profit
              234,000 ÷ 1,300,000 =  18.00%   → deck states 18%   ✓
```

All three of the deck's percentages reconcile, and the base is **total
cost**, not end value. On end value the margins would be 6.59%, 13.04% and
15.25%.

> **The two answers disagree and you must say which one you are using.**
> Retail returns the largest profit in **dollars** ($240,000 vs $234,000).
> Residential returns the largest profit as a **percentage of the
> investment required** (18% vs 15%). The deck's own definition of maximum
> productivity is "the highest net return (profit) to the developer/owner,
> **based on the investment required**" — that phrase points at the
> percentage, so **residential apartments** is the maximally productive
> use. Answer it that way and state the basis. An answer that picks retail
> on the dollar figure without naming the base has not applied the test.

The formal criterion is the use producing the highest **NPV** or **IRR**.
A $6,000 difference in profit against a $300,000 difference in capital
outlay would not survive a discounted analysis.

</details>

**Q24.** A project costs $10,000 at time 0 and returns $2,000, $3,000,
$3,000, $4,000 and $4,000 at the ends of years 1 to 5. The discount rate
is 4%. Calculate the NPV and explain why it is not $6,000.

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

```
NPV = Σ [ cash flow ÷ (1 + i)^t ] − initial investment

Year 1   2,000 ÷ 1.04^1  = 1,923.08
Year 2   3,000 ÷ 1.04^2  = 2,773.67
Year 3   3,000 ÷ 1.04^3  = 2,666.99
Year 4   4,000 ÷ 1.04^4  = 3,419.22
Year 5   4,000 ÷ 1.04^5  = 3,287.71
                          ----------
Sum of present values    = 14,070.66
Less initial investment  = (10,000.00)
                          ----------
NPV                      =  4,070.66     → deck states 4,071   ✓
```

The undiscounted net total is $16,000 − $10,000 = **$6,000**. The NPV is
**$4,070.66**. The $1,929.34 difference is the **time value of money** —
money today is worth more than money tomorrow, and later cash flows are
discounted harder. Writing $6,000 as the NPV is the single most common
error on this slide.

**IRR** is the discount rate that makes NPV equal zero; the higher the
IRR, the more desirable the investment.

</details>

**Q25.** Explain, using the RR / SS diagram from Week 5, when a site should
be redeveloped.

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

- **Line RR** is the capital value of the *house*, falling with age
  through physical depreciation and economic obsolescence.
- **Line SS** is the capital value of the *cleared site*, assumed
  constant.
- Up to year **L**, the capital value of the house exceeds the capital
  value of the cleared land — hold.
- After year **L**, the value of the cleared site exceeds the value of the
  land and building together — **redevelop**.

Formally: if the value of the land as vacant exceeds the value of the
property as improved, **less demolition/reversion costs**, redevelopment
is the maximally productive use. Continuing an existing use that does not
represent the highest net value of the site is not maximising the site's
productive capability.

> **Don't drop the demolition cost.** The comparison is land-as-vacant
> against improved-value-less-demolition. A student who compares raw land
> value against improved value will call for redevelopment years too early.

**Economic life** (API): the total period over which an asset is expected
to generate economic benefits for one or more users. It is not the
physical life — a structurally sound building whose layout no longer suits
any occupier has reached the end of its economic life while standing up
perfectly well.

</details>

**Q26.** Distinguish highest and best use from most probable use.

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**Most probable use** is the use of the property when all the HABU factors
are considered. A property may have the *potential* for a higher use, but
if that use is not feasible, the valuer has to value the **most probable
use**.

In practice they usually coincide for an established residential dwelling
in an established suburb — which is the answer for 80 Bertha Street, where
the existing use is the highest and best use given the overlays and the
alignment with the local market. Where they diverge is on a redevelopment
site: the HABU may be a six-pack of units, but if finance and approval are
not obtainable, the most probable use is the house that is on it.

</details>

**Q27.** In what capacities may a valuer appear before a court, and what
is the consequence of getting it wrong?

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Opinion evidence is generally **not admissible** unless given by a witness
called as an expert. A registered valuer is regarded by the courts as
having the specialised training and experience in real property matters to
qualify as an expert.

A valuer may:

- act as an **expert for the court**, not for a particular party;
- act as an **arbitrator** (there are fine differences between the two
  roles);
- **be sued for damages** if a loss is suffered because of the valuation.

The duty of care goes **far beyond the mere expression of an opinion of
value**. A valuation must reflect the valuer's honest opinion of the true
market value at the date of valuation. A failure to exercise reasonable
care and diligence exposes the valuer to damages for negligence.

> **"I am acting for the court, not for the party who is paying me" is the
> examinable sentence.** The expert's duty runs to the court. A valuer who
> shades an opinion toward the instructing party has become an advocate and
> has forfeited the standing that made the evidence admissible in the first
> place.

</details>

**Q28.** Set out the property purchase process in order.

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

```
1. Inspection and negotiation           — real estate agent
2. Contract                             — in writing, s59 PLA
3. Due diligence period                 — finance, investigations, searches
      building inspection, town planning, flooding,
      legal searches, income, expenses
4. Stamping                             — Titles Office
5. Settlement / completion
      → land registration system
```

The contract must be **in writing** — s59 Property Law Act 1974. Valuation
touches nearly every step: the buyer's lender orders one before finance is
approved, and the due diligence searches are the same searches the valuer
runs.

</details>
