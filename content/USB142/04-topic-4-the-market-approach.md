# TOPIC 4 — The Market Approach

Week 6. Who writes the rules a valuer works to, the five methods available
and when each is the right tool, and then the one method this unit is
actually about: comparing the subject property against real sales and
reasoning to a number. The technique is simple. The discipline — which
sales you let in, which you throw out, and how you defend where you land —
is where the marks are.

## 4.1 Standards and the Bodies That Set Them

| Body | Scope | What it does |
|---|---|---|
| **IVSC** — International Valuation Standards Council | Global | The independent global standard setter for the profession. Publishes the **International Valuation Standards (IVS)**, promoting consistency and professionalism in the public interest |
| **RICS** — Royal Institution of Chartered Surveyors | International | Develops *and enforces* standards across the built and natural environment; sets the ethical, conduct and competence expectations for members. The document to name is the **"Red Book"** |
| **API** — Australian Property Institute | Australia | The peak association for the valuation industry in Australia. Sets and maintains professional practice, education, ethics and conduct standards for members |

IVSC *sets* global standards, RICS *develops and enforces* its own, the API
is the *peak Australian association*. Only RICS is described as enforcing.
Membership of a recognised body plus evidence of CPD is essential to
practise in most countries — which is why the WBP valuation of 80 Bertha
Street prints the valuer's AAPI Certified Practising Valuer number (66753)
and Queensland Registered Valuer number (2756) on its face.

The four core principles, from IVS page 13:

```
1. Ethics          3. Compliance
2. Competency      4. Professional Scepticism
```

> **Professional scepticism is the forgotten one, and this whole topic is
> built on it.** Verifying a sale, excluding an out-of-line transaction,
> refusing to average — each is professional scepticism applied to data
> someone else handed you. Asked why a valuer doesn't simply accept a
> CoreLogic extract, the answer is a named IVS core principle, not "good
> practice".

## 4.3 The Five Valuation Methodologies

The deck's five, in the deck's order:

| # | Method | Alt. name | Process | Typical use | Observation |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Market approach** | Direct market evidence | Comparison of the subject with comparable recent sales evidence | Fundamental basis of value; applicable across all sectors | Primary methodology and the **basis for all others**. *This unit* |
| 2 | **Summation** | Cost approach | Land value (deduced from sales evidence) **plus** the depreciated value of improvements | Less often used. Secondary or check method. Replacement valuations (insurance), assets of nominal value | Pertains more to **cost** than market value. Quick and easy, not as reliable |
| 3 | **Capitalisation** | Income approach | `CV = NI ÷ Y`; Net Income and Yield both derived from market evidence | Income-producing assets with stable cashflows. Explicit link between income, risk and value | Simplistic. A **"static model"** — future cashflows assumed equal to current. *Investment Valuation* |
| 4 | **Hypothetical development** | Reverse feasibility | Backwards analysis from forecast revenue of a proposed development, less development costs | Preliminary assessment of a redevelopment site | "Back of the beer coaster". Ignores the **time value of money**. *Property Development* |
| 5 | **Discounted cashflow (DCF)** | Cashflow analysis | Spreadsheet tracking forecast income and expenditure over the lifecycle; output is an NPV | Multi-tenanted properties, development sites. Establishes NPV and IRR | Handles complex cashflows; needs many assumptions. **Garbage in, garbage out**. *Property Investment Analysis* |

The one formula to be able to write from memory:

```
CAPITALISATION

           NI          NI = Net Income   (estimated, from market evidence)
   CV = ────────       Y  = Yield        (derived,  from market evidence)
            Y

   Worked:  NI = $80,000 p.a.,  Y = 5.0%
            CV = 80,000 ÷ 0.05 = $1,600,000
   Check:   1,600,000 × 0.05 = $80,000   ✓
```

> **Name the variable each method borrows, not just "they all use the
> market".** Summation compares **land values**; capitalisation compares
> **yields and rents**; hypothetical development compares **future sales
> projections — price *and* speed of sales**; DCF compares current and
> forecast yields, rents, vacancy rates and incentives. The generic answer
> is half a mark; the specific list is the whole mark.

## 4.4 Primary and Secondary Methods

Every valuation uses a primary method, then **checks** it with a secondary
method. The secondary method for one property type may be the primary method
for another; which is which depends on the data available and the nature of
the property.

| Market approach is **primary** for | Market approach is **secondary** for |
|---|---|
| Vacant residential land | Industrial property |
| Residential property | Small commercial (office) property |
| Residential strata — units, townhouses, villas, community title | Small retail property |
| Small strata industrial units (< 500 m²) | Subdivision land (data can be limited) |
| Rural property (depending on complexity) | Special premises — hotels, motels, marinas, nursing homes, retirement villages, childcare centres, funeral parlours |

The primary rule is unconditional: **always use the market approach when
there are adequate similar sales to analyse.** The secondary rule is
conditional — it always depends on how similar the sales are. The deck's
phrasing: *always compare apples with apples.*

> **Never average the two methods.** Market approach $1,150,000, summation
> $1,090,000 — the answer is **not** $1,120,000. The secondary method is a
> check: it either supports the primary figure or tells you to find out why
> it doesn't. The same prohibition applies inside a method — never average
> the sales. It appears in the deck three separate times.

## 4.5 What the Market Approach Is

Widely used, the basis for **all** sales analysis, simplistic in operation
but demanding sound reasoning, the **primary** method for residential
property, and reliant on **actual market sales evidence**.

The deck asks whose perception governs — the owner/seller's, the
buyer/tenant's, or the valuer's. The Topic 3 definition answers it: neither
the seller's hope nor the buyer's bid, but the price a hypothetical
willing-but-not-anxious buyer and seller would agree.

The method requires the valuer to identify the market **and any
submarkets**; understand and describe the subject; know the features that
affect value in that market; identify comparable sales, comparing like with
like and adjusting for market variables; and apply logic to the analysis,
reconciliation and valuation.

### The eight steps

```
1. Confirm the property details (legal description, location, property rights)
2. Collect sales evidence and other relevant data (economic and market data)
3. Inspect the property — enough detail for complete comparison, plus value drivers
4. Compare each sale to the subject (property rights, value drivers)
5. Construct a SALES MATRIX and a SALES LINE CONTINUUM
6. Apply the secondary valuation method
7. Explain the analysis in the valuation rationale, including adjustments
8. Adopt a market value — NOT a range, NEVER an average of the sales
```

> **Step 1 is a legal step, not paperwork.** "Confirm … property rights"
> means you value the *interest*, not the dirt. WBP states the interest
> valued for 80 Bertha Street as **fee simple vacant possession** on the
> face of the report. Value a leasehold or tenanted property against
> fee-simple-vacant-possession sales and every number downstream is wrong,
> however good the comparables.

## 4.6 Collecting Sales Evidence

The stated basis: the value of real estate is determined by analysing
prices paid for similar real estate exchanged in the open market by
**willing but not anxious** sellers and purchasers. In Australian practice,
**binding contracts of sale** are generally the best evidence of value.

```
A COMPARABLE PROPERTY is one which:
  (a) would APPEAL TO THE BUYER of the subject property, and
  (b) possesses ENOUGH SIMILARITY to enable meaningful comparison
```

Sales should be similar with respect to seven attributes: **location and
surrounding development, date of sale, age of the building, accommodation,
construction, zoning and use, and quality and condition.** The more similar
the sales, the easier it is to adopt an estimate of value.

Data comes from councils, the Valuer-General's department, and commercial
databases — **CoreLogic (RP Data)** and **Pricefinder** are named as primary
data. The valuer still has to decide whether it is relevant and reliable.

```
RECENT   hot market          3–6 months
         stagnating market   12+ months

SIMILAR  hot market          within a 500 m radius   (suburban residential)
         stagnating market   1–2 km
         small/limited market or specialist type → look further afield
```

The governing test is not distance but the buyer test: **would the same
buyer consider buying any of these?** If not, they are probably not
sufficiently similar and should be excluded. Non-similar sales are excluded
— **or used as a cap or collar** for the evidence.

The purpose is *not* to find the perfectly comparable sale but to analyse
the whole market and the most relevant sales in it: compare all sales to the
subject (this sets the market range), select the most comparable, exclude
the rest, adjust for the current market if necessary, never average.

> **"Recent" and "similar" move together, and that is the trap.** Hot
> market: tighten both (3–6 months, 500 m). Thin or stagnating: loosen both
> (12+ months, 1–2 km). What you must never do is loosen one silently. A
> 14-month-old sale in a hot market is not slightly stale, it is a different
> market. State the market condition you assessed, then the window you
> adopted because of it.

> **Verification is live, not ceremonial.** The CoreLogic property list
> report for the 80 Bertha Street area labels four of its five sales "Normal
> Sale" and one — 12 Emma Street, Kalinga, $2,100,000 — as **Pending
> Settlement Advice**. That is an unsettled contract sitting in a comparables
> table. It is admissible under the deck's binding-contract rule, but the
> price can still change and the report footnotes that some data points were
> edited by the author and not verified by CoreLogic. Say so in the
> rationale.

## 4.7 Out-of-Line Sales

> If a sale does not meet the conditions that define market value, the sale
> is **out of line**.

Every category is a failure of one of Topic 3's conditions — willing buyer,
willing seller, arm's length, prudent, knowledgeable, **without
compulsion**.

| Type | Condition that fails |
|---|---|
| **Special value to the purchaser** | Not the general market's price — one buyer's price |
| **Family sales** | Not arm's length |
| **Adjoining owner sales** | Special value (marriage value of the amalgamated site) |
| **Intercompany sales** | Not arm's length |
| **Resumed / acquired properties** | Compulsion — the vendor could not decline |
| **Forced or mortgagee sales** | Compulsion; the vendor is anxious |
| **Extreme economic situations** (bubble or bust) | Parties not acting prudently or knowledgeably |

```
Sufficient market evidence   → IGNORE it; leave it out of the analysis
Insufficient sales evidence  → it MAY HAVE TO BE USED, adjusted to market value
Either way                   → it may illustrate the END of a value range
                               (a CAP or a COLLAR)
```

> **"Adjoining owner sale" is the missed one, and the Kalinga file has a
> live example.** 72 Bertha Street sold for $3,300,000 in February 2025 —
> the top sale in the CoreLogic CMA, 0.04 km from the subject. Its lot plan
> is **Lots 168–169 RP19535**: two allotments held together, 814 m². 48 Emma
> Street ($2,690,000) is likewise **Lots 69–70 RP19534**, 810 m². Neither is
> strictly an adjoining-owner sale, but both are amalgamated double
> allotments while 80 Bertha Street is a single 448 m² lot on the same plan.
> Valuing a 448 m² lot off an 814 m² double block without saying so is what
> the exclusion rule exists to prevent.

## 4.9 The Adjustment Process

The comparison adjusts the sale price *to the subject*. First establish
direction:

| Sale relative to subject | Consequence for the subject's value |
|---|---|
| Sale is **better** | The subject is worth **less** |
| Sale is **similar** | The value will be **similar** |
| Sale is **not as good** | The subject is worth **more** |

The governing question: what would this sale property have sold for at the
subject's valuation date? Test the result against the principles behind the
definition of market value.

| Head | The question |
|---|---|
| **Date of sale** | What has the price trend been since the sale? Rising → increase. Falling → decrease. Stable → no change. The general change in *other* sales indicates market sentiment |
| **Accommodation** | What is the main house type here — beds, baths, car spaces? Is there a premium for extra accommodation, and **is it visible in the sales evidence?** |
| **Land size** | Define the typical lot size for the market. Premium for views or aspect? Soil type, slope and dimensions may also matter |
| **Condition** | What is average condition *for this location*? Is there a premium above it or a discount below it? |
| **Time on market** | Compare days on market to the suburb average and determine **why** it was shorter or longer |
| **Encumbrances** | An outstanding charge or liability limiting use or preventing full transfer. Mortgages, easements, unpaid taxes, pending legal actions — **may or may not** affect value |

Examinable wording on encumbrances: any legal right or interest over the
property held by a third party is an encumbrance; encumbrances **do not
affect the conveyancing process** but must be clearly specified in the
Certificate of Title; a property free of them has a **clear or free title**.

Land comparison, for both direct comparison and summation, considers lot
size, surrounding development, area/location, aspect, topography, frontage,
pedestrian and vehicle access, and highest and best use.

> **The direction is counter-intuitive and it is worth a mark every time.** A
> *superior* sale pushes the subject's value **down**. The instinct is to
> drift toward a big number; the discipline is to recognise that an
> $1,830,000 sale on 759 m² tells you the subject on 460 m² is worth *less
> than* that. Superior → subject worth less. Inferior → subject worth more.

> **"Is there a premium — and is it in the evidence?" is two tests, and
> students answer one.** Deciding a fourth bedroom is worth $50,000 because
> it feels right is not analysis. Point at two sales, alike in other
> respects, whose price gap demonstrates the premium. No evidence, no
> adjustment.

|---|---|---|---|---|

```
1. Sales are OFTEN SORTED BY DATE.
2. Every Comments cell ENDS with:
      "Overall — superior / inferior / similar to subject"
```

> **The summary statement is the only cell the marker reads twice.** A row
> describing a smaller renovated home on a busy road with difficult access,
> concluding "Overall — inferior to subject", has done its job. A beautiful
> description with no verdict has done nothing, because the continuum is
> built entirely out of those verdicts.

## 4.11 The Sales Line Continuum

A visual representation answering two questions: how do the sales relate to
**each other**, and how do they relate to the **subject**?

```
   lower $ ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────► higher $
────────┬──────────────┬──────────┬───────────┬───────────┬──────────┬─────
   inferior sale   inferior   SUBJECT     similar    superior   superior
                                          sale         sale       sale
   ◄──── FLOOR: highest inferior ──┼── CEILING: lowest superior ────►
```

The technique is **bracketing**: not picking a sale but closing a gap. The
highest inferior sale is a floor, the lowest superior sale is a ceiling, and
any "similar" sale pins the value inside. Plot roughly to scale — it makes
an implausible adoption look implausible. All relevant sales must be
considered: **Brewarrina Pty Ltd v Commissioner of Highways (SA)**.

> **Do not rank these sales on dollars per m² of land — the rate inverts the
> quality ranking.**
>
> ```
> Sale 2  $1,805,000 ÷ 465 = $3,881.72/m²   superior
> Sale 1  $1,280,000 ÷ 405 = $3,160.49/m²   INFERIOR ← 2nd highest rate

> Sale 5  $1,620,000 ÷ 562 = $2,882.56/m²   superior

> Sale 3  $1,830,000 ÷ 759 = $2,411.07/m²   superior ← LOWEST rate, highest price
>

>   (base = Sale 1's rate)

Every verdict lines up with its price in ascending order — inferior,
inferior, comparable, superior, superior. That is what a clean sales analysis
looks like: the comparable sale pins the value, the inferior and superior
pairs bracket it, and the adopted figure sits 0.70% above the comparable.

> **The mean of these five is $1,149,400 against an adopted $1,150,000 — and
> that coincidence is a trap, not a validation.** They differ by 0.05%. Write
> "the average of the sales supports the adopted value" and you have broken
> the unit's most repeated rule and happened to get away with it once. The
> correct sentence names the comparable sale and the bracket. Change one sale
> price and the mean moves; the bracket doesn't.

> **Note what the summation split does *not* say.** Improvements are $250,000
> across 158 m² of living area — $1,582.28/m² — while the same report's
> **insurance estimate is $450,000**, 80.00% higher (base = the $250,000
> improvements figure). Not inconsistent: improvements is the *depreciated
> value added* to the land; insurance is *reinstatement cost*. It is the
> cleanest illustration in the dossier of Topic 3's price ≠ cost ≠ value.

## Summary

- **IVSC** sets global standards (IVS); **RICS** develops *and enforces* its
  own (the Red Book); the **API** is the peak Australian association. Four
  IVS core principles: ethics, competency, compliance, professional
  scepticism
- Five methodologies: **market**, **summation** (cost), **capitalisation**
  (`CV = NI ÷ Y`), **hypothetical development** (reverse feasibility), and
  **DCF**. The market approach underpins all four because each borrows a
  specific variable from it — land values, yields and rents, future sales
  projections, forecast yields/vacancy/incentives
- Every valuation uses a primary method **checked** by a secondary method.
  **Never average the two methods, and never average the sales**
- Market approach is **primary** for residential, vacant residential land,
  residential strata, small strata industrial (< 500 m²) and rural;
  **secondary** for industrial, small office and retail, subdivision land and
  special premises
- A **comparable** property (a) appeals to the buyer of the subject and
  (b) has enough similarity for meaningful comparison. Judge similarity on
  location, date, age, accommodation, construction, zoning/use, and
  quality/condition
- "Recent" is **3–6 months** hot, **12+ months** stagnating; "similar" is
  **500 m** hot, **1–2 km** stagnating. Move both together and say which you
  did
- **Out-of-line sales** fail a condition of market value — special value,
  family, adjoining owner, intercompany, resumption, mortgagee, bubble/bust.
  Exclude when evidence is sufficient, adjust when it isn't, or use as a
  **cap or collar**
- Adjustment direction: **superior sale → subject worth less; inferior sale →
  subject worth more.** Six heads: date of sale, accommodation, land size,
  condition, time on market, encumbrances
- The **sales matrix** is five columns and every row ends "Overall —
  superior / inferior / similar to subject". The **sales line continuum**
  plots those verdicts to scale and brackets the subject between the highest
  inferior and lowest superior sale. *Brewarrina Pty Ltd v Commissioner of
  Highways (SA)* — all relevant sales must be considered
- Bardon: sales $1,280,000–$1,830,000, adopted **$1,400,000** — inside the
  $1,280,000–$1,620,000 bracket and 14.21% below the mean of the five, but
  **4.11% below the sale the deck itself calls "similar"** ($1,460,000, base
  = Sale 4) with no adjustment shown. Confirm with the tutor
- The Bardon dates contradict each other across five slides. Sale 4 is
  **28 November 2022**, not 2023 — a 2023 date would post-date the September
  2023 valuation. Sale 1's year is truncated on the slide and unrecoverable
- Quote a median with its source document and date, always. Kalinga appears
  as $2,162,500, $1,883,899, $1,845,157 and $2.25m across four sources —
  three of them the same PDF
