# TOPIC 3 — Market Value and Highest and Best Use

Week 5 and the Week 5 tutorial. The conceptual centre of the unit. Topic 1
taught you to describe a property and Topic 2 taught you what the law lets
its owner do with it; this topic joins the two and asks the only question
that matters — what is it worth, on what assumed use, to whom, and could
you defend that number from a witness box. The lecturer's own label for the
market value definition is "the most important definition in your course."

## 3.1 The Property Purchase Process

The deck opens with the transaction, because market value and highest and
best use are live at every step of it, not just at the valuation.

```
Inspection and Negotiation  —  Real Estate Agent
            ↓
Contract: in writing  —  s59 PLA
            ↓
Due Diligence Period  —  Finance, Investigations and Searches
   · building inspection      · legal searches
   · town planning            · income
   · flooding                 · expenses
            ↓
Stamping  —  Titles Office          (land registration system)
            ↓
Settlement / Completion
```

`PLA` is the **Property Law Act 1974** (Qld) — the statute the Week 3 and
Week 4 decks cite throughout Topic 2. The Week 5 slide gives the requirement
as "Contract: in writing – s59 PLA" and nothing more, so do not paraphrase
beyond "contracts for the sale of land must be in writing." "Land
registration system" sits *alongside* the stamping box rather than in the
flow — treat it as a caption on the Titles Office step, not a sixth stage.

> **The valuation is not on this diagram, and that is the point.** A
> mortgage valuation happens *inside* the due diligence box, as a condition
> of finance. The WBP valuation of 80 Bertha Street in the source folder was
> instructed by Macquarie Bank, not by the buyer, and its own text says the
> valuer "will not assume any responsibility for reliance by the
> lender-client/LMI on the Report after the expiration of 90 days from the
> date of valuation." A valuation is a dated, addressee-specific instrument
> attached to one step of one transaction. If an exam question asks who a
> mortgage valuation is *for*, the answer is the lender — never the buyer.

## 3.2 What Highest and Best Use Means

The deck gives the definition twice, from two bodies, and then gives its own
four-criteria list. They do not match, so learn the fuller one.

**Australian Property Institute** (cited to `api.org.au/standards/definitions/`):

```
"The use of an asset that maximises its potential and that is physically
 possible, legally permissible and financially feasible."
```

**International Valuation Standards (2025)**, as quoted on the slide:

```
"The highest and best use must be physically possible (where applicable),
 financially feasible, legally allowed and result in the highest value."
```

**The deck's own four criteria**, which appear three separate times — on the
HABU slide, on the 42 Mollison Street slide and on the closing overview
slide — always in this order:

```
1. Legal permissibility
2. Physical possibility
3. Financial feasibility
4. Maximum productivity
```

| Source | Tests named | Order | Fourth test |
|---|---|---|---|
| API definition | 3 explicit | physical → legal → financial | Folded into "maximises its potential" |
| IVS 2025 | 4 | physical → financial → legal → highest value | "result in the highest value" |
| Deck's four criteria | 4 | **legal → physical → financial → maximum productivity** | Named separately as maximum productivity |
| Tutorial 5 | 4 | legal → physical → financial → maximum productivity | Named separately |

> **Use the deck's four-criteria list, not the API sentence.** The API
> definition names only three tests and buries maximum productivity inside
> "maximises its potential"; IVS names four but calls the last one "result
> in the highest value" and puts legal permissibility third. The deck's
> four-item list is the fullest and is the one Tutorial 5 repeats
> word-for-word, so it is the one the marker is holding. If you quote the
> API sentence in an assessment, quote it *and then* set out the four
> criteria — an answer that stops at three tests has dropped the test that
> actually picks the winner.

Tutorial 5 attributes the four-test framing to "IVSC, RICS and API." **RICS
appears nowhere in the lecture deck** and no RICS definition is quoted in the
source folder. Do not invent one; cite IVS and API.

The sequencing rule is explicit and examinable on its own:

> The tests of physical possibility and legal permissibility must be applied
> before the tests of financial feasibility and maximum productivity. A use
> may be financially feasible, but this is irrelevant if it is legally
> prohibited or physically impossible.

Note the small wobble: that sentence names **physical before legal**, while
the four-criteria list names **legal before physical**. Within the first
pair the order does not matter — both are gates and both must pass. Between
the pairs it matters absolutely.

> **Failing a gate is not a deduction, it is an exclusion.** Feasibility
> tests rank the survivors; the first two tests decide who survives. A
> student answer that says "the eight-storey option is legally prohibited
> but has the best return, so it is a close second" has misunderstood the
> structure. It is not second. It is not in the list.

## 3.3 Tests 1 and 2 — Legally Permissible and Physically Possible

### Legally permissible

The deck's checklist:

- Certificate of Title details
- Zoning and land use planning of the subject property
- Uses forbidden by government regulations
- Uses prohibited by deed restrictions or covenants
- Easements — may limit the use of the land

The worked example is 42 Mollison St, South Brisbane: zoned **District
Centre (DC1)** — "concentration of district-serving retail, commercial,
residential and other uses," low-medium rise of similar height to
surrounding residential areas, maximum height **4 storeys unless varied by a
neighbourhood plan**. The site falls in the South Brisbane riverside
neighbourhood plan, **Boundary and Vulture precinct NPP-003**, which varies
it on a sliding scale by site area:

| Site area | Building height (storeys) |
|---|---|
| Less than 1,000 m² | 3 |
| 1,000 m² or greater, less than 2,500 m² | 5 |
| 2,500 m² or greater, less than 10,000 m² | 8 |
| 10,000 m² or greater | 15 |

42 Mollison St has a **lot area of 304 m²**, so it lands in the first row.

```
304 m² < 1,000 m²   →  3 storeys
```

> **The neighbourhood plan beat the zone, and it beat it downwards.** DC1
> allows 4 storeys; NPP-003 cuts this site to 3. Students expect a
> neighbourhood plan to unlock height, and often it does — but the slide's
> purpose is that you must open the plan and read the table, not stop at the
> zone code. "DC1, therefore 4 storeys" loses the mark on the deck's own
> worked example.

### The same test on 80 Bertha Street, Kalinga

The council property holding report (City Plan 2014 v32, effective

> flood layers have changed since.** The WBP report lists **35 distinct QPP
> overlay codes**, including `QPP-P10-2 – OTHER PLANS (SOUTH BANK CORPORATE
> AREA APPROVED DEVELOPMENT PLAN)`, which has nothing to do with a house in
> Kalinga. It also lists exactly one creek flood layer, `QPP-FO-CW4`, where
> the 2025 council report lists **three** — creek/waterway flood planning
> areas 3, 4 **and** 5. Either the mapping was re-cut between 2021 and 2025,

> Pull the current property holding report yourself and name the overlays
> that actually bite.

### Physically possible

The deck's list: size, shape, topography, load bearing capacity of soil,
existing structures, and other characteristics of the site — then a prompt
in the corner of the slide, **"What about flooding?"**, followed by the
Brisbane River flood overlay map.

Flooding is the physical-possibility question at 80 Bertha Street. The
figures the 2021 valuation reproduces from council:

```
Minimum ground level          7.5 mAHD
Maximum ground level         11.1 mAHD
Defined Flood Level (DFL)     9.1 mAHD   source: CREEK/WATERWAY

Estimated peak flood levels
  20% AEP   N/A (council holds no level)
   5% AEP   8.4 mAHD
   2% AEP   8.7 mAHD
   1% AEP   9.1 mAHD

Site fall            11.1 − 7.5 = 3.6 m
Low point below DFL   9.1 − 7.5 = 1.6 m
High point above DFL 11.1 − 9.1 = 2.0 m
```

So part of the site sits 1.6 m under the defined flood level and part sits
2.0 m over it. The valuer's conclusion is consistent: "The subject allotment
is flood affected. The living areas of the dwelling are not affected," with
a **3** risk rating for environmental issues and a recommendation for flood
insurance.

> **"Flood affected" and "the house floods" are different findings.** The
> allotment is affected; the habitable floor is above the DFL. Say only
> "flood affected" and the lender assumes the worst; say only "the house is
> above the flood level" and you have hidden a constraint on any future
> ground-level addition. The minimum habitable floor level for new work is
> given as "Contact Council," so **the figure is not in the source
> material** — say so rather than guessing it.

## 3.4 Tests 3 and 4 — Financially Feasible and Maximally Productive

### Financially feasible

> The proposed use of a property must generate adequate revenue to justify
> the costs of demolition, and construction plus a profit for the developer.

| Site analysis | Demand analysis | Supply analysis |
|---|---|---|
| Define end product; legal aspects; site improvements; building improvement; location determinants | Population; employment; income; affordability; migration patterns; revenue $ | Existing and future inventory (competition analysis); building permits; vacancy rates; lease terms; absorption rates; construction costs $ |

Note where the money lines sit: **revenue** is a demand-side input and
**construction cost** a supply-side input. Both are set by the market, not
by the developer's wishes.

### Maximally productive

> The highest and best use that generates the highest net return (profit) to
> the developer/owner, **based on the investment required**.

The deck's three-scenario comparison. Land price $100,000, building area
1,000 m² in every case:

| Legally permissible and physically possible use | Construction cost | End market value | Total cost | Profit $ | Profit % |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1. Industrial warehouse | $750,000 | $910,000 | $850,000 | $60,000 | 7% |
| 2. Retail neighbourhood centre | $1,500,000 | $1,840,000 | $1,600,000 | $240,000 | 15% |
| 3. Residential apartments | $1,200,000 | $1,534,000 | $1,300,000 | $234,000 | 18% |

Every figure reconciles:

```
Total cost = construction + land ($100,000)
  1.   750,000 + 100,000 =   850,000   ✓
  2. 1,500,000 + 100,000 = 1,600,000   ✓
  3. 1,200,000 + 100,000 = 1,300,000   ✓

Profit = end market value − total cost
  1.   910,000 −   850,000 =  60,000   ✓
  2. 1,840,000 − 1,600,000 = 240,000   ✓
  3. 1,534,000 − 1,300,000 = 234,000   ✓

Profit % = profit ÷ TOTAL COST   (not ÷ end value)
  1.  60,000 ÷   850,000 = 7.06%  → deck: 7%    ✓
  2. 240,000 ÷ 1,600,000 = 15.00% → deck: 15%   ✓
  3. 234,000 ÷ 1,300,000 = 18.00% → deck: 18%   ✓
```

> **Option 2 makes the most money and option 3 is the highest and best use.**
> Retail returns $240,000 against residential's $234,000 — $6,000 more — but
> ties up $300,000 more capital to do it. The deck's definition is the
> highest net return "based on the investment required," which is the
> percentage, not the dollar. **Residential apartments, 18%, is the
> maximally productive use.** If you rank on the dollar column you get the
> wrong answer, and the $6,000 gap is deliberately small so that only the
> students reading the definition notice.

Check that the ranking is not an artefact of the denominator:

```
Profit ÷ end market value
  1.  60,000 ÷   910,000 =  6.59%
  2. 240,000 ÷ 1,840,000 = 13.04%
  3. 234,000 ÷ 1,534,000 = 15.25%   ← still first
```

Residential wins on either base. Only the raw dollar ordering differs, so
the dollar column is the trap and nothing else is.

Cross-check against the hierarchy of uses the deck gives elsewhere —
agricultural, residential, industrial, commercial office, retail, "from
lowest to highest value per square metre":

```
End market value per m² of building area (1,000 m² in each case)
  Industrial   $  910/m²
  Residential  $1,534/m²
  Retail       $1,840/m²   ← highest value per m², as the hierarchy predicts
```

> **The use with the highest value per square metre is not the highest and
> best use.** Retail tops the hierarchy *and* tops the value-per-m² table,
> and still loses, because it costs $1,500/m² to build against residential's
> $1,200/m². The hierarchy tells you which uses are worth investigating; it
> never tells you which one wins. Two further cautions: it ranks **per m²**,
> so a 2-hectare industrial site can beat a 100 m² shop outright; and the
> slide prints retail at the top while the caption reads "lowest to highest,"
> so read the caption, not the layout.

The change-in-land-use mechanisms that move a property up that hierarchy: a
new building for a higher order use; a new building for the same use at
higher intensity; or modification of an existing building for a more
intensive use — "e.g. from house to flats or from house to offices."

### NPV and IRR

> The use that produces the highest **Net Present Value (NPV)** or **Internal
> Rate of Return (IRR)** is the maximally productive use.

```
        Cash flow
NPV = ───────────── − initial investment
        (1 + i)^t

i = required return or discount rate
t = number of time periods
```

The deck's worked table, discount rate 4%:

```
Year          0        1        2        3        4        5     Net total
Cash    (10,000)   2,000    3,000    3,000    4,000    4,000       6,000
PV      (10,000) 1923.08  2773.67  2666.99  3419.22  3287.71       4,071

Recomputed at i = 0.04:
  2,000 ÷ 1.04^1 = 1,923.08   ✓
  3,000 ÷ 1.04^2 = 2,773.67   ✓
  3,000 ÷ 1.04^3 = 2,666.99   ✓
  4,000 ÷ 1.04^4 = 3,419.22   ✓
  4,000 ÷ 1.04^5 = 3,287.71   ✓
  Σ PV = 14,070.66
  NPV  = 14,070.66 − 10,000 = 4,070.66  → 4,071   ✓

Undiscounted:  16,000 − 10,000 = 6,000   ✓ (the "Net total" row)
```

Both figures are right, and the pair is the lesson: **$6,000 undiscounted,
$4,071 in today's money.** The $1,929 difference is the time value of money.

IRR is defined as "a discount rate that makes the net present value (NPV) of
all cash flows equal to zero," and "the higher an internal rate of return,
the more desirable an investment is to undertake."

> **Do not average the cash flows before discounting.** Every PV figure uses
> its own exponent. The two $3,000 years discount to different numbers
> ($2,773.67 and $2,666.99) precisely because year 3 is a year further away.
> Two identical PVs for two identical cash flows means you used one exponent
> for the whole column.

### Most probable use vs HABU

Most probable use is the use of the property once all the HABU factors are
considered. "Although a property may have the potential for a higher use, if
this is not feasible, then the valuer has to consider the most probable use
of the property." In a residential valuation the two almost always coincide
— which is why saying so explicitly is evidence you tested it.

## 3.5 HABU as Vacant, HABU as Improved, and Economic Life

The deck sets out the questions by scenario:

```
If the site is VACANT
  · What is the most likely highest and best use of the land, given the
    valuer's investigations and knowledge of the market?

If valuing an IMPROVED property
  · What is the highest and best use of the current built form?
  · Does the site have a higher and better use if redeveloped?

If it is a PROPOSED DEVELOPMENT
  · What is the highest and best use of the proposed development when
    completed?
  · Does the site have development approval?

Remember to apply the 4 criteria to any assessment of highest and best use.
```

The closing overview slide compresses this to the two questions a
residential valuer actually asks on every job: **what is the highest and
best use as it currently is improved, and what would its highest and best
use be if vacant?**

### Economic life

API definition, as quoted:

```
The total period of time over which an asset is expected to generate
economic benefits for one or more users.
```

The diagram plots two lines against time for a site with a house on it,
where the house was the most profitable use at Year 0:

| Line | What it shows |
|---|---|
| **RR** | Capital value of the house, decreasing with age due to **physical depreciation and economic obsolescence** |
| **SS** | Value of the cleared site, assuming constant values |
| **Year L** | Where they cross |

Up to **L**, the capital value of the house exceeds the capital value of the
cleared land. After **L**, the capital value of the site exceeds the capital
value of land and building — **redevelopment**.

> **Economic life ends at the crossover, not when the building falls down.**
> Year L is a *value* event: the day the cleared site is worth more than the
> site plus improvements. The building can be structurally sound and fully
> tenanted on the day its economic life ends. Same idea as Topic 1's
> durability characteristic — "structures designed for long life but may be
> economically 'destroyed' through obsolescence."

| | Economic life | Continued physical existence |
|---|---|---|
| Defined by the deck? | **Yes** — API definition quoted above | No — the deck never uses the term "physical life" |
| What ends it | The RR/SS crossover at year L: cleared site worth more than site + improvements | Structural failure, demolition, destruction |
| Driven by | Physical depreciation **and** economic obsolescence together | Physical deterioration alone |
| Consequence in a valuation | Triggers the redevelopment question and can reset HABU | None on its own |

> **"Effective age" and "physical life" are NOT FOUND in the Week 5 deck.**
> Neither term appears on any slide, and no depreciation table or age-life
> formula is given — the Week 11 deck says only "deduct depreciation" in the
> summation method, with no worked numbers. If an exam question asks you to
> compute an effective age or age-life ratio, this material does not support
> it; get the Week 9 or Week 10 recording first.

### What makes HABU change

The deck's causes: demographic changes (population growth), urban growth and
development, changes to planning schemes, and the economic life of a
building. Government planning policy manages urban change through zoning and
planning policy; changes to a higher use create higher values, increased
government revenue and provision of infrastructure, "balanced with
preservation of heritage policy." The structural change mechanisms named are
greenfield development, brownfield development, urban renewal,
transit-oriented (mixed-use) development and gentrification, with rezoning
where required. The refrain repeated on four consecutive slides: **the
property market responds to these changes through supply and demand and
creates the necessary adjustments.**

The aspects "very likely to change" are the closest the deck comes to a
taxonomy of obsolescence:

| Aspect | Deck's example | Reads as |
|---|---|---|
| Physical | Age, condition | Physical deterioration |
| Economic | Changing supply and demand | Economic obsolescence (the RR line) |
| Institutional | Planning scheme changes | Legal/institutional obsolescence |
| Functional | "Say disability accessible?" | Functional obsolescence (Topic 1: an unbalanced mix of the production factors) |

### Maximum productivity applied to 80 Bertha Street

The deck states the redevelopment test in words:

> If the value of the land as vacant exceeds the value of the property as
> improved less reversion/demolition costs, then redevelopment of the site
> becomes the maximally productive use of the property.

The 2021 valuation gives both halves of that comparison for a real house:

```
Land                    $  900,000
Improvements            $  250,000
                        ──────────
MARKET VALUE            $1,150,000     ✓  900,000 + 250,000 = 1,150,000

Improvements' share of value   250,000 ÷ 1,150,000 = 21.74%
Land's share of value          900,000 ÷ 1,150,000 = 78.26%
Land rate                      900,000 ÷ 448 m²    = $2,008.93/m²
Whole-property rate on land  1,150,000 ÷ 448 m²    = $2,566.96/m²
```

The value components reconcile to the total exactly — no rounding gap. Now
run the test. Let **LV** = land as vacant ($900,000), **IV** = value as
improved ($1,150,000), **D** = demolition cost.

```
Deck's wording:      redevelop if  LV > IV − D
                                   900,000 > 1,150,000 − D
                                   D > $250,000

Conventional test:   redevelop if  LV − D > IV
                                   900,000 − D > 1,150,000
                                   D < −$250,000   (impossible)
```

> **The deck's sentence puts the demolition cost on the wrong side, and the
> arithmetic proves it.** Read literally, it triggers redevelopment once
> demolition costs exceed $250,000 — the *more* expensive the knock-down,
> the *more* attractive the redevelopment. That cannot be right. The
> defensible reading is the conventional one: redevelop when the cleared
> land, **net of** the cost of clearing it, is worth more than the property
> standing. On this house that never happens, so **the highest and best use
> of 80 Bertha Street is its continued use as improved.** Flag the phrasing
> to your tutor; if you must quote it, quote it and then show the algebra.

The legal test reaches the same answer faster. Character zone, Traditional
building character overlay with the neighbourhood character sub-category,
Dwelling house character overlay — demolition is controlled, so **the
redevelopment option fails gate one before feasibility is reached.**

## 3.6 What Is Market Value

Effective valuation practice requires established processes, principles and
logical methods leading to "estimates of the price for which interests in
land may be expected to realise if offered for sale on reasonable terms and
conditions." Those principles evolved from long-term experience, **case law
and legislation**. Value means different things depending on the property
and the purpose, and — the line students skip — **a property can have more
than one value, and all of them are relevant even though they differ.**

The other bases of value named:

- Insurance value
- Investment value
- Replacement value
- Going concern value
- Forced sale value (liquidation value)
- Special value to the owner
- Heritage value

Market value dominates because most property is valued for sale, purchase or
finance, and because financiers need a current market value to set lending
criteria and lending margins (**Loan to Value Ratio, LVR**).

### The definition

Attributed on the slide to the **Australian Property Institute (API) and the
International Valuation Standards (IVSC)**:

```
"Market Value is the estimated amount for which a property should exchange
 on the date of valuation between a willing buyer and a willing seller in
 an arm's length transaction after proper marketing wherein the parties had
 each acted knowledgeably, prudently, and without compulsion."
```

The 2021 WBP valuation in the same folder states it with two differences:

```
"Market Value is defined as the estimated amount for which an ASSET OR
 LIABILITY should exchange on the date of valuation, between a willing
 buyer and a willing seller in an arms length transaction after proper
 marketing, wherein the parties had each acted knowledgeably, prudently
 and without compulsion."
```

> **"A property" versus "an asset or liability."** The professional report
> uses the wider IVS formulation; the slide narrows it to property. Every
> other element is identical word for word. For a residential valuation the
> narrowing is harmless, but if you are quoting it as *the IVS definition*,
> the report's wording is the one that matches the standard. Quote the
> deck's version in this unit's exam — it is what the marker has — and know
> the real instrument says "asset or liability."

### The five determinants

| Element | What it does |
|---|---|
| **Estimated amount** | Never "accurate" — it is the valuer's **opinion** of value |
| **As at a specific date** | The number is tied to one day |
| **Willing buyer, willing seller** | Both hypothetical; neither is compelled |
| **Arm's length transaction** | No special relationship between the parties |
| **Prudently, knowledgeably, without compulsion** | Both parties informed and unpressured |

> **Every element is an assumption, so every element can be breached by a
> real sale.** A sale between family members breaches arm's length. A
> deceased estate on a four-week deadline breaches "without compulsion." A
> buyer who did not know about the flood overlay breaches "knowledgeably."
> This is the machinery Topic 4 uses to throw out an out-of-line sale — the
> comparable is not rejected because the price looks odd, it is rejected
> because one of these five assumptions did not hold.

## 3.7 *Spencer v The Commonwealth of Australia* (1907)

Cited on the slide as **"Spencer v The Commonwealth of Australia (1907)
C.L.R. 418."** Note the citation as printed carries no volume number before
"C.L.R." — check the full citation before writing it into an assessment
rather than filling the gap from memory.

The passage quoted:

> "In my judgement the test of value of land is to be determined, not by
> inquiring what price a man desiring to sell could actually have obtained
> for it on a given day, i.e., whether there was, in fact, on that day a
> willing buyer, but by inquiring: What would a man desiring to buy the land
> have had to pay for it on that day to a vendor willing to sell it for a
> fair price but not desirous to sell?"

The deck then distils it. The market value of land at a certain date may be
defined as the amount of money the land would bring:

```
· in the open market
· by voluntary bargaining between vendor and purchaser
· both willing to trade
· but neither of them so anxious to do so, that they would overlook any
  ordinary business decision
· both parties should be perfectly acquainted with the land, cognisant of
  all circumstances which might affect its value, either advantageously or
  prejudicially
```

> **The buyer and seller in *Spencer* do not exist, and that is the whole
> mechanism.** The judgment expressly rejects asking whether there was "in
> fact, on that day a willing buyer," substituting a hypothetical one who is
> willing but *not anxious* and "perfectly acquainted" with everything good
> and bad about the land. That last limb is why the flood overlay, the
> character controls and the creek at the rear all belong in the valuation:
> the *Spencer* purchaser knows about them, so the price already reflects
> them. Omit a known detriment and you have valued a sale to a less-informed
> buyer than the law assumes.

The deck introduces it as "case law since 1907" and the summary slide calls
market value "a 100+ year old concept" — the point being that the modern
API/IVS definition and the 1907 judgment describe the same test in different
vocabulary. Line them up:

| API / IVS wording | *Spencer* wording |
|---|---|
| Willing buyer and willing seller | Both willing to trade |
| Without compulsion | Neither so anxious that they would overlook any ordinary business decision |
| Knowledgeably | Perfectly acquainted with the land, cognisant of all circumstances |
| Arm's length, after proper marketing | In the open market, by voluntary bargaining |
| The estimated amount | The amount of money the land would bring |

"Not desirous to sell" is doing specific work: the vendor is willing to sell
at a fair price but is not *seeking* a sale, so the number is not a
forced-sale figure — which the deck lists separately as **forced sale value
(liquidation value)**.

<summary>Checkpoint — Section 3.5 to 3.7</summary>

**Q1.** A 60-year-old house is structurally sound and fully occupied. Can its
economic life have ended?

**A1.** Yes. Economic life ends at the RR/SS crossover — the point at which
the cleared site is worth more than the site plus improvements. That is a
value event driven by physical depreciation *and* economic obsolescence
together; the building's structural condition on the day is beside the point.

**Q2.** Land as vacant $900,000; property as improved $1,150,000; demolition
would cost $40,000. Redevelop?

**A2.** No. On the defensible test, redevelop only if LV − D > IV:
$900,000 − $40,000 = $860,000, which is $290,000 short of $1,150,000. The
improvements contribute $250,000 of value and removing them costs a further
$40,000. Continued use as improved is the maximally productive use. (Note
the deck's own sentence, read literally, inverts this — see §3.5.)

**Q3.** Reconcile the 2021 valuation's components against its market value,
and state what proportion the improvements contribute.

**A3.** Land $900,000 + improvements $250,000 = $1,150,000, matching the
stated market value exactly. Improvements are 250,000 ÷ 1,150,000 =
**21.74%** of value; the land is 78.26%. For a character-zoned inner-north
Brisbane site that split is unremarkable and is itself a signal that the
land, not the dwelling, is carrying the value.

**Q4.** Which limb of *Spencer* requires you to disclose the flood overlay,
and why?

**A4.** The limb requiring both parties to be "perfectly acquainted with the
land, cognisant of all circumstances, which might affect its value, either
advantageously or prejudicially." The hypothetical purchaser knows about the
flooding, so the market value must already reflect it. Leaving it out values
a sale to a buyer the law does not assume.

**Q5.** Name the five determinants of market value from the definition.

**A5.** An **estimated amount** (an opinion, never "accurate"); **as at a
specific date**; **willing buyer and willing seller**; an **arm's length
transaction**; parties acting **prudently, knowledgeably and without
compulsion**.

## 3.8 Price, Cost and Value

The deck's core statement: price does not necessarily equate to value,
because "in valuation parlance, **price is an historical fact** and value is
not a fact, but **an opinion** of the likely amount that would be paid for
the property under normal market conditions at the date of valuation."

| | Price | Cost | Value |
|---|---|---|---|
| Definition | What you pay for goods or services you acquire | The amount of inputs incurred in producing a product | What goods or services pay you, i.e. worth |
| Whose money | Money given **to** the seller for the product | The **seller's** money, spent to produce | Neither — an opinion about exchange |
| Same for everyone? | Yes | Yes | **No** — "varies from customer to customer" |
| Calculated? | Numerically | Numerically | The deck says value "can never be calculated in numbers" |
| Includes | — | Labour, capital, materials, bills, salaries and wages, marketing, distribution, shipping | — |
| Status | Historical fact | Historical fact | Opinion, at a date |

> **Two of those cells contradict the rest of the topic.** (i) "Value can
> never be calculated in numbers" sits directly against the market value
> definition — "the **estimated amount**." Reconcile it this way: value is
> not *computed from inputs* the way cost is; it is estimated as an opinion.
> Written down, it is absolutely a number. (ii) "Value varies from customer
> to customer" describes **worth** or **investment value** — the deck's own
> separate bases — not **market value**, which is built on hypothetical
> parties precisely so that it does *not* vary by purchaser. Name which
> basis you mean before you claim it varies.

Overcapitalisation is where the three come apart in practice: buoyant
conditions stimulate investment-led purchase and renovation, a downturn
follows, and the owner insists the property is worth more than the market
will pay. "It is a basic tenet for the property industry that **value is
determined by the Market**."

The two lines to leave with:

```
1. Cost does not always equal value, does not always equal price
2. The asking price for a property may or may not be its market value
```

### The gap, in real numbers

The 2021 valuation of 80 Bertha Street prices all three concepts on one
page:

```
Improvements' contribution to MARKET VALUE   $250,000
Insurance (replacement COST) estimate        $450,000
                                             ─────────
Gap                                          $200,000

Insurance estimate ÷ improvements value  =  450,000 ÷ 250,000 = 1.80×

Per m² of the stated 158 m² living area:
  Improvements  250,000 ÷ 158 = $1,582.28/m²
  Insurance     450,000 ÷ 158 = $2,848.10/m²
```

It costs $450,000 to rebuild a dwelling that adds $250,000 to what the
property is worth. Neither figure is wrong — they answer different
questions, and the report says as much, noting the valuer "is not a quantity
surveyor and has relied upon published building costing guides" for the
reinstatement value.

> **This $200,000 gap is the exam answer to "does cost equal value."**
> Replacement cost of the improvements is 1.80 times their contribution to
> market value. If a client argues "I spent $450,000 on this house so it must
> be worth $450,000 more," this property refutes it with the client's own
> valuation. Quote both figures and name the bases — replacement cost versus
> the improvements component of market value.

A second reconciliation on the same page **does not** balance:

```
Areas, 2021 WBP valuation      Areas, Cal's 2025 A1 measurement
  Living   158.00 m²             Dwelling GFA   214.979 m²   (+56.979)
  Outdoor   19.00 m²             Alfresco        25.655 m²   (+6.655)
  Car       18.00 m²             Carport         33.750 m²   (+15.750)
  Other      0.00 m²

Site area 448.00 m² (WBP)  =  Title area 448 m² (council)   ✓
```

The site areas agree exactly; the building areas do not. Two plausible
causes: a different measurement basis (a PropertyPRO "living area" is not a
GFA, and the report states its primary methodology was direct comparison on
a "quantum of value" basis rather than a rate per m²), and a four-year gap
in which the report itself records "Additions: Circa 2020." **The sources do
not say which**, so do not assert one. If you cite the 2021 areas, call them
the valuer's living-area figures, not measured GFA — and confirm with your
tutor which basis your own report should adopt.

## 3.9 Value Drivers — Macro and Micro

Value drivers split two ways: **macroeconomic factors**, external to the
property and general — "the relative health of the property industry is
closely correlated to the condition of the national, regional and local
economies" — and **micro factors**, features of the property itself or its
immediate environment. This is Topic 1's micro/macro division applied to a
single number, and Topic 5 picks it up again as economic and market
indicators.

| Macro | Micro |
|---|---|
| **GDP** — total value of all finished goods and services produced within a country's borders in a period; a measure of the size of the economy | **Location** (general locality); **Position** (noise, views, climate); **Access** (transport, amenities) |
| **Cash rate** — the interest rate on unsecured overnight loans between banks; direct relationship with interest rates | **Services** (public utilities); **Site characteristics** (size, shape, frontage, topography) |
| **CPI** — household inflation, including price change by category of household expenditure | **Building characteristics** — bedrooms, bathrooms, garage, pool, condition |
| **Unemployment rate**; **Government initiatives** | **Emotional factors** — street appeal, landscaping/gardens, neighbours, fashion and social trends, colours, natural disasters, crime, schools |

> **"Emotional factors" are not soft — they are the ones you must write
> objectively.** Street appeal, gardens, neighbours and colours sit in the
> deck's own list of value drivers, so they belong in the report. Topic 1's
> rule governs how: the 2021 valuation records "Street Appeal: Good Appeal"
> and "established gardens," not "charming" and "lovely." A value driver you
> can only express in adjectives is one you have not analysed.

## 3.10 Valuers in Court

The last block of the deck, and the answer to the summary slide's question:
"Are you prepared to defend that opinion in court?"

- Valuers can be called upon to give evidence in cases before the courts.
- **In general, opinion evidence is not admissible unless it is given by a
  witness called as an expert.**
- A registered valuer is regarded by the courts as a person with the
  specialised training and experience in real property matters and is
  therefore considered qualified to be called as an expert.

Valuers can **act as an expert for the court, not for a particular party**;
act as arbitrators — "there are some fine differences between acting as an
expert or as an arbitrator"; and **get sued for damages** if a loss is
suffered due to the valuation.

| Expert witness (for the court) | Advocate (acting for a party) |
|---|---|
| Duty runs to the court | Duty runs to the instructing party |
| Produces an honest opinion of the true market value | Produces the position that suits the case |
| The deck's express formulation: expert **for the court not for a particular party** | The role the deck contrasts it against |
| Opinion evidence admissible because of the expert status | Opinion evidence is not admissible except from a witness called as an expert |

> **The deck says there are "fine differences" between expert and arbitrator
> and never says what they are — NOT FOUND.** No slide distinguishes the two
> roles and no arbitration material appears in the source folder. Say a
> valuer may be engaged in either capacity and that the distinction affects
> whose decision binds the parties; do not invent a list of differences and
> attribute it to the unit.

### Liability and negligence

The most quotable passage in Week 5 after the market value definition:

```
· The duty of care that a registered valuer must exercise in undertaking a
  valuation goes FAR BEYOND the mere expression of an opinion of value.
· A valuation must reflect the HONEST OPINION of the valuer of the TRUE
  MARKET VALUE of the land AT THE DATE of valuation.
· If the valuer fails to exercise REASONABLE CARE AND DILIGENCE in making a
  valuation, that valuer may be liable for damages arising as a result of
  his or her incompetence/negligence.
```

> **"Honest" and "reasonable care and diligence" are two separate hurdles.**
> A valuer who genuinely believes a number can still be negligent if they
> did not inspect, did not search, or did not investigate the comparables.
> Honesty defeats a fraud allegation; only process defeats a negligence
> claim. This is why Topic 5's inspection procedure and the report's
> annexures matter — the working papers *are* the defence.

The 2021 WBP report shows what that defence looks like built into the
document rather than argued afterwards:

| Device in the report | What it is protecting against |
|---|---|
| **Valuer declaration** — "I personally physically inspected the property on the date below," plus a no-conflict statement | Allegations of a desktop valuation or an undisclosed interest |
| **Countersignatory declaration** — a supervising member reviewed the draft and working papers | Sole-practitioner error; establishes internal review |
| **Named reliant parties** and a **90-day reliance limit** | Claims by anyone else (expressly including the borrower), or after the market has moved |
| **Scope disclaimers** — not a structural expert, quantity surveyor, geotechnical engineer or environmental expert; "our report is not a site survey" | Claims that the valuer should have identified something outside their discipline |
| **Market uncertainty clause** (COVID-19, dated to the WHO declaration of 11 March 2020) | Claims arising from an unusually volatile valuation date |
| **Capped liability scheme** — "Liability limited by a scheme approved under Professional Standards Legislation" | The size of any judgment |

> **The disclaimers narrow the scope; they do not lower the standard inside
> it.** Every clause says "this is not what I was asked to do." None says "I
> did what I was asked to do badly, and that is fine." The valuer still
> personally certified an inspection, a market value and five comparable
> sales, and can still be sued on those. Topic 5 covers the API guidance
> paper ANZPGP-201 on wording disclaimers properly; the trap here is
> thinking boilerplate substitutes for the work.

The deck's own summary of the whole topic:

- The purchase process has a couple of key steps — matters of market value
  and highest and best use apply all along the way
- **Valuations are based on the highest and best use of a property**
- "Market Value" is a 100+ year old concept in valuation
- Property values are impacted by macro and micro factors
- Cost does NOT = Price, does NOT = Value
- Value is always an estimate. **It is your opinion as an expert**

<summary>Checkpoint — Section 3.8 to 3.10</summary>

**Q1.** An owner spent $450,000 renovating. The valuer says the improvements
contribute $250,000 to market value. Who is wrong?

**A1.** Neither — they are measuring different things. $450,000 is
replacement **cost** (inputs); $250,000 is the improvements' contribution to
market **value** (what the market will pay). The ratio here is 1.80×. The
deck's tenet applies: value is determined by the market, and this is exactly
the overcapitalisation case it describes.

**Q2.** Why is a registered valuer allowed to give opinion evidence when most
witnesses are not?

**A2.** Because opinion evidence is generally inadmissible unless given by a
witness **called as an expert**, and the courts regard a registered valuer as
having the specialised training and experience in real property matters that
qualifies them as an expert.

**Q3.** A valuer honestly believes a property is worth $2m, states it, and is
wrong. Is honesty a defence?

**A3.** Only partly. A valuation must reflect the valuer's honest opinion of
the true market value at the date of valuation — but the duty of care "goes
far beyond the mere expression of an opinion of value." Failure to exercise
**reasonable care and diligence** exposes the valuer to damages for
incompetence or negligence regardless of sincerity.

**Q4.** Sort into macro and micro: cash rate, frontage, unemployment rate,
street appeal, CPI, topography.

**A4.** **Macro** — cash rate, unemployment rate, CPI. **Micro** — frontage
and topography (site characteristics), street appeal (emotional factors).

**Q5.** The deck says value "can never be calculated in numbers," and also
that market value is "the estimated amount." Resolve it in one sentence for
a marker.

**A5.** Value is not *derived from inputs* the way cost is — it is an opinion
formed from market evidence — but that opinion is expressed as a specific
estimated amount at a specific date, so the two statements describe how the
number is *reached*, not whether it is a number.

## Summary

- The purchase process runs inspection and negotiation → **contract in
  writing, s59 PLA** → due diligence (finance, investigations, searches) →
  stamping at the Titles Office → settlement. The mortgage valuation lives
  inside due diligence, is addressed to the lender, and expires
- **HABU has four tests in this order: legal permissibility, physical
  possibility, financial feasibility, maximum productivity.** The API
  definition names only three; use the deck's four
- **Legal and physical are gates; financial and maximum productivity rank
  the survivors.** A prohibited use is excluded, not ranked
- Read the neighbourhood plan, not just the zone — NPP-003 cuts 42 Mollison
  St's 304 m² site from DC1's 4 storeys to 3
- Maximum productivity is the highest return **based on the investment
  required**: retail's $240,000 loses to residential's $234,000 at 18%
- The highest value per square metre is not the highest and best use —
  retail leads at $1,840/m² of building area and still loses
- **Economic life ends at the RR/SS crossover** (cleared site worth more than
  site plus improvements), not when the building fails. "Physical life" and
  "effective age" are not taught in this material
- Market value = "the **estimated amount** for which a property should
  exchange on the **date of valuation** between a **willing buyer** and a
  **willing seller** in an **arm's length transaction** after proper
  marketing wherein the parties had each acted **knowledgeably, prudently,
  and without compulsion**" (API/IVSC)
- ***Spencer* (1907)** substitutes a hypothetical buyer and a vendor willing
  but "not desirous to sell," both "perfectly acquainted" with everything
  advantageous and prejudicial about the land
- **Price is historical fact; cost is inputs; value is an opinion at a date.**
  80 Bertha Street: $450,000 to rebuild improvements worth $250,000 — a
  1.80× gap
- 80 Bertha Street reconciles exactly: land $900,000 + improvements $250,000
  = market value $1,150,000, improvements 21.74% of value. Continued use as
  improved is the HABU, and the character overlays settle it before
  feasibility is reached
- Valuers give opinion evidence because the courts treat a registered valuer
  as an expert; the expert acts **for the court, not for a party**; and a
  valuer who fails to exercise reasonable care and diligence is liable for
  damages
