# Topic 7 — Fiscal and Monetary Policy

Lecture 11 · Tutorial 11 · textbook Ch. 24 p. 623, Ch. 15 pp. 404–405,
pp. 234, 274, 278–279

The lecture's own framing question: **are fiscal stimulus and rate cuts
effective policies to combat recession?** The exam version of that question
wants the mechanism, the diagram, *and* the limitations — a discussion answer
that only says "cutting rates boosts spending" gets a fraction of the marks.

## The Australian government budget

```
Budget balance = Revenue − Expenses

    balance > 0  →  SURPLUS
    balance < 0  →  DEFICIT
```

| Sources of revenue (receipts) | Categories of expense (payments) |
|---|---|
| Taxes on individuals | Transfer payments |
| Taxes on companies | Expenditure on goods and services |
| Indirect and other taxes | Debt interest and other payments |
| Non-tax revenue | |

**Why deficits and debt matter** (L11, slide 12), two reasons given:

1. They **lower the credit rating and increase the interest rate** the
   government must pay.
2. They **redistribute C and G across generations** — today's spending is
   funded by tomorrow's taxpayers. The lecture calls this "the budget time
   bomb", driven by the ageing of the Australian population and documented in
   the 2023 Intergenerational Report.

> Do not confuse the **deficit** (a flow — one year's shortfall) with the
> **debt** (a stock — the accumulated total of past deficits). A government
> can reduce the deficit while the debt is still rising, because a smaller
> deficit is still a deficit. Exam wording deliberately mixes the two.

**Tutorial 11 Q1** asks what slower growth does to the budget. Slower growth
means lower incomes and profits, so **tax revenue falls**; and higher
unemployment, so **transfer payments rise**. Both push the balance towards
**deficit**, even with no change in policy. That is the **automatic
stabiliser** effect — it happens without anyone legislating anything.

## Fiscal stimulus

**Fiscal stimulus** is an increase in government expenses or a decrease in tax
revenue.

### The Keynesian argument for

Keynes (*The General Theory*, 1936): a failure of aggregate demand can plunge
the economy into recession, and business and consumer **confidence** plays a
large role in AD — as in the Great Depression (1929–1939). So the government
should raise G or cut T, and the **multiplier** amplifies that into a larger
rise in real GDP, creating or saving jobs.

```
FISCAL STIMULUS IN AS–AD  (L11, slide 18)

   P │           LAS                Potential GDP  = $2.0 bn
     │            │  AS             Real GDP       = $1.9 bn
 105 │──────────●─┤                 Recessionary gap = $0.1 bn
     │        ╱ │ │
 100 │──────●───┤ │                 ΔG (or a tax cut) raises expenditure by ΔE.
     │    ╱ │ ╲ │ ╲                 The MULTIPLIER adds induced expenditure.
     │  ╱   │  AD0 AD1              AD shifts RIGHT, AD0 → AD1.
     └──────┴───┴──────── Y         Price level rises 100 → 105,
           1.9  2.0                 real GDP rises to $2.0 bn,
                                    the gap is eliminated.
```

Note what the diagram gives you and what it costs: the gap closes, **but the
price level rises**. Stimulus is not free even when it works.

### The mainstream arguments against

**1. The multiplier is overestimated.** The effects are "small, short-lived
(unsustainable) and incapable of working fast enough to be useful", and
inflation becomes a problem.

**2. The crowding-out effect.** Government borrowing competes with private
borrowing in the loanable funds market:

```
CROWDING OUT  (L11, slide 17)

   Real interest rate (%)
      │      SLF2   SLF1 = total savings
      │        ╲     ╲
    5 │─────────●─────╲──────      Gov runs a DEFICIT.
      │          ╲     ╲           → no public saving, so total savings
    4 │───────────╲─────●            is only PRIVATE saving
      │            ╲     ╲          → the government borrows, which
      │             ╲     ╲ DLF       REDUCES loanable funds available
      └──────────────┴─────┴────     → SLF shifts LEFT, SLF1 → SLF2
            Loanable funds ($bn)     → the real interest rate RISES 4% → 5%
                160   180            → private investment falls 180 → 160
                └─────┘                = PRIVATE INVESTMENT CROWDED OUT
```

The lecture notes that Figure 24.8 on p. 623 of the textbook "does not do a
decent job" of illustrating this — use the slide's version.

> Crowding out is a claim about the **real interest rate**, not about
> government "using up" resources in some physical sense. The chain is:
> deficit → government borrowing → supply of loanable funds falls → real
> interest rate rises → private investment falls. Every link has to be in the
> answer. And note the direction: it is the **supply** of loanable funds that
> shifts left in this treatment, not demand.

### Limitations of discretionary fiscal policy

The examinable list (L11, slide 19), and the same list is offered as the
answer to Sample Exam v2 Q4:

| Limitation | What goes wrong |
|---|---|
| **Legislation lag** | Parliament takes time to pass the law |
| **Impact lag** | Economic forecasting is not an exact science; the money arrives after the problem has moved |
| **Potential GDP is imperfectly estimated** | You cannot size a gap you cannot measure |
| **Inflation becomes a problem** | If AS is steep, stimulus goes into prices, not output |
| **Multiplier effect** | Its size is uncertain and may be smaller than assumed |
| **Crowding-out effect** | As above |
| **Behavioural economics** | Consumers do not always behave rationally — a cash handout may be saved, not spent |

## Supply-side fiscal policy

**Supply-side effects** are the effects of fiscal policy on **potential GDP**,
i.e. on the LAS curve rather than AD. Potential GDP depends on the
full-employment quantity of labour, the quantity of capital and the state of
technology — and fiscal policy can move all three through **tax changes**,
**government expenditure** and **government reforms**.

| Instrument | Supply-side claim | The lecture's counterweight |
|---|---|---|
| **Tax cuts** | Reaganomics: cuts give firms cash to hire and expand, and give workers more incentive to work, increasing labour supply | Reagan's 1980 cuts made the US deficit explode. Feldstein: "Tax cuts without spending cuts will swell the budget deficit and bring a crowding-out effect" |
| **Government expenditure** | Infrastructure, health and education raise output, potential GDP and long-term growth | Is this really supply-side or just demand-side? And can government spend better than the private sector? — the NBN is offered as the test case |
| **Government reforms** | Deregulation to increase competition: Australian telecommunications from the late 1980s; the financial system in the early 1980s | — |

> Keep the two channels separate. **Demand-side** fiscal policy shifts **AD**
> and closes an output gap. **Supply-side** fiscal policy shifts **LAS and
> AS** and raises potential GDP itself. The same measure can do both — road
> spending is G today and capacity tomorrow — but the diagrams are different
> and an exam answer should say which one it means.

## Monetary policy: objectives

The RBA's statutory objectives (L11, slide 28, pp. 404–405):

1. the **stability of the currency** of Australia;
2. the **maintenance of full employment** in Australia; and
3. the **economic prosperity and welfare** of the people of Australia.

Under the Statement on the Conduct of Monetary Policy, the Reserve Bank and
the government agree on **keeping consumer price inflation between 2 and 3 per
cent, on average, over the business cycle** — inflation targeting.

The slide asks *why 2–3% and not 0%?* Because a small positive target leaves
room to cut real interest rates in a downturn, offsets the CPI's upward bias
(Topic 5), and keeps the economy away from deflation, which is much harder to
escape.

## The transmission mechanism

### Fighting a recession — an expansionary move

```
RBA buys Commonwealth Government Securities (CGS) from banks
   via OPEN-MARKET OPERATIONS
        ↓
banks' RESERVE SUPPLY increases  (money supply MS0 → MS1)
        ↓
the CASH RATE falls
        ↓
OTHER INTEREST RATES fall
        ↓
borrowing for CONSUMPTION and INVESTMENT increases   (↑C, ↑I)
        ↓
AD shifts RIGHT  (amplified by the MULTIPLIER)
        ↓
real GDP rises, the recessionary gap closes, price level rises
```

### Fighting inflation — a contractionary move

Everything reverses. The RBA **sells** CGS to banks, which **decreases**
banks' reserve supply, **raises** the cash rate and other interest rates,
**discourages** borrowing for C and I, and shifts **AD left** (again amplified
by the multiplier). Real GDP and the price level fall.

> **Buy to loosen, sell to tighten.** The RBA *buys* securities to *lower*
> rates and *sells* them to *raise* rates. It feels backwards until you see
> that buying puts cash into the banking system. Getting this the wrong way
> round inverts the whole answer, and it is worth writing "buys → reserves up
> → cash rate down" in the margin before you start.
>
> Also: monetary policy changes **real GDP, not potential GDP**. The L12
> review question (slide 3) is exactly this — if real GDP exceeds potential
> GDP the RBA "raises the cash rate to decrease real GDP **but not potential
> GDP**".

## Credit creation and the money multiplier

An open-market purchase does not just add its own value to the money supply —
the banking system multiplies it.

**Worked example (L11, slides 32–33).** Initial cash deposit \$100, minimum
reserve ratio R = 20%.

| Round | Bank | Deposit | Reserve (20%) | Loan (80%) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | A | 100.00 | 20.00 | 80.00 |
| 2 | B | 80.00 | 16.00 | 64.00 |
| 3 | C | 64.00 | 12.80 | 51.20 |
| 4 | D | 51.20 | 10.24 | 40.96 |
| … | … | … | … | … |
| **Total** | | **500** | **100** | **400** |

```
                 Total deposits created
Money multiplier K = ────────────────────  =  1 / R
                     Initial deposit

R = 20%  →  K = 1 / 0.20 = 5
Total deposits = K × initial deposit = 5 × $100 = $500
   cross-check: 100 + 80 + 64 + 51.2 + … = 100 / (1 − 0.8) = $500   ✓
Loans created  = $500 − $100 = $400
Reserves held  = 20% × $500  = $100  ( = the original cash )
```

> **L11 slide 33 has a typo worth knowing about.** It writes "Amount of money
> created = **R** × initial deposit = 5 × \$100 = \$500". The multiplier used
> is **K = 5**, not R = 0.2 — the letter is wrong, the arithmetic is right.
>
> A second point of care: **\$500 is total deposits, of which \$100 was
> already money.** The *new* money created by lending is **\$400**. Read
> whether the question asks for total deposits or money created.
>
> And note the direction of R: a **smaller** reserve ratio gives a **bigger**
> multiplier. R = 10% gives K = 10.

## Money market equilibrium and asset prices

**MS is vertical** because the quantity of money is fixed by the RBA
regardless of the nominal interest rate. **MD slopes downward** because a
higher interest rate raises the opportunity cost of holding non-interest-
bearing money.

**Interest rates and asset prices move in opposite directions.** L11 slide 36
makes it arithmetic:

```
A bond sells for $200 and pays a FIXED $6 a year:
    yield = 6/200 × 100 = 3%

The bond price rises to $300; the coupon is still $6:
    yield = 6/300 × 100 = 2%

Bond price ↑  →  interest rate ↓        Bond price ↓  →  interest rate ↑
```

The adjustment story: if `MD < MS`, people are holding more money than they
want, so they buy interest-bearing assets. **Demand for bonds rises → bond
prices rise → the interest rate falls** until money demand equals money
supply.

## Effectiveness of monetary policy

The lecture's answer is: **it depends on behavioural parameters** (slide 43).

| Parameter | Question it answers | If it is small/weak |
|---|---|---|
| **Interest elasticity of investment** (h) | How much does investment demand rise when rates fall? | Rate cuts barely move I — the loanable-funds channel is blocked |
| **The price effect** (slope of AS) | How much of the AD increase goes into P rather than Y? | A steep AS means stimulus becomes inflation, not output |
| **The multiplier** (K) | How much induced spending follows? | A small K means a small final effect |
| **Expectations and behaviour** | Do households deposit and banks lend? | The RBA controls **neither** how much money households hold as deposits **nor** how much banks choose to lend |

**The liquidity trap** (slide 42): a situation where the central bank's efforts
to stimulate spending fail because **people hoard cash instead**. Rates are
already near zero, so cutting them further does nothing.

### The exam-style discussion — RBA policy in 2020

Sample Exam v1 Q4 asks you to discuss the effectiveness of the RBA's 2020
monetary policy. The solution's structure is worth copying:

1. **State the theory.** Lower rates should encourage borrowing and spending,
   because saving becomes unattractive.
2. **Introduce interest elasticity.** Rates were cut three times in 2019 and
   again in March 2020 to 0.25% — repeated cuts with little response *are the
   evidence* that consumers and producers are interest **inelastic**.
3. **Bring in the real economy.** Businesses shut, employment fell, incomes
   fell, consumption fell. Firms will not borrow to invest when demand is
   collapsing, no matter how cheap credit is.
4. **Note the price side.** Grocery prices rose, cutting real purchasing
   power.
5. **Conclude.** It was unlikely to be effective.

> The stronger version of that answer adds the AS–AD point from Topic 6:
> **monetary policy shifts AD, so it cannot fix a supply-side problem.**
> Lockdowns, supply-chain breaks and labour shortages moved **AS**, and no
> cash rate solves that. The lecture asks this explicitly at slide 44 —
> "monetary policy is only effective in curbing demand-side factors".

**Tutorial 11 Q3 and Q4** are the two-sided drill. In **Artica** and
**Freezone**, potential GDP is \$300 billion; you read the AS–AD intersection
off the figure and diagnose:

```
Real GDP < $300bn  →  RECESSIONARY gap  →  UNEMPLOYMENT problem
                      → advise the RBA to LOWER the cash rate
                      → buy securities, AD shifts RIGHT, Y rises to potential

Real GDP > $300bn  →  INFLATIONARY gap  →  INFLATION problem
                      → advise the RBA to RAISE the cash rate
                      → SELL securities, AD shifts LEFT, P falls, Y falls
                        to potential
```

If the central bank does nothing, the gap eventually self-corrects through
money-wage adjustment moving the **AS** curve (Topic 6) — but slowly, and at
the cost of a change in the price level.

> Tutorial 11 Q4's last part is the trap: "what happens if the central bank
> conducts an open market **sale** of securities?" In an economy with a
> **recessionary** gap, a sale raises the interest rate, shifts AD **further
> left** and makes the recession **worse**. The answer is "do not recommend
> it". Always check which gap you are in before recommending a direction.

## Fiscal versus monetary policy at a glance

| | Fiscal policy | Monetary policy |
|---|---|---|
| **Run by** | Government (Treasury, Parliament) | Reserve Bank of Australia |
| **Tools** | G, taxes, transfer payments | Open-market operations → cash rate |
| **Expansionary** | ↑G or ↓T → AD right | Buy CGS → reserves ↑ → cash rate ↓ → C, I ↑ → AD right |
| **Contractionary** | ↓G or ↑T → AD left | Sell CGS → reserves ↓ → cash rate ↑ → C, I ↓ → AD left |
| **Main lags** | **Legislation lag** (slow to decide) | Short decision lag, long **impact** lag |
| **Main criticism** | **Crowding out**, uncertain multiplier | Interest **inelasticity**, the **liquidity trap** |
| **Can shift LAS?** | Yes — supply-side measures | No |

## Checkpoint

<details>
<summary>1. The RBA wants to lower the cash rate. Does it buy or sell government securities, and what happens to bond prices?</summary>

It **buys** Commonwealth Government Securities from banks. That increases
banks' **reserve supply**, which pushes the cash rate **down**.

Buying raises the **demand** for bonds, so **bond prices rise** — and a higher
price on a fixed coupon means a **lower yield**. Price up, interest rate down:
the same relationship from both directions.
</details>

<details>
<summary>2. The reserve ratio is 12.5% and the RBA injects $200 million. How much in total deposits is created, and how much new money?</summary>

```
K = 1 / 0.125 = 8
Total deposits = 8 × $200m = $1,600 million

New money created by lending = $1,600m − $200m = $1,400 million
Reserves held = 12.5% × $1,600m = $200m   ( = the injection )   ✓
```
</details>

<details>
<summary>3. Explain crowding out in four steps.</summary>

1. The government runs a **budget deficit** and must borrow to fund it.
2. Public saving is zero (or negative), so the **supply of loanable funds
   falls** — SLF shifts **left**.
3. The **real interest rate rises**.
4. **Private investment falls** — it has been crowded out by public borrowing.

The net effect on AD is therefore smaller than the raw multiplier suggests.
</details>

<details>
<summary>4. Name two limitations of fiscal policy for economic stabilisation and explain each in a sentence.</summary>

Any two of the lecture's list. Two clean ones:

- **Legislation lag** — Parliament must debate and pass the measure, so by the
  time money is flowing the recession may already have turned, making the
  stimulus pro-cyclical.
- **Crowding-out effect** — the borrowing needed to fund the deficit pushes up
  the real interest rate and reduces private investment, offsetting part of
  the intended increase in aggregate demand.

Others available: impact lag, imperfect estimation of potential GDP,
uncertainty about the multiplier, inflation.
</details>

<details>
<summary>5. Why might a cash rate cut fail to raise investment even though the theory says it should?</summary>

Because investment may be **interest inelastic**. Firms invest when they
expect demand for their output; if demand is collapsing, cheap credit does not
make a project profitable. Add the **liquidity trap** — households hoard cash
rather than spend it — and the fact that the RBA controls **neither** how much
money households hold **nor** how much banks choose to lend.

And if the shock came from the **supply** side, AD-based policy was never the
right instrument.
</details>

## Summary

- `Budget balance = revenue − expenses`. **Deficit is a flow; debt is a
  stock.** Slower growth automatically worsens the balance.
- **Fiscal stimulus** = ↑G or ↓T. Keynesian case: AD failure causes recession,
  the multiplier amplifies the fix. Against: the multiplier is overstated, and
  **crowding out** — deficit → borrowing → SLF left → real interest rate up →
  private investment down.
- Limitations of discretionary fiscal policy: **legislation lag, impact lag,
  imperfect estimate of potential GDP, inflation, uncertain multiplier,
  crowding out, irrational consumer behaviour.**
- **Supply-side** fiscal policy targets **potential GDP** (LAS), not AD, via
  tax cuts, spending on capacity and deregulation.
- **RBA objectives**: currency stability, full employment, prosperity and
  welfare — operationalised as **2–3% inflation on average over the cycle**.
- **Transmission**: buy CGS → reserves ↑ → cash rate ↓ → other rates ↓ →
  C and I ↑ → AD right. Reverse for tightening. **Buy to loosen, sell to
  tighten.**
- `Money multiplier K = 1/R`; total deposits = K × initial deposit; new money
  = total deposits − initial deposit.
- **Bond prices and interest rates move in opposite directions**, because the
  coupon is fixed.
- Effectiveness depends on the **interest elasticity of investment**, the
  **slope of AS**, the **multiplier**, and **expectations** — and monetary
  policy shifts **AD only**, so it cannot fix a supply shock.
