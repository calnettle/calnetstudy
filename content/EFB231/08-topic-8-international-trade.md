## The production possibilities frontier

> The **PPF** is the boundary between the combinations of goods and services
> that **can** be produced and those that **cannot**, given the available
> factors of production and the state of technology.

```
   Coal
    │╲
 48 │ ●╲                  ● on the frontier  = efficient, all resources used
    │   ╲     ● G         ● inside           = attainable but inefficient
 24 │    ●A ╲             ● G outside        = UNATTAINABLE without trade
    │        ╲                                 or a technology/resource change
    └────────●───── Cars
        12   24
```

Points inside or on the frontier are attainable; points outside are not.
**Trade is how you reach a point like G**, which is the whole argument of the
lecture: trade is "in some ways a type of technology" (a callback to L8's
growth policies).

The **opportunity cost** of one good is the amount of the other you give up —
the absolute slope of the PPF.

## Absolute versus comparative advantage

| | Definition |
|---|---|
| **Absolute advantage** | One person or nation is **more productive** than another — needs **fewer inputs or less time** to produce a good |
| **Comparative advantage** | One person or nation can produce a good at a **lower opportunity cost** than another |

**Specialisation and trade follow comparative advantage, not absolute
advantage.**

### Worked example — Australia and Japan (L12, slides 14–19)

Before trade, each country produces at a point on its own PPF:

| Before trade | Cars | Coal (MT) |
|---|---|---|
| Australia | 12 | 24 |
| Japan | 18 | 12 |
| **Total** | **30** | **36** |

Each country's PPF intercepts (from slide 11): Australia can make **24 cars
or 48 MT of coal**; Japan can make **36 cars or 24 MT of coal**.

```
Opportunity cost of 1 CAR (how much coal is given up)
   Australia: 48 coal ÷ 24 cars = 24/12 = 2.000 MT of coal
   Japan:     24 coal ÷ 36 cars = 12/18 = 0.667 MT of coal
   → JAPAN has the lower opportunity cost → Japan has COMPARATIVE ADVANTAGE
     in cars → Japan should produce cars

Opportunity cost of 1 MT of COAL (how many cars are given up)
   Australia: 24 cars ÷ 48 coal = 12/24 = 0.5 cars
   Japan:     36 cars ÷ 24 coal = 18/12 = 1.5 cars
   → AUSTRALIA has the lower opportunity cost → Australia has COMPARATIVE
     ADVANTAGE in coal → Australia should produce coal
```

**Specialise completely:**

| With specialisation | Cars | Coal (MT) |
|---|---|---|
| Australia (all coal) | 0 | 48 |
| Japan (all cars) | 36 | 0 |
| **Total** | **36** | **48** |

World output rose from 30 cars and 36 MT to **36 cars and 48 MT** — six more
cars and twelve more tonnes, from exactly the same resources.

**Now trade 16 cars for 16 MT of coal:**

| After trade | Cars | Coal (MT) | vs before trade |
|---|---|---|---|
| Australia | 16 | 32 | +4 cars, +8 coal |
| Japan | 20 | 16 | +2 cars, +4 coal |
| **Total** | **36** | **48** | |

**Both countries consume more of both goods.** That is the gain from trade,
and on the PPF diagram each country is now consuming at a point **outside**
its own frontier.

> The two opportunity costs are **reciprocals**, and the trap is inverting
> one of them. Australia gives up 2 coal per car, so it gives up **½ car per
> coal** — not 2. Write the units out (`MT of coal per car`, `cars per MT of
> coal`) before you divide, and check that whoever has the comparative
> advantage in one good has the *dis*advantage in the other. If the same
> country wins both, you have inverted a ratio.
>
> Second trap: **absolute advantage decides nothing.** Here Japan has the
> absolute advantage in cars (18 > 12) *and* Australia in coal (24 > 12), so
> the two criteria happen to agree. Change the numbers so one country is
> better at both and the comparative-advantage answer is unchanged — trade
> still pays.

**Tutorial 12 Q1** is the same calculation with people. Tom produces 2
racquets and 20 balls an hour; Abby produces 2 racquets and 40 balls an hour.

```
Tom:  20 balls ÷ 2 racquets  →  1 racquet costs 10 balls
Abby: 40 balls ÷ 2 racquets  →  1 racquet costs 20 balls

→ TOM has the comparative advantage in RACQUETS (10 < 20 balls given up)
→ ABBY has the comparative advantage in BALLS
     (Tom: 1 ball costs 1/10 racquet;  Abby: 1 ball costs 1/20 racquet)

Trading 15 balls for 1 racquet sits BETWEEN the two opportunity costs
(10 < 15 < 20), so it benefits both:
   Tom gets 15 balls for a racquet he could only turn into 10 balls himself
      → gains 5 balls per racquet
   Abby gets a racquet for 15 balls, when making it herself costs 20 balls
      → gains 5 balls' worth per racquet
```

> **A trade price only produces gains if it lies strictly between the two
> opportunity costs.** At 10 balls per racquet Tom gains nothing; at 20, Abby
> gains nothing; outside that range one party is worse off and would refuse.
> Any question that asks "will they trade at this price?" is asking you to
> check that interval.

## Gains from trade in the demand–supply diagram

Switch from the PPF to a single market. The **world price** is given (the
country is small relative to the world), and it determines whether the country
imports or exports.

### The importing case — Australian cars (L12, slides 22–24)

```
   Price ($'000 per car)
     │╲                        ╱ S (Australian producers)
  24 │ ╲          ●          ╱          no-trade equilibrium: $24,000
     │  ╲    CS  ╱ ╲       ╱
     │   ╲      ╱   ╲    ╱
  15 │────╲────╱─────╲──●──────────  WORLD PRICE $15,000
     │  A  ╲  ╱   B   ╲╱ │
     │      ╲╱  PS     ╲ │  D
     └───────┴──────────┴─┴────── Quantity (million cars)
             1          3
             ↑          ↑
        Aus. output  Aus. purchases
             └── IMPORTS = 2 million ──┘
```

```
World price $15,000 < domestic price $24,000
   → the rest of the world has the COMPARATIVE ADVANTAGE in cars
   → the Australian price FALLS to $15,000
   → Australians BUY MORE:      quantity demanded rises to 3 million
   → Australian makers PRODUCE LESS: quantity supplied falls to 1 million
   → IMPORTS = 3 − 1 = 2 million cars a year
```

**Welfare effects (slide 24):**

```
Consumer surplus  rises from CS  to  CS + A + B
Producer surplus  FALLS by area A
   A = a TRANSFER from producers to consumers
   B = the GAIN FROM TRADE — a net increase in total surplus
```

### The exporting case — Australian coal (L12, slides 25–27)

```
World price $75/tonne > domestic price $50/tonne
   → Australia has the COMPARATIVE ADVANTAGE in coal
   → the Australian price RISES to $75
   → Australian miners PRODUCE MORE:   175 million tonnes
   → Australian power stations BUY LESS:  50 million tonnes
   → EXPORTS = 175 − 50 = 125 million tonnes a year

Producer surplus rises from PS to PS + A + B
Consumer surplus FALLS by area A
   A = transfer from consumers to producers
   B = the gain from trade
```

| | Importer | Exporter |
|---|---|---|
| World price vs domestic | **Lower** | **Higher** |
| Domestic price moves | Down | Up |
| Domestic **consumption** | **Up** | **Down** |
| Domestic **production** | **Down** | **Up** |
| Who gains area A | Consumers, from producers | Producers, from consumers |
| Area B (gain from trade) | Added to CS | Added to PS |
| Total surplus | **Rises** | **Rises** |

> Trade **always raises total surplus** in this model, but it **always
> creates losers**. Australian car makers lose from importing cars;
> Australian coal *buyers* lose from exporting coal. Tutorial 12 Q2(d) asks
> exactly this — "who in Australia loses from free trade in shoes?" — and the
> answer is domestic shoe **producers** and their workers, not consumers.
> Naming the losing group and the area they lose is what earns the mark.

**Tutorial 12 Q3 — Brazilian sugar.** The world price is 20¢/kg and Brazil's
no-trade equilibrium is 10¢/kg.

```
World price (20¢) > domestic price (10¢)
   → the Brazilian price RISES to 20¢
   → Brazilians BUY LESS sugar
   → Brazilian growers PRODUCE MORE sugar
   → Brazil EXPORTS sugar, because it has the comparative advantage
```

## Tariffs

> A **tariff** is a **tax on a good imposed by the importing country** when
> the good crosses its international boundary.

The lecture's illustration: Country A imposes a **100% tariff** on Australian
wine, so a merchant importing a \$10 bottle pays the government \$10 of import
duty.

### The tariff diagram

```
   Price ($'000 per car)
     │╲                         ╱ S
     │ ╲     ┌── CS after ──┐ ╱
  21 │──╲────┼──────●───────●─────────  world price + $6,000 TARIFF
     │   ╲   │  ▓D │   C    │▓D│           imports shrink to 0.5m
  15 │────╲──●─────┼────────┼──●───────  WORLD PRICE $15,000
     │  B  ╲╱   B  │        │  ╲            imports were 2.0m
     │     ╱╲      │        │   ╲ D
     └────┴──┴─────┴────────┴────┴─── Q (million cars)
         1.0 1.75          2.25  3.0

   B = transfer from consumers to DOMESTIC PRODUCERS  (PS expands)
   C = TARIFF REVENUE to the government
   D = the two DEADWEIGHT LOSS triangles
```

**Worked example (L12, slide 30).**

```
Free trade:  world price $15,000
             Australian production 1.00m, purchases 3.00m
             IMPORTS = 2.00 million cars

$6,000 tariff → the Australian price rises to $21,000
             Australian production rises to 1.75m
             Australian purchases fall to   2.25m
             IMPORTS = 2.25 − 1.75 = 0.50 million cars
```

Now the welfare arithmetic, every figure recomputed:

```
Tariff revenue = tariff × imports
               = $6,000 × 500,000 = $3,000,000,000  = $3 BILLION a year

Consumer surplus LOST
   = $6,000 × 2.25m  +  ½ × $6,000 × (3.00 − 2.25)m
   = $13.50bn        +  $2.25bn                     = $15.75 bn

Producer surplus GAINED
   = $6,000 × 1.00m  +  ½ × $6,000 × (1.75 − 1.00)m
   = $6.00bn         +  $2.25bn                     =  $8.25 bn

DEADWEIGHT LOSS = CS lost − PS gained − tariff revenue
                = $15.75bn − $8.25bn − $3.00bn      =  $4.50 bn
   cross-check (the two D triangles):
   ½ × $6,000 × 0.75m  +  ½ × $6,000 × 0.75m
   = $2.25bn + $2.25bn                              =  $4.50 bn   ✓
```

> **L12 slide 30 states the tariff revenue as "\$3 million a year". It is
> \$3 billion.** 500,000 cars × \$6,000 = \$3,000,000,000. Every other figure
> on the slide reconciles — the imports arithmetic (2.25 − 1.75 = 0.5) is
> stated correctly — so this is a units slip, not a different model. Use
> \$3 billion, show the multiplication, and mention it to your tutor.

### Who wins and who loses from a tariff

| Group | Effect | Area |
|---|---|---|
| **Domestic consumers** | **Lose** — higher price, less consumed | CS shrinks by B + C + both D |
| **Domestic producers** | **Gain** — higher price, more produced | PS expands by B (a transfer *from* consumers) |
| **Government** | **Gains** tariff revenue | C |
| **Society** | **Net loss** | The two **D** triangles — the **deadweight loss** |

The left-hand D triangle is **production inefficiency**: domestic firms with
costs above the world price are now producing. The right-hand D triangle is
**consumption inefficiency**: consumers who valued a car above \$15,000 but
below \$21,000 no longer buy one.

> A tariff is *not* a pure transfer. B and C move surplus from one group to
> another and net out to zero, but the two D triangles are **destroyed** —
> nobody receives them. An answer that says "the tariff just redistributes
> from consumers to producers and government" has missed the deadweight loss,
> which is the point of the diagram.
>
> And note that the government's revenue is `tariff × imports`, **not**
> `tariff × total consumption`. The tariff is only paid on the 0.5 million
> cars that still cross the border. Using 2.25 million gives \$13.5 billion
> and destroys the welfare arithmetic.

**Tutorial 12 Q4 — roses.** Who lobbies for the restriction? **Australian rose
growers** — they are the group that gains area B. With a \$10-per-dozen
tariff: the Australian price rises by \$10, domestic production rises, domestic
purchases fall, imports shrink, consumer surplus falls, producer surplus rises,
government collects `$10 × (new import quantity)`, and two deadweight-loss
triangles appear either side of the tariff revenue rectangle.

## Trade and sustainable development

Lecture 12 links trade to the UN Sustainable Development Goals. The Rio+20
outcome and the 2030 Agenda recognise that international trade plays "a
fundamental role in promoting sustained, inclusive and sustainable economic
growth, creating jobs, raising incomes and enhancing welfare". Agenda 21's four
calls: promote sustainable development through **trade liberalisation**; make
**trade and environment mutually supportive**; provide **adequate financial
resources** to developing countries with international debt; and encourage
**macroeconomic policies conducive to environment and development**.

**Sustainable development** is defined in the lecture as *economic development
conducted without depletion of natural resources* — with the **tragedy of the
commons** as the counter-case.

<details>
<summary>1. Country X can produce 100 tonnes of wheat or 50 cars. Country Y can produce 60 tonnes of wheat or 60 cars. Who has the comparative advantage in each good?</summary>

```
Opportunity cost of 1 CAR:
   X: 100 wheat ÷ 50 cars = 2.0 tonnes of wheat
   Y:  60 wheat ÷ 60 cars = 1.0 tonne of wheat      → Y, comparative
                                                       advantage in CARS

Opportunity cost of 1 TONNE OF WHEAT:
   X: 50 cars ÷ 100 wheat = 0.5 cars                → X, comparative
   Y: 60 cars ÷  60 wheat = 1.0 car                    advantage in WHEAT
```

Note X has the absolute advantage in wheat (100 > 60) and Y in cars (60 > 50);
here the two criteria agree, but it is the opportunity costs that decide.

Any trade price strictly between **1 and 2 tonnes of wheat per car** benefits
both.
</details>

<details>
<summary>2. The domestic price of steel is $800 a tonne. The world price is $500. Does the country import or export, and what happens to domestic production and consumption?</summary>

World price (\$500) is **below** the domestic price (\$800), so the country
**imports** steel.

- Domestic price **falls** to \$500.
- Domestic **consumption rises** (move right along D).
- Domestic **production falls** (move left along S).
- Imports = the horizontal gap between them at \$500.
- **CS rises**, **PS falls**, **total surplus rises** by the gain-from-trade
  triangle.
</details>

<details>
<summary>3. A country imports 4 million units at a world price of $30. A $5 tariff raises the price to $35, domestic production rises from 2m to 3m and consumption falls from 6m to 5m. Find imports, tariff revenue and the deadweight loss.</summary>

```
Imports after tariff = 5m − 3m = 2 million    (were 4 million)

Tariff revenue = $5 × 2,000,000 = $10,000,000 = $10 million

DWL = production triangle + consumption triangle
    = ½ × $5 × (3 − 2)m  +  ½ × $5 × (6 − 5)m
    = $2.5m + $2.5m
    = $5 million
```

Cross-check: CS lost = `$5 × 5m + ½ × $5 × 1m` = \$27.5m; PS gained =
`$5 × 2m + ½ × $5 × 1m` = \$12.5m; 27.5 − 12.5 − 10 = **\$5m** ✓
</details>

<details>
<summary>4. Australia exports coal. Name the group that loses and explain why the country still gains overall.</summary>

**Australian coal buyers** (domestic users such as power stations) lose:
exporting raises the domestic price from \$50 to \$75, so they pay more and
buy less. Their lost surplus is area **A**.

The country still gains because producers gain **A + B** — area A is only a
**transfer** from consumers to producers, while area **B** is a **net
addition** to total surplus. `PS gain (A + B) > CS loss (A)`, so total surplus
rises by B.
</details>

<details>
<summary>5. Why does a tariff create two deadweight-loss triangles rather than one?</summary>

Because it distorts **both sides** of the market at once.

- The **left** triangle is **production inefficiency**: domestic producers
  whose marginal cost exceeds the world price are induced to produce, so
  resources are wasted making at home what could be bought cheaper abroad.
- The **right** triangle is **consumption inefficiency**: buyers who value the
  good above the world price but below the tariff-inclusive price stop buying,
  so mutually beneficial trades do not happen.

Between them sits the tariff revenue rectangle, which is a transfer rather than
a loss.
</details>

## Summary

- The **PPF** shows what is attainable; **trade lets a country consume
  outside its own PPF**.
- **Absolute advantage** = more productive. **Comparative advantage** = lower
  **opportunity cost**. **Specialise on comparative advantage.**
- Opportunity cost of good A = (units of B given up) ÷ (units of A gained).
  The two goods' opportunity costs are **reciprocals**; the same country
  cannot have the comparative advantage in both.
- A trade price benefits both parties only if it lies **strictly between** the
  two opportunity costs.
- **World price < domestic price → IMPORT.** Domestic price falls, consumption
  rises, production falls, CS rises by A + B, PS falls by A, total surplus
  rises by **B**.
- **World price > domestic price → EXPORT.** Domestic price rises, production
  rises, consumption falls, PS rises by A + B, CS falls by A, total surplus
  rises by **B**.
- A **tariff** raises the domestic price by the tariff, raises domestic
  production, cuts domestic consumption, and **shrinks imports**.
  `Tariff revenue = tariff × IMPORTS` (not total consumption). Two deadweight
  loss triangles — production inefficiency and consumption inefficiency.
- **Only tariffs are examinable.** Import quotas, other import barriers, export
  subsidies and "the case against protection" are excluded.
