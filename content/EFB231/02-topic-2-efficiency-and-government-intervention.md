pp. 433–442

This is the topic Assessment 1 is built on, and the diagram apparatus here
(surplus areas, deadweight loss triangles) comes back in monopoly, in tariffs
and in every "evaluate this policy" question in the unit. Learn to *shade*
the areas, not just name them.

## Gains from exchange

The lecture opens with Jack and Jill and a vase (L4, slides 11–13). Jill will
part with it for \$30; Jack values it at \$100; they settle on \$50.

```
Jack's consumer surplus  = value to buyer − price paid   = $100 − $50 = $50
Jill's producer surplus  = price received − opportunity cost = $50 − $30 = $20
Total surplus (gain from this one trade)                 = $70
```

Both sides gain. That is the whole point: a voluntary trade at any price
between \$30 and \$100 makes both of them better off. The price only decides
how the \$70 is split.

Two identities make every diagram in this topic work:

| Curve | Also called | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Demand | **Marginal benefit (MB)** | Height of the demand curve at quantity Q = what someone is willing to pay for the Qth unit |
| Supply | **Marginal cost (MC)** | Height of the supply curve at Q = what it costs to produce the Qth unit |

> "Demand = marginal benefit" is a statement about *height*, not area. The
> height of D at Q = 5,000 is the value of the 5,000th unit to its buyer, not
> the total value of 5,000 units. Total value is the area *under* the curve.
> Mixing height and area is what produces surplus answers that are out by a
> factor of two.

## Consumer surplus, producer surplus, total surplus

```
Consumer surplus (CS) = area BELOW the demand curve and ABOVE the price
Producer surplus (PS) = area ABOVE the supply curve and BELOW the price
Total surplus (TS)    = CS + PS
```

With straight-line curves both areas are triangles, so:

```
CS = ½ × Q* × (demand choke price − P*)
PS = ½ × Q* × (P* − supply intercept)
```

**Worked example — pizzas (L4, slides 15–19).** Market price \$10, 10,000
pizzas a day. The demand curve hits the price axis at \$20 (the 5,000th pizza
is valued at \$15, and the curve is linear), and the supply curve starts at
\$2.

```
Consumer spending    = $10 × 10,000                  = $100,000
CS                   = ½ × 10,000 × ($20 − $10)      =  $50,000
Total benefit        = $100,000 + $50,000            = $150,000

PS                   = ½ × 10,000 × ($10 − $2)       =  $40,000
Cost of the pizzas   = $100,000 − $40,000            =  $60,000
  cross-check: ½ × ($10 − $2) × 10,000 + $2 × 10,000 =  $60,000   ✓
```

The slide gives that cross-check explicitly — a triangle plus a rectangle,
because the supply curve starts at \$2 rather than at the origin. Always ask
where the supply curve hits the price axis before you draw one triangle.

**Efficiency.** Total surplus is maximised at the quantity where `MB = MC`,
which is exactly where D crosses S. That quantity is the **efficient
quantity**. Any intervention that moves the traded quantity away from it
destroys surplus, and the surplus destroyed is the **deadweight loss (DWL)** —
"a cost to society created by market inefficiency" (L4, slide 26).

### The market used throughout this note

Tutorial 4 Q1 gives a sandwich market. From the answers that *are* verifiable
(equilibrium \$4 at 200 sandwiches an hour, CS = PS = \$400, quota DWL =
\$200) the schedules are:

```
Demand:  Qd = 400 − 50P      i.e.  P = 8 − Q/50     (choke price $8)
Supply:  Qs = 50P            i.e.  P = Q/50         (intercept $0)

Equilibrium:  400 − 50P = 50P  →  P* = $4,  Q* = 200
CS = ½ × 200 × ($8 − $4) = $400
PS = ½ × 200 × ($4 − $0) = $400
TS = $800
```

Every number below is computed from those two lines.

> **Two answers in the Tutorial 4 sheet do not follow from its own table.**
> Part (b) — demand falls by 100 at every price — gives `Qd = 300 − 50P`,
> so P\* = \$3, Q\* = 150, and TS = ½ × 150 × \$6 = **\$450**, a fall of
> **\$350**. The sheet says \$300 and a fall of \$500. Part (c) — supply
> falls by 100 at every price — gives `Qs = 50P − 100`, so P\* = \$5,
> Q\* = 150, and TS = ½ × 150 × (\$8 − \$2) = **\$450** again, a fall of
> **\$350**. The sheet says \$750 and a fall of \$50. Parts (a) and (d) both
> reconcile exactly, so the schedules are right and (b) and (c) are the
> errors. Check with your tutor before relying on the sheet.

## Price ceiling

A **price ceiling** is a legal maximum price. It only bites if it is set
**below** equilibrium.

```
         P
         │╲                 ╱ S
      $8 │ ╲              ╱
         │  ╲     DWL   ╱
      $5 │   ╲___     ╱          ← MB of the 150th sandwich
      $4 │      ╲ ● ╱            ← free-market equilibrium
      $3 │───────╳────────────── ← CEILING (binding, below $4)
         │     ╱   ╲
         │   ╱  ↑    ╲  D
         └──╱───┼──────╲──────── Q
           150  │      250
            Qs  │       Qd
                └── SHORTAGE of 100
```

**Worked example — a \$3 ceiling on the sandwich market.**

```
At P = $3:   Qs = 50 × 3       = 150   (this is what actually gets traded)
             Qd = 400 − 50×3   = 250
             Shortage          = 100 sandwiches an hour

MB of the 150th sandwich = 8 − 150/50 = $5     (buyers value it at $5)
MC of the 150th sandwich =     150/50 = $3

CS = ½ × 150 × ($8 − $5)  +  150 × ($5 − $3)  =  $225 + $300 = $525
PS = ½ × 150 × ($3 − $0)                      =  $225
TS = $750        (was $800)

DWL = ½ × (200 − 150) × ($5 − $3) = ½ × 50 × $2 = $50   ✓ matches $800 − $750
```

Note the shape of CS: a triangle *plus* a rectangle. The rectangle is surplus
transferred from producers to consumers by the lower price. That is why CS can
**rise** under a ceiling even though total surplus always falls.

The lecture lists three consequences (L4, slide 26): deadweight loss, a
**shortage** (and the black market it invites), and **resources lost to
non-monetary costs** — queuing, search, waiting. That third one matters:
if buyers burn \$X of time queuing, that \$X comes straight out of consumer
surplus, so the real CS is lower than the diagram suggests.

> The number that changes hands is **not** the number demanded. Under a
> binding ceiling the quantity traded is the quantity **supplied** — the short
> side of the market always wins. Compute every surplus area over `Q = Qs`,
> never over `Qd`. Getting this backwards is the single most common error in
> ceiling questions.

## Price floor

A **price floor** is a legal minimum price and only bites if set **above**
equilibrium. The canonical example is the minimum wage.

**Worked example — a \$5 floor on the same market.**

```
At P = $5:   Qd = 400 − 50×5   = 150   (this is what actually gets traded)
             Qs = 50 × 5       = 250
             Surplus (glut)    = 100

MC of the 150th sandwich = 150/50 = $3

CS = ½ × 150 × ($8 − $5)                      = $225
PS = ½ × 150 × ($3 − $0)  +  150 × ($5 − $3)  = $225 + $300 = $525
TS = $750        DWL = $50
```

Mirror image of the ceiling: the transfer rectangle now runs from consumers to
producers, and the quantity traded is the quantity **demanded**.

L4 slide 30 lists the consequences of a floor in a labour market: higher
**unemployment**, deadweight loss, **increased search activity**, and illegal
hiring below the floor.

| | Ceiling | Floor |
|---|---|---|
| Set | Below equilibrium | Above equilibrium |
| Binding when | P<sub>ceiling</sub> < P\* | P<sub>floor</sub> > P\* |
| Excess | Shortage (Qd > Qs) | Surplus (Qs > Qd) |
| Quantity traded | Qs (the smaller one) | Qd (the smaller one) |
| CS | May rise | Falls |
| PS | Falls | May rise |
| TS | Falls — DWL | Falls — DWL |
| Wasted resources | Queuing, search | Job search, idle output |

> A ceiling set *above* the equilibrium price and a floor set *below* it do
> **nothing at all**. No shortage, no surplus, no deadweight loss. If an exam
> question gives you a ceiling of \$6 in a market that clears at \$4, the
> answer is "not binding — the market outcome is unchanged". Check which side
> of equilibrium the control sits on before you start shading triangles.

## Taxes and tax incidence

A per-unit tax on sellers shifts the **supply curve up (leftwards) by exactly
the amount of the tax**, because sellers now need the old price plus the tax
to be willing to supply the same quantity.

```
         P                S+tax
         │              ╱  ╱ S
    P_b  │────────────●╱  ╱          buyer's price
         │           ╱│ ╱
     P*  │─────────╱──┼●───────      old equilibrium
         │       ╱    │  ╲
    P_s  │─────╱──────●    ╲         seller's net-of-tax price
         │   ╱        │      ╲ D
         └──────────Q_new──Q*──────── Q
                     ↑
              tax wedge = P_b − P_s
```

**Worked example (L4, slide 31).** Pre-tax equilibrium at \$100. A \$10 tax
is imposed. The buyer ends up paying \$105 and the seller keeps \$95 — a
\$5/\$5 split.

**Worked example with full arithmetic — a \$2 tax on the sandwich market.**

```
Supply with tax:  Qs = 50(P_b − 2) = 50P_b − 100
Equilibrium:      400 − 50P_b = 50P_b − 100  →  P_b = $5,  Q = 150
Seller receives:  P_s = $5 − $2 = $3

Buyer's burden  = $5 − $4 = $1 per sandwich  → 50% of the tax
Seller's burden = $4 − $3 = $1 per sandwich  → 50% of the tax

Tax revenue     = $2 × 150                   = $300
CS              = ½ × 150 × ($8 − $5)        = $225   (was $400)
PS              = ½ × 150 × ($3 − $0)        = $225   (was $400)
CS + PS + revenue = $225 + $225 + $300       = $750
DWL             = $800 − $750                = $50
  cross-check:  ½ × (200 − 150) × $2         = $50    ✓
```

The DWL of a tax is also called its **excess burden** — the loss over and
above the revenue raised.

### Who bears the tax? Elasticities decide

```
Buyer's share of the tax   =        PES
                              ──────────────
                               PES + |PED|
```

At the sandwich equilibrium `PED = (dQ/dP)(P/Q) = −50 × 4/200 = −1` and
`PES = 50 × 4/200 = +1`, so the shares are 1/(1+1) = 50% each. That is why the
split came out even.

| Case | Diagram | Who pays more |
|---|---|---|
| **D elastic, S inelastic** (L4 slide 34, panel 1) | Flat D, steep S. The vertical tax wedge sits mostly *below* the old price | **Sellers** — buyers walk away, so the price to buyers barely rises |
| **D inelastic, S elastic** (panel 2) | Steep D, flat S. The wedge sits mostly *above* the old price | **Buyers** — buyers have nowhere to go, so the price to buyers rises nearly the full tax |

The rule in one line: **the more inelastic side bears more of the tax.**

> The legal side of the tax is irrelevant to who actually pays it. Taxing
> buyers shifts D down by the tax instead of shifting S up by the tax, and the
> resulting P<sub>b</sub>, P<sub>s</sub>, Q, revenue and DWL are *identical*.
> Never answer "the sellers pay it because the tax is levied on sellers".

## Production quota

A **production quota** is an upper limit on output. Set below the efficient
quantity, it produces a deadweight loss for the same reason a ceiling does —
units whose MB exceeds their MC do not get made.

**Worked example (Tutorial 4, Q1d).** A monopolising buyer limits sandwich
production to 100 an hour.

```
At Q = 100:  MB = 8 − 100/50 = $6      MC = 100/50 = $2
Every unit between 100 and 200 has MB > MC and is not produced.

DWL = ½ × (200 − 100) × ($6 − $2) = ½ × 100 × $4 = $200
```

L4 slides 35–36 add the mirror case: a quota that *forces* output **above** the
efficient quantity (10,000 in the slide, pushed to 12,500). Then MC > MB on
every extra unit, and:

- at point A, **consumer surplus is negative** — the value to the buyer is
  below the market price;
- at point B, **producer surplus is negative** — the cost to the seller is
  above the market price.

The slide describes this as "deadweight loss is a negative value", meaning the
change in total surplus is negative in the *overproduction* direction. Either
way, surplus is destroyed.

> A quota *above* the equilibrium quantity is not binding — firms would not
> have produced that much anyway. Overproduction only happens if something
> also compels the extra output (a subsidy, a mandate). Read whether the
> question sets a *cap* or a *requirement*.

## Negative production externalities

**Only negative production externalities are assessable** (L4, slide 39,
in terms). Positive externalities and consumption externalities are
explicitly out.

```
Marginal private cost (MC)    = cost borne by the producer
Marginal external cost (MEC)  = cost falling on everyone else
Marginal social cost (MSC)    = MC + MEC
```

The market equates MB with **MC**, but efficiency requires MB = **MSC**. Since
MSC sits above MC, the market **overproduces**.

```
         P
         │╲            ╱ MSC = MC + MEC
         │ ╲         ╱ ╱ MC (= S)
      $8 │  ●      ╱ ╱          efficient point (MB = MSC)
         │   ╲   ╱ ╱
      $4 │    ╲╱ ●              market equilibrium (MB = MC)
         │   ╱ ╲    ╲
         │ ╱     ╲    ╲ D = MB
         └───┬─────┬────────── Q (million litres)
             2     4
             ↑     ↑
        efficient  market      DWL = the triangle between MSC and MB
                               over the range 2 → 4
```

**Worked example (numbers constructed to match L4 slide 41's Q = 4 market
outcome and Q = 2 efficient outcome).**

```
MB  = 12 − 2Q        MC = Q        MEC = $6 per litre        (Q in millions)

Market:     12 − 2Q = Q       →  Q = 4 million,  P = $4
Efficient:  12 − 2Q = Q + 6   →  Q = 2 million,  MB = $8

At Q = 4:   MSC = 4 + 6 = $10   but   MB = $4    → $6 of loss on that litre
DWL = ½ × (4 − 2) × ($10 − $4) = ½ × 2 × $6 = $6 million
```

### Two fixes (L4, slides 42–43)

| Fix | How it works | Weakness stated in the lecture |
|---|---|---|
| **1. Mandate clean technology** | The EPA requires best-practice abatement and caps emissions | "Not always the least-cost solution" — every firm must abate the same way regardless of its own abatement cost |
| **2. Tax equal to the marginal external cost** | Set `tax = MEC`. Firms' private supply curve shifts up onto MSC, so the market clears at `P = MSC` and the efficient quantity | (Requires knowing MEC) |

With the numbers above, a \$6-per-litre tax shifts private MC from `Q` to
`Q + 6`, the market lands on Q = 2 million, and the government collects
`$6 × 2 million = $12 million` (the purple rectangle in the slide).

> The tax that corrects a negative externality is set equal to the **marginal
> external cost**, not to the deadweight loss and not to the total external
> cost. And after the tax the quantity **falls to the efficient level** — it
> does not fall to zero. "Tax it until the pollution stops" is not the
> efficient answer; some pollution is worth tolerating because the output is
> worth more than the harm.

## Are markets fair?

Efficiency and fairness are different questions (L4, slides 21–24). The
lecture puts two views of fairness side by side:

- **"It's not fair if the rules aren't fair"** — fairness of *process* and
  ownership. Why am I taxed more?
- **"It's not fair if the result isn't fair"** — fairness of *outcome*. Why am
  I not paid more?

And it distinguishes **equality** (equal income) from **equity** (equal
opportunity) — they are not the same thing.

The recurring application is the cyclone. Cyclone Larry destroyed north
Queensland's banana crop; supply shifted **left**, so the equilibrium price
**rose** and equilibrium quantity **fell**. That high price is *efficient* —
it rations the scarce bananas to whoever values them most and signals other
growers to send fruit north. Whether it is *fair* is a separate argument, and
the lecture deliberately leaves it open (the in-class poll on capping banana
prices is asked twice, before and after the price-ceiling analysis, so that
students can change their answer).

> Tutorial 4 Q2 asks you to explain why the market price method is efficient
> *and* whether the high price is fair. The tutorial's filled-in answer only
> does the supply-shift half. A full answer needs the efficiency argument
> (price = MB = MC allocates to the highest-value users, and no ceiling means
> no shortage or queuing) *and* an explicit fairness position using one of the
> two views above. Answering only "supply shifted left so price rose" leaves
> most of the marks on the table.

## Checkpoint

<details>
<summary>1. The market for DVDs clears at $16. A price ceiling of $12 is imposed. Is consumer surplus higher or lower than before?</summary>

**Ambiguous by design — you must compute it.** The ceiling does two things to
CS at once: it *adds* a transfer rectangle (buyers who still get a DVD pay \$4
less each) and it *subtracts* a triangle (some buyers get no DVD at all).
Whether CS rises or falls depends on how big the shortage is.

What is *never* ambiguous: producer surplus falls, and total surplus falls by
the deadweight loss.
</details>

<details>
<summary>2. A $20 tax is imposed on textbooks and the demand for textbooks is perfectly elastic. What price do buyers pay?</summary>

**Unchanged.** A perfectly elastic demand curve is horizontal: buyers will not
pay a cent more. Sellers bear the **entire** \$20.

Use the incidence formula: buyer's share = PES / (PES + |PED|). With
|PED| = ∞ that ratio is 0.
</details>

<details>
<summary>3. In the sandwich market (Qd = 400 − 50P, Qs = 50P), the government imposes a $2 subsidy per sandwich paid to sellers. Find the new quantity, the two prices, the cost to government and the deadweight loss.</summary>

A subsidy is a negative tax — supply shifts **down** by \$2.

```
Qs = 50(P_b + 2) = 50P_b + 100
400 − 50P_b = 50P_b + 100  →  P_b = $3,  Q = 250
Seller receives P_s = $3 + $2 = $5

Cost to government = $2 × 250 = $500

CS = ½ × 250 × ($8 − $3) = $625
PS = ½ × 250 × ($5 − $0) = $625
TS = $625 + $625 − $500 = $750

DWL = $800 − $750 = $50
  cross-check: ½ × (250 − 200) × $2 = $50   ✓
```

A subsidy causes **over**production and destroys surplus just as a tax causes
underproduction and destroys surplus. Both move Q away from 200.
</details>

<details>
<summary>4. Petrol production emits pollution. The market is unregulated. Is the equilibrium quantity above, below or equal to the efficient quantity?</summary>

**Above.** Firms equate MB with their *private* MC and ignore the marginal
external cost, so they produce past the point where MB = MSC. The correction
is a tax equal to MEC, which reduces output to the efficient level.

The L5 review question (slide 6) inverts this: "there is *no* external cost
from petrol", in which case the unregulated equilibrium is simply where D
crosses S and is already efficient. Read whether the question switches the
externality off.
</details>

<details>
<summary>5. Under a binding price ceiling, which is bigger — the quantity demanded or the quantity actually bought?</summary>

Quantity **demanded** is bigger. Buyers want Qd but only Qs exists, so
`Qd − Qs` is the shortage and only Qs changes hands. All surplus areas are
measured over Qs.
</details>

## Summary

- **CS** = area below D, above P. **PS** = area above S, below P.
  **TS = CS + PS**, and it is maximised where **MB = MC**.
- Demand is the **marginal benefit** curve; supply is the **marginal cost**
  curve. Height is the value of the marginal unit; area is the total.
- Any intervention that moves quantity off Q\* creates a **deadweight loss**
  triangle, whose base is the change in quantity and whose height is the
  MB–MC gap at the new quantity.
- **Ceiling** below equilibrium → shortage; trade at **Qs**; CS may rise, PS
  falls, TS falls.
- **Floor** above equilibrium → surplus; trade at **Qd**; PS may rise, CS
  falls, TS falls.
- A **per-unit tax** shifts S up by the tax. The wedge `P_b − P_s` equals the
  tax; revenue = tax × new Q; **excess burden** = the DWL triangle. The more
  **inelastic** side bears more of it, whichever side is legally liable.
- A **quota** below Q\* underproduces (positive DWL); forced output above Q\*
  drives CS and PS negative on the extra units.
- A **negative production externality** means `MSC = MC + MEC`, so the market
  overproduces. The efficient correction is a tax **equal to MEC**, which
  moves output to where MB = MSC — not to zero.
- **Efficiency ≠ fairness.** Fairness comes in a rules version and a results
  version, and equality is not the same as equity.
