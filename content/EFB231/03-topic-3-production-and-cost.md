Lecture 5 · Tutorial 5 · textbook Ch. 10 pp. 266–267, Ch. 11

Everything in Topic 4 (the four market structures) is built on the cost curves
derived here. If you cannot fill in a TP/TVC/TC/AFC/AVC/ATC/MC table quickly
and accurately, you will lose marks in three separate topics, not one.

## Two ways to measure profit

The firm's goal in this unit is **profit maximisation** (L5, slide 12). But
"profit" means two different things depending on who is holding the
calculator.

```
Total revenue     = Price × Quantity

Accounting profit = Total revenue − accounting cost
   where accounting cost = explicit costs + accounting depreciation

Economic profit   = Total revenue − opportunity cost
   where opportunity cost = explicit costs + implicit costs
   and implicit costs include normal profit and economic depreciation
```

**Implicit cost** is the opportunity cost of a factor of production the firm
**already owns and therefore does not pay rent on** — the owner's own labour,
the owner's own capital, the shopfront the owner bought outright.

**Normal profit** is the cost of entrepreneurship: the minimum profit a firm
must earn to stay in business, counted as an opportunity cost of production
(L5, slide 17).

**Worked example.** Sam quits a \$70,000 job to run a coffee shop. She puts in
\$200,000 of her own savings that would otherwise have earned 5% in the bank.
Revenue is \$400,000; wages, beans, rent and power come to \$310,000.

```
Accounting profit = $400,000 − $310,000                       = $90,000

Implicit costs    = forgone salary $70,000
                  + forgone interest 5% × $200,000 = $10,000
                                                              = $80,000

Economic profit   = $400,000 − ($310,000 + $80,000)           = $10,000
```

Both numbers are correct answers to different questions. Accounting profit is
\$90,000; economic profit is \$10,000; the firm is earning **more than normal
profit** and is worth continuing.

> Economic profit is always ≤ accounting profit, and the gap is exactly the
> implicit costs. If a question says "the owner could earn \$X elsewhere" or
> "the owner's own building could be rented for \$Y", those are implicit costs
> and they belong in the economic-cost total. Zero **economic** profit is not
> failure — it means the firm is earning exactly normal profit, which is the
> long-run outcome for a competitive firm.

## Short run versus long run

| | Definition |
|---|---|
| **Short run** | A time frame in which **at least one input is fixed** |
| **Long run** | A time frame in which **all inputs can be varied** |

Not a number of months. A **fixed input** does not change in quantity when
output changes; a **variable input** does. For a coffee shop the shop lease
and the espresso machine are fixed; baristas, milk and beans are variable.

## Short-run production: TP, MP, AP

```
Total product   (TP) = total output in a period
Marginal product(MP) = ΔTP from employing one more unit of labour
Average product (AP) = TP ÷ L        (also called labour productivity)
```

Three phases as labour rises (L5, slide 24):

1. **Increasing marginal returns** — MP rising. Specialisation and division of
   labour.
2. **Decreasing (diminishing) marginal returns** — MP falling but still
   positive. Eventually inevitable, because the fixed input gets crowded.
   *"Too many cooks spoil the broth."*
3. **Negative marginal returns** — MP < 0. Extra workers actually reduce
   output.

**The MP–AP relationship (L5, slide 25):**

```
MP > AP  →  AP is rising
MP < AP  →  AP is falling
MP = AP  →  AP is at its maximum
```

MP cuts AP from above at AP's peak. This is the same arithmetic as the
lecture's weight illustration (slides 35–36): add someone heavier than the
average and the average goes up.

> MP falling is **not** the same as TP falling. Through the whole decreasing-
> marginal-returns phase, TP is still *rising* — just more slowly. TP only
> falls once MP goes negative. "Diminishing returns means output falls" is
> wrong.

## Short-run cost

```
TFC = total fixed cost      — unchanged by output; paid even at Q = 0
TVC = total variable cost   — zero at Q = 0; rises with output
TC  = TFC + TVC

AFC = TFC / Q
AVC = TVC / Q
ATC = TC  / Q = AFC + AVC

MC  = ΔTC / ΔQ   ( = ΔTVC / ΔQ, since TFC doesn't change )
```

Two links back to the product curves that make everything click:

```
MC  = wage ÷ MP          (the extra cost of a unit is the wage divided by
                          how many units the extra worker adds)

AVC = wage ÷ AP          (variable cost per unit is the wage divided by
                          output per worker)
```

So **MC is the mirror image of MP**, and **AVC is the mirror image of AP**.
When MP is at its maximum, MC is at its minimum. When AP is at its maximum,
AVC is at its minimum.

### Worked example — Pete's peanut farm (Tutorial 5, Q1)

Labour costs \$35 a worker; total fixed cost is \$100.

| L | TP | TVC | TC | AFC | AVC | ATC | MC | MP | AP |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0 | 0 | 0 | 100 | — | — | — | — | — | — |
| 1 | 10 | 35 | 135 | 10.00 | 3.50 | 13.50 | 3.50 | 10 | 10.0 |
| 2 | 24 | 70 | 170 | 4.17 | 2.92 | 7.08 | 2.50 | 14 | 12.0 |
| 3 | 38 | 105 | 205 | 2.63 | 2.76 | 5.39 | 2.50 | 14 | 12.7 |
| 4 | 44 | 140 | 240 | 2.27 | 3.18 | 5.45 | 5.83 | 6 | 11.0 |

Every cell recomputed from the definitions:

```
TVC at L=3     = $35 × 3          = $105
TC  at L=3     = $100 + $105      = $205
AFC at L=3     = $100 ÷ 38        = $2.6316
AVC at L=3     = $105 ÷ 38        = $2.7632
ATC at L=3     = $205 ÷ 38        = $5.3947   ( = 2.6316 + 2.7632 ✓ )
MC  from 2→3   = ($205 − $170) ÷ (38 − 24) = $35 ÷ 14 = $2.50
  cross-check  = wage ÷ MP = $35 ÷ 14 = $2.50   ✓
AVC at L=3     = wage ÷ AP = $35 ÷ (38/3) = $35 ÷ 12.667 = $2.7632  ✓
```

Read the story off the table: MP rises from 10 to 14 (increasing marginal
returns through the second worker), holds at 14, then collapses to 6
(**diminishing marginal returns set in with the fourth worker**). MC does the
reverse — \$3.50, \$2.50, \$2.50, \$5.83. ATC bottoms out at \$5.39 and
starts rising once MC (\$5.83) climbs above it.

> **Marginal cost is per unit of output, not per worker.** Going from 2 to 3
> workers costs \$35 and buys 14 peanuts, so MC = \$35/14 = \$2.50, not
> \$35. Divide by the marginal *product*, always. This is the most common
> arithmetic slip in the whole topic.
>
> Also: the L = 0 row has no AFC, AVC or ATC — you cannot divide by zero
> output. The tutorial sheet writes 100 in the AFC cell and 100 in the ATC
> cell of that row; those cells are undefined. Leave them blank.

### Worked example — Yolanda's fish farm (Tutorial 5, Q2)

Equipment costs \$1,000 a week (fixed); each worker costs \$500. One worker
produces 1,000 fish; a second doubles total product; a third doubles it again;
a fourth adds only 1,000.

| L | TP | MP | AP | TVC | TC | AFC | AVC | ATC | MC |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0 | 0 | — | — | 0 | 1,000 | — | — | — | — |
| 1 | 1,000 | 1,000 | 1,000 | 500 | 1,500 | 1.000 | 0.500 | 1.500 | 0.50 |
| 2 | 2,000 | 1,000 | 1,000 | 1,000 | 2,000 | 0.500 | 0.500 | 1.000 | 0.50 |
| 3 | 4,000 | 2,000 | 1,333 | 1,500 | 2,500 | 0.250 | 0.375 | 0.625 | 0.25 |
| 4 | 5,000 | 1,000 | 1,250 | 2,000 | 3,000 | 0.200 | 0.400 | 0.600 | 0.50 |

```
TFC = $1,000  (the equipment — it is there at L = 0 and never changes)

Increasing marginal returns: MP goes 1,000 → 1,000 → 2,000, so the rise
happens with the THIRD worker.
Diminishing marginal returns: MP falls 2,000 → 1,000 with the fourth.

MC from 2→3 = ($2,500 − $2,000) ÷ (4,000 − 2,000) = $500 ÷ 2,000 = $0.25
  cross-check = wage ÷ MP = $500 ÷ 2,000 = $0.25   ✓
```

Minimum ATC in the table is **\$0.60 at 5,000 fish** (four workers), which is
what the tutorial answer says.

> But notice MC at the fourth worker is \$0.50, still **below** ATC of \$0.60.
> The rule "MC < ATC → ATC is falling" says a fifth worker would push ATC
> down further. So \$0.60 is the minimum **within the tabulated range**, not
> the true minimum of the ATC curve. Say which one you mean; a question that
> asks for "the minimum ATC" from a truncated table is asking for the smallest
> value shown.

## The shape of the cost curves

```
  $ per unit
    │
    │╲                                    ╱ MC
    │ ╲                                 ╱
    │  ╲                              ╱
    │   ╲  ATC ╲                    ╱
    │    ╲      ╲__            __ ╱ ╱ ATC
    │     ╲ AVC  ╲__●________╱__ ╱
    │      ╲___    ╲___●____╱ AVC
    │  AFC     ╲_____________________
    └───────────────────────────────────── Q
                 ↑        ↑
            min AVC   min ATC
       (MC cuts AVC   (MC cuts ATC
        at its low)    at its low)
```

Facts the diagram encodes, all examinable:

1. **AFC falls continuously** — TFC is a constant divided by a rising Q. It
   never turns up.
2. **The gap between ATC and AVC is AFC**, and it *narrows* as output rises,
   because AFC is shrinking. The two curves converge but never meet.
3. **MC is U-shaped** and cuts **both** AVC and ATC at their **minimum
   points** (L5, slide 33).
4. `MC < ATC → ATC falling`; `MC > ATC → ATC rising`; `MC = ATC → ATC at its
   minimum`. Same three lines with AVC.
5. **min AVC occurs at a lower output than min ATC**, because ATC is still
   being dragged down by falling AFC after AVC has bottomed out.

> Short-run cost curves are U-shaped because of **diminishing marginal
> returns** to the variable input. Long-run average cost is U-shaped because
> of **economies and diseconomies of scale**. These are different causes and
> the exam asks about them separately (L6, review question 2). Never explain
> the LRAC shape with diminishing returns — in the long run there is no fixed
> input, so there are no diminishing returns at all (L5, slide 38).

## Long-run cost

In the long run every input varies, so the firm chooses its **plant size**.
Each plant size has its own short-run average cost curve (SRAC). The
**long-run average cost curve (LRAC)** is the lower envelope of all of them —
the cheapest way to produce each output once you are free to rebuild.

```
  $ per unit
    │
    │╲ SRAC_S       SRAC_M1      SRAC_M2       ╱ SRAC_L
    │ ╲   ╱          ╲   ╱        ╲   ╱      ╱
    │  ╲ ╱            ╲ ╱          ╲ ╱      ╱
    │   ●──────────────●────────────●──────●
    │    ╲__________ LRAC __________╱
    └────────────────────────────────────────── Q
      economies of  │ constant  │ diseconomies
        scale       │ returns   │  of scale
      (LRAC falling)│  (flat)   │ (LRAC rising)
```

| Outcome | Definition (L5, slides 39–42) | Source |
|---|---|---|
| **Economies of scale** | Output increases **and** average total cost **decreases** | Greater specialisation of labour and capital — the Ford assembly line |
| **Constant returns to scale** | Output increases and ATC **stays constant** | The firm can replicate its existing facility, management system and all |
| **Diseconomies of scale** | Output increases **and** ATC **increases** | Difficulty coordinating and controlling a large enterprise; management complexity |

The lecture's own counterweight to the assembly line (slide 43): a bottleneck
in one place stops everything, work becomes repetitive, and errors are hard to
trace. That is diseconomies of scale in concrete form.

**A natural monopoly** is a firm with economies of scale over the entire
relevant range of market demand — LRAC is still falling where demand runs out,
so one firm can always undercut two. This is the link forward to Topic 4.

**Tutorial 5 Q3 — the A380 and the Dreamliner.** The A380 is a bigger "plant":
more seats, higher fixed cost, lower cost per seat-kilometre *if you can fill
it*. Airlines on thick trunk routes choose the large plant and slide down the
LRAC. Airlines on thin routes cannot fill an A380, so at their output level the
large plant's SRAC sits *above* the smaller plant's — they choose the
Dreamliner. Plant size is chosen for the output you expect, not for the lowest
point on any one curve.

**Tutorial 5 Q4 — self-checkouts.** Self-checkouts substitute capital for
labour: TVC per unit falls and TFC rises. So **AVC shifts down, AFC shifts up,
MC shifts down**, and ATC shifts down at high volumes but may shift **up** at
low volumes where the extra fixed cost is spread thinly. Draw both ATC curves
crossing.

> "Economies of scale" describes what happens to **average total cost** as the
> firm changes **plant size** in the long run. It is not "the firm produces
> more", and it is not "total cost falls" — total cost rises with output in
> every case. Say *average* total cost, or lose the mark.

## Checkpoint

<details>
<summary>1. A firm's TFC is $200. At Q = 50, ATC = $12. What is AVC?</summary>

```
TC  = ATC × Q = $12 × 50 = $600
TVC = TC − TFC = $600 − $200 = $400
AVC = $400 ÷ 50 = $8

cross-check: AFC = $200 ÷ 50 = $4, and $4 + $8 = $12 ✓
```
</details>

<details>
<summary>2. MC is $18 and ATC is $22 at the current output. Is ATC rising or falling? What should the firm do if it wants minimum ATC?</summary>

MC < ATC, so **ATC is falling**. To reach minimum ATC the firm should
**increase** output until MC has risen to meet ATC.

Careful: minimum ATC is not the profit-maximising output. That is a different
question (Topic 4) and the answer is MR = MC.
</details>

<details>
<summary>3. A total product schedule reads 0, 12, 28, 40, 48, 52, 52, 48 for L = 0…7. Where do decreasing marginal returns start? Where do negative marginal returns start?</summary>

```
MP:  12, 16, 12, 8, 4, 0, −4    (for the 1st…7th worker)
```

MP rises to 16 with the **second** worker, then falls. **Decreasing marginal
returns start with the third worker.** MP first goes negative with the
**seventh** worker (TP falls from 52 to 48).

Note the sixth worker has MP = 0 — TP is flat, at its maximum. Returns are not
yet negative there.
</details>

<details>
<summary>4. A firm makes $50,000 accounting profit. The owner turned down a $65,000 salary elsewhere and has no other implicit costs. Should they stay in business?</summary>

```
Economic profit = $50,000 − $65,000 = −$15,000
```

**No** — on economics grounds. Economic profit is negative, meaning the owner
is \$15,000 a year worse off than their next-best alternative, even though the
accounts show a profit. Whether they stay is then a question about what else
they value in running the business, which is outside the model.
</details>

<details>
<summary>5. Why does the gap between ATC and AVC get smaller as output rises?</summary>

Because `ATC − AVC = AFC = TFC ÷ Q`. TFC is constant, so as Q rises AFC falls
towards zero and the two curves converge. They never touch, because AFC is
never exactly zero.
</details>

## Summary

- **Accounting profit** = TR − explicit costs (+ accounting depreciation).
  **Economic profit** = TR − opportunity cost, where opportunity cost adds
  **implicit costs** including **normal profit**. Economic profit is the
  smaller number.
- **Short run**: at least one input fixed. **Long run**: all inputs variable.
- `MP = ΔTP/ΔL`, `AP = TP/L`. MP > AP → AP rising; MP cuts AP at AP's peak.
  Increasing → diminishing → negative marginal returns, in that order.
- `TC = TFC + TVC`; `ATC = AFC + AVC`; `MC = ΔTC/ΔQ`.
- `MC = wage/MP` and `AVC = wage/AP` — cost curves are product curves turned
  upside down.
- **AFC always falls.** MC is U-shaped and cuts AVC and ATC at their minima.
  min AVC comes at a lower Q than min ATC.
- Short-run U-shape ← **diminishing marginal returns**. Long-run U-shape ←
  **economies and diseconomies of scale**. Do not swap them.
- **LRAC** is the envelope of the SRAC curves. Economies of scale from
  specialisation; diseconomies from coordination and management complexity;
  constant returns from replication.
