## The One-Sentence Version

**A share fund either tries to copy the market cheaply (passive) or
tries to beat it (active), and the big decision behind both is how the
money is split across asset classes (asset allocation).**

## The Picture

Passive is cruise control: set the speed to match the traffic and stay
in your lane. You'll never win the race, but you'll never crash out
and fuel (fees) is cheap. Active is driving yourself, trying to
overtake. Some drivers do get ahead, but most burn more fuel and end up
behind the cruise-control car. Asset allocation is choosing which
*road* to take in the first place, and research says the road matters
far more than the driving.

## The Words You Need

| Term | Plain meaning |
|---|---|
| **Passive management** | Buy and hold, usually tracking an index. Judged on how closely it tracks |
| **Active management** | Trying to beat a benchmark after costs and risk, i.e. earn **alpha** |
| **Tracking error** | How much the fund's return wanders from the index's. The standard deviation of (fund return − index return) |
| **Full replication** | Buy every share in the index at its index weight. Tracks closely, costs more |
| **Sampling** | Buy a representative subset. Cheaper, more tracking error |
| **Optimisation** | A computer picks the mix that minimises tracking error using past data |
| **Index fund vs ETF** | Both cheap and passive. An **ETF trades on the exchange** like a share (and can be short sold). An index fund is bought from the manager |
| **Asset allocation vs stock selection** | Which asset classes, versus which shares within them. Brinson et al. (1986): allocation explained **93.6%** of the variation in returns |
| **Top-down vs bottom-up** | Economy → sector → share, versus find cheap shares first and ignore the big picture |
| **130/30** | 130% long in favoured shares, 30% short in disliked ones. Uses research both ways |
| **Contrarian vs momentum** | Buy what everyone hates (expecting reversal) versus buy what's been rising (expecting it to continue) |
| **Factor investing** | Tilt toward traits that have paid off: small size, value, low volatility, momentum, quality |
| **Value vs growth** | Value buys *cheap prices* (low P/E). Growth buys *fast earnings growth*, paying up for it |
| **Style analysis** | Work out what a manager really does by matching their returns to style indexes (the size × value/growth grid) |

## How It Works, Step by Step

**1. Tracking error.** Four months of fund-minus-index differences:
+0.5%, −0.3%, +0.2%, −0.4%. The average is 0, so the fund matches the
index on average, but it wobbles.

```
Squares: 0.25 + 0.09 + 0.04 + 0.16 = 0.54
Sheet form (÷ T):  √(0.54 / 4) = 0.37% a month
```

Lower is better for a passive manager. (The official sheet divides by
T, not T − 1. Use the sheet's form.)

**2. Tax efficiency (formula on the sheet).** An active fund earns 10%
before tax (PTR) and 8.5% after (TAR).

```
Tax cost ratio = 1 − (1 + TAR) / (1 + PTR) = 1 − 1.085 / 1.10 = 1.36%
```

High **turnover** (selling a lot, PT = sales ÷ average assets) locks in
capital gains and raises the tax bill. So **active funds usually have a
higher tax cost ratio than passive funds**. That's a checkpoint question.

**3. The four asset allocation strategies** (slides 42–48). Learn them
as a set:

| Strategy | In plain words |
|---|---|
| **Integrated** | Look at market conditions **and** the investor's needs together, then set the mix |
| **Strategic** | A long-run policy mix from long-run averages (e.g. 70% growth / 30% defensive). Rebalance back to it periodically |
| **Tactical** | Shift the mix often to exploit short-term mispricing. Contrarian, relies on mean reversion |
| **Insured** | Two assets (shares and T-bills). As shares rise, hold more shares; as they fall, move to bills. Protects a floor as wealth changes |

**4. Why superannuation matters.** Super is about **85%** of
Australia's managed funds (slide 5). It grew because contributions are
**compulsory** (the Superannuation Guarantee), money is **locked away**
until retirement, contributions and earnings get **tax concessions**,
and the population is ageing.

## How the Exam Asks It

**Sample Q3** (4 marks): why has super grown (2) and what asset
allocation would a super fund use and why (2). Shape for (b):

> A diversified, multi-asset portfolio (Australian and global shares,
> bonds, cash, property, infrastructure, some alternatives) run on
> **strategic asset allocation**: a long-run policy mix rebalanced
> periodically. Why: members' money is locked away for decades, so the
> fund can ride out volatility and hold illiquid assets; steady
> contributions give liquidity; growth assets are needed to beat
> inflation; and diversification reduces risk.

**Sample Q10(a)** (1 mark): two ways to beat a benchmark. **Asset
allocation** (over-weight the segments you expect to do well) and
**security selection** (pick better shares within each segment).

## Say It Back

<details><summary>1. What's the passive manager's main goal?</summary>

To minimise tracking error: match the index as closely and cheaply as
possible.
</details>

<details><summary>2. One difference between an index fund and an ETF?</summary>

An ETF trades on a stock exchange during the day like a share (and can
be short sold). An index fund is bought and redeemed through the
manager.
</details>

<details><summary>3. Strategic vs tactical asset allocation, in one line each?</summary>

**Strategic:** a long-term fixed policy mix, rebalanced back to target.
**Tactical:** frequent short-term shifts to exploit perceived
mispricing between asset classes.
</details>

<details><summary>4. Why do active funds usually have a higher tax cost ratio?</summary>

They trade more (higher turnover), which crystallises capital gains
and creates tax for investors sooner.
</details>

<details><summary>5. Value investor vs growth investor: what does each focus on?</summary>

**Value:** the price. Buys shares that look cheap (low P/E) and waits
for the market to correct. **Growth:** the earnings. Buys companies
expected to grow EPS fast and accepts a higher price.
</details>
