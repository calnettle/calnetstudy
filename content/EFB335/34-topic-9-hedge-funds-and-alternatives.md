## 9.1 What makes an investment "alternative" (slides 4–7)

Alternative investments are opportunities that **fall outside the traditional asset classes**. They tend to:

- require **large initial capital**
- have **barriers to entry**
- be **illiquid**
- be **restricted to professional investors**.

The **principles of investment still apply**: diversification, and the risk–return trade-off. The deck frames the whole topic with two questions:

1. Do alternative investments offer **superior risk–return trade-offs**?
2. Do they **enhance portfolio diversification**?

**The asset classes (slide 6):** hedge funds, private equity, real estate, and natural resources and commodities.

**Management structure:** usually a **limited partnership** rather than a mutual fund, managing the investors' commingled assets.

**The fund's "alpha" (α):** the **excess return generated by the fund**, read as evidence of superior performance by the manager.

> **Alpha means excess return *after allowing for risk*, not raw return.** A fund that earns 15% while carrying twice the market's beta has not produced 15% of alpha. This is Jensen's alpha from Topic 10 (`α = (R_p − R_f) − β(R_m − R_f)`, which is on the official sheet). Every "do alternatives really outperform?" result in this topic turns on exactly this adjustment.

## 9.2 Private equity (slides 8–16)

### The basic idea

Business ideas need **seed capital**, and private equity generally funds them. It involves a **small group of investors**, is **not available to the public**, and is **common in the early stages** of a business (slide 8).

**Definition (slide 9):** any **ownership interest in an asset that is not tradable in a public market**. Typical uses:

- funding **new companies**
- funding **established firms** that want to **change their organisational structure** or are in **financial distress**.

Private equity is **far less liquid** than listed shares, so it is treated as a **long-term position** in a portfolio. Its characteristics: **higher return and low liquidity**, and a **good source of diversification**.

It **includes family businesses**. The typical industries are **services, retail and wholesale, and light manufacturing** (slide 11).

### Why PE returns might be higher, and why they are hard to measure (slide 13)

PE returns **might be higher** to compensate for:

- **illiquidity**
- **poor diversification**
- **low survival rates**.

PE returns are **difficult to estimate** because:

- by definition there is **no public trading**, so there is no market price
- there is a **lack of disclosure**.

### Why PE returns may look no better than public equity (slide 14)

Entrepreneurs accept returns that a pure risk–return investor would not, because they:

- **overestimate** their ability to succeed
- are **more risk tolerant**
- enjoy **non-pecuniary benefits** (being your own boss)
- have a **preference for skewness** (a small chance of a huge payoff).

> **Slides 13 and 14 pull in opposite directions, and an exam answer should use both.** Slide 13 gives reasons the *required* return should be high (illiquidity, poor diversification, low survival). Slide 14 explains why the *realised* return can still end up no better than listed shares: the people supplying the capital are not demanding full compensation.

### Growth, dilution and the move to funds (slides 15–16)

- As a business grows, its **capital requirements increase**, and **not all good ideas succeed**.
- Raising more capital **dilutes the entrepreneur's ownership**, which creates **conflict**.
- In Australia the average **IPO size** over the last 10 years is quoted as **$60m–$70m** (no source or date on the slide).
- **Private equity funds** bundle investments and offer them to a **broader range of investors**. Capital is typically **locked up for several years**, and investors can **increase exposure through additional capital contributions**.

> **Check the "$95.4 billion" on slide 16.** The slide says private equity funds in Australia managed an estimated **$95.4bn in 2023**. ## 9.3 Returns to private equity: the J-curve (slides 17–21)

PE commitments should be viewed as **long-term, highly illiquid** investments. Four facts from slides 17 and 20:

1. Returns follow the **"J-curve effect"**.
2. **Average annual returns are quite high over time**.
3. The **initial years usually produce negative returns**.
4. Overall risk is higher, and there is **huge dispersion** in fund returns between good and bad managers.

### Exhibit 17.22 — the J-curve (slide 21)

The chart plots since-inception **IRR (%)** against years. Approximate readings from the chart:

| Years | Actual returns (IRR) |
|---|---|
| 0–2 | Falls from 0 to a trough of about **−8%** around years 2–3 |
| about 4 | Crosses **zero** |
| about 6.5 | Crosses the smoothed line, at about 7% |
| 10 | About 14–15% |
| 12 | Rises steeply to about 20% |

A dotted **"smoothed returns"** line rises steadily from 0 to about 14%.

Why the J: in the early years the fund pays **fees and set-up costs** and has **no exits yet**, so returns are negative. Value is realised later, as portfolio companies are improved and sold. (The deck shows the shape without stating the causes; that explanation is the textbook's standard reading.)

**Worked example (practice numbers, not from the deck).** You commit $100. The fund's net asset value after fees at each year-end is $92, $88, $95, $115, $150, with no distributions. The since-inception annualised return (Topic 1's geometric annualising) is:

```
Annualised return to year t = (NAV_t / 100)^(1/t) − 1

Year 1:  (92/100)^(1/1)  − 1 = −8.00%
Year 2:  (88/100)^(1/2)  − 1 = −6.19%
Year 3:  (95/100)^(1/3)  − 1 = −1.70%
Year 4:  (115/100)^(1/4) − 1 = +3.56%
Year 5:  (150/100)^(1/5) − 1 = +8.45%
```

The return dips, recovers past zero in year 4, then climbs: a J.

> **Don't judge a PE fund on its early returns.** Negative years 1–3 are the normal pattern, not evidence of a bad manager. The flip side: a fund's *reported* IRR in its first few years says almost nothing about where it will finish. That is one more reason PE performance is hard to measure (slide 13).

### Exhibit 17.12 — the SML for traditional and alternative assets (slide 18)

This plots **excess return (%)** against **beta**, with beta measured against the **Global Investment Market (GIM)**, a proxy for all investable assets. GIM therefore sits at β = 1. Approximate readings:

- **Private equity** sits far up and to the right, at a beta near **3.8** and an excess return near **8%**, **above** the line.
- **Hedge funds** have a low beta (about **0.5**) and an excess return of about **2%**, also **above** the line.
- Real estate and natural resources sit near hedge funds, while US and non-US bonds sit near the origin.
- US and non-US equities have betas of about **2**, because GIM includes bonds and the other low-risk assets.

The point: on this (adapted, 2002) evidence, alternatives plot on or above the SML, so they look attractive *after* adjusting for systematic risk. This is the case *for* alternatives. Slides 25 and 48 then challenge it.

> **Beta depends on the benchmark, which is Topic 3's Roll critique again.** US equity has a beta of about 2 here only because the benchmark is the whole global market, bonds included. Against the S&P 500, the same asset's beta would be 1. Always state the benchmark when quoting a beta.

## 9.4 Venture capital (slides 23–26)

**Venture capital (VC) is a sub-set of private equity.** VC funds:

- **specialise** in private equity investments
- give investors **better access** to private equity
- **work with the business** to get it to an exit, typically an **IPO**.

### Funding stages (slide 24)

| Stage | Name |
|---|---|
| Stage one | **Start-up** (pre-seed and seed) |
| Stage two | **Development** (Series A) |
| Stage three | **Expansion** (Series B and C) |
| Possible | **Mezzanine financing** (Series D) |
| Exit | **Bridge and pre-IPO** funding |

VC is **costly** compared with traditional investments: VC firms take **large fees** and **large ownership stakes**.

### Returns to VC funds (slide 25)

| Evidence | Finding |
|---|---|
| Early research | High returns: **Gompers & Lerner (1997) 30.5% p.a.**, **Chen et al. (2002) 45% p.a.** |
| Distribution | VC returns are **non-normal**, so traditional averages are **not appropriate** |
| Risk | High: **Gompers & Lerner β = 1.4**, **Cochrane β = 1.7** |
| Risk-adjusted (against NASDAQ) | **Mean return −7.1%** |
| Verdict | Evidence **does not support superior risk-adjusted returns** |

> **Raw 30–45% p.a. becomes −7.1% risk-adjusted. This is the topic's central lesson in one slide.** High betas (1.4–1.7) mean VC *should* earn much more than the market. Once that required return is deducted, the average VC return is below what its risk demands. The non-normality point matters too: a few huge winners drag the mean far above what a typical fund earns.

## 9.5 Hedge funds: what they are (slides 28–33)

"Hedge" strictly means taking a position to **offset downturns**, and the general view is that hedge funds **avoid losses**. The deck puts the global industry at **about 10,000 funds with $5 trillion** (an average of $500m per fund, no date given). Popularity **rose through the 1990s** (slide 28).

**Development (slide 29):**

- Hedge funds are one of the most significant developments in professional asset management over the past 20 years.
- They are not new: the first can be traced back to **1949**, structured as a **partnership with an incentive fee** for superior performance.
- Because they can go **long and short** and use **financial leverage**, they are able to produce superior returns compared with traditional structures such as mutual funds.

> **Slide 29 and slide 48 contradict each other.** Slide 29 says hedge funds are "able to produce superior returns" compared with mutual funds. Slide 48 says research shows they "do not earn superior risk-adjusted returns". The reconciliation is that hedge funds have the *tools* (shorting, leverage, derivatives, few restrictions) that *could* produce superior returns, but the evidence says the average fund doesn't deliver them after adjusting for risk. Use slide 48's conclusion in an answer.

### Characteristics (slides 30–32)

| Feature | Hedge fund | Mutual fund (the contrast in slide 31) |
|---|---|---|
| Minimum investment | **Large** | Small |
| Public status / regulation | **Not regarded as public funds; light regulation** | Public, regulated |
| Size | **Smaller** than super and mutual funds | Larger |
| Where and how it can invest | **Less restricted**; unorthodox strategies and assets | Restricted mandate |
| Correlation with traditional assets | **Lower**, giving diversification benefits | High |
| Liquidity | **Far less liquid**, even than closed-end fund shares | Liquid |
| Contributions and withdrawals | **Severe limits** on when and how often | Daily |
| Fees | **High**, including a performance fee subject to a **high-water mark** | Lower |

**Investment characteristics (slide 32):** **levered positions**; a focus on **absolute rather than relative returns**; **low correlations** with traditional portfolios; **use of derivatives**.

**Claimed benefits (slide 32):** **higher returns**, **low risk**, **diversifying power**.

> **Absolute vs relative return is a definition marks hang on.** A mutual fund is judged *relative* to a benchmark: losing 8% when the index loses 10% is a success. A hedge fund targets an *absolute* return: positive whatever the market does. That is why hedge funds short-sell and use derivatives, and why "benchmark" is awkward for them (see §9.8).

## 9.6 Hedge fund strategies (slides 34–42)

The deck groups strategies into four families (slides 34–37):

| Family | Strategy | How it makes money |
|---|---|---|
| **Equity-based** | **Long–short equity** | Identify misvalued stocks: **long the undervalued, short the overvalued** |
| | **Equity market neutral** | **Offsetting long and short risk positions** (possibly with derivatives) to limit the fund's overall volatility exposure |
| **Arbitrage-based** | **Fixed-income arbitrage** | Exploit **bond pricing disparities** caused by market events, investor preferences or fixed-income fluctuations |
| | **Convertible arbitrage** | Exploit disparities between **convertible bond prices and the underlying shares** |
| | **Merger (risk) arbitrage** | Returns depend on the **size of the spread** on merger deals, which is directly related to the **likelihood the deal completes** |
| **Opportunistic** | **High yield and distressed** | Invest in **risky bonds** |
| | **Global macro** | Profit from **changes in global economies** |
| | **Managed futures** | **Long and short positions across futures contracts** |
| | **Special situations** | Events such as **bankruptcies and spin-offs** |
| **Multiple strategies** | **Fund of funds** | Invest in **a number of hedge funds** for a well-diversified allocation |

> **"Market neutral" means low market exposure, not low risk.** The longs and shorts offset the *market* risk, but the fund still carries security-selection risk and usually leverage. In Exhibit 17.18 (§9.8), the equity market neutral index was the **worst-performing strategy of 2008, at −40.3%**.

### Merger (risk) arbitrage in detail (slides 39–40)

- After a takeover is announced, the manager takes equity positions in the **target**.
- The manager compares their **own judgement** of the deal's success with the **success probability implied by the target's share price**.
- If the manager thinks the takeover is **more likely** to happen than the market does, they **buy** the target's shares.
- If they think it is **less likely**, they **short-sell** the target's shares.

> **Slide 39 is titled "Equity-based Strategies", but its content is merger arbitrage, which slide 36 classifies as *arbitrage-based*.** Use slide 36's classification. Slide 39 also cites "Exhibits 17.15, 17.16", but only 17.15 appears in the deck.

**Exhibit 17.15 — implied probabilities for proposed takeovers (slide 40)** (Brown & Raymond, 1986). This tracks the market-implied probability of completion over the 12 weeks before resolution:

- **Deals that completed** (two lines, "Completed–C" and "Completed–NC") start at about **0.93–0.99** and drift down to about **0.72–0.76**.
- **Deals that failed** start at about **0.22**, jump to about **0.41** after a week, and then stay around **0.40–0.47**.

The market price does separate the deals that will succeed from those that will fail, but not perfectly. That gap is the merger arbitrageur's opportunity. The slide does not define the "C" and "NC" labels; check the textbook if you need them.

**Worked example (practice numbers; the formula is the textbook's standard approach and is not on the slides).** A target traded at $20 before the bid. The offer is $30, and the target now trades at $27. Assume the price reverts to $20 if the deal fails.

```
Market-implied probability = (current − fail price) / (offer − fail price)
                           = (27 − 20) / (30 − 20) = 0.70

Manager believes 0.85:  expected price = 0.85(30) + 0.15(20) = $28.50
                        > $27  →  BUY;  expected gain $1.50 = 5.56% on $27

Manager believes 0.50:  expected price = 0.50(30) + 0.50(20) = $25.00
                        < $27  →  SHORT; expected gain $2.00 = 7.41% on $27
```

### Long-Term Capital Management (slide 41)

The slide's five lessons:

1. **Too much money can be a burden.**
2. **Specific arbitrage opportunities are finite.**
3. **Overconfidence and leverage are not a good mix.**
4. **Make sure you have exit options.**
5. **Markets can remain irrational longer than you can remain solvent.**

## 9.7 Fees: management fee, performance fee, high-water mark (slides 31, 45–46)

Fee structure (slide 45):

- a **management fee** of **around 1.5%** of assets
- a **performance fee**, subject to a **high-water mark**
- overall, **fees are higher than for traditional funds**.

Exhibit 17.14 puts the mean management fee at 1.4% (median 1.0%) and the mean performance fee at 17.2% (median 20%).

**High-water mark (slide 31):** when calculating the performance fee, investors usually require that **any past losses be recouped before the manager receives the additional payout**.

```
High-water mark (HWM) = highest unit price at which a performance fee was last charged
                        (or the starting price)

Performance fee this period = rate × max(0, price − HWM)
If price > HWM:  charge the fee, then reset HWM = price
If price ≤ HWM:  no fee; HWM unchanged
```

### The deck's example (slide 46)

| | Month 1 | Month 2 | Month 3 | Month 4 | Month 5 | Month 6 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Unit price | $7.80 | $5.60 | $8.20 | $7.70 | $8.00 | $8.70 |
| New high? | (start) | No | **Yes** | No | No | **Yes** |
| HWM | $7.80 | $7.80 | $8.20 | $8.20 | $8.20 | $8.70 |
| Fee | | | **$0.10** | | | **$0.13** |

What the deck computes:

```
Month 3:  25% × (8.20 − 7.80) = 25% × 0.40 = $0.10
Month 6:  25% × (8.70 − 8.20) = 25% × 0.50 = $0.125 → shown as $0.13
Total:    $0.225
```

> ⚠️ **Deck error: the row is labelled "Fee applied (at 20%)", but the formulas use 25%.** At the stated 20%:
>
> ```
> Month 3:  20% × 0.40 = $0.08
> Month 6:  20% × 0.50 = $0.10
> Total:    $0.18
> ```
>
> The *mechanics* (fee only above the HWM, HWM reset on new highs, no fee in months 2, 4 and 5) are right whichever rate you use. Use whatever rate an exam question states, and show the formula so the method marks are safe. Confirm with your tutor which figure the deck intended.

Two further points about this example:

- **Month 5 is a gain but earns no fee.** The price rose $7.70 → $8.00, but it is still below the $8.20 high-water mark. Month 2's fall from $7.80 to $5.60 had to be fully recouped before month 3 earned anything, and the fee is charged only on the $0.40 above the old high.
- **What the mark saves the investor.** Without it, a 20% fee would be charged on every month's rise. Note that the deck's fees ignore the effect of the fee on the unit price itself.

```
Rises:        m2→m3 2.60,  m4→m5 0.30,  m5→m6 0.70   = $3.60
No HWM fee:   20% × 3.60 = $0.72
With HWM:     20% × (8.70 − 7.80) = 20% × 0.90 = $0.18

The investor's net gain over six months is $0.90.
  With the HWM, fees = 20% of that net gain.
  Without it, fees = 0.72 / 0.90 = 80% of the net gain, four times as much.
```

> **The high-water mark stops the manager being paid twice for the same ground.** With a HWM, total performance fees over any period equal the fee rate times the gain above the *previous peak*. A common error is computing the fee on the month-on-month rise (month 3: 20% × 2.60 = $0.52), which charges the manager's fee on the recovery of earlier losses.

### Putting the fees together: management fee, hurdle and performance fee (practice numbers)

The deck gives the 1.5% management fee and the high-water mark. A **hurdle rate** appears only in Exhibit 17.14 (14% of funds have one). A hurdle is a minimum return the fund must earn before any performance fee is paid. Conventions vary, so **state your assumptions** in any answer. Here they are: the management fee is charged on opening NAV; the performance fee is charged on the gain after the management fee; and a **hard** hurdle means the fee applies only to the excess over the hurdle.

**Worked example.** NAV $100m, gross return 15%, fees "1.5 and 20", hurdle 5%, and the fund is above its high-water mark.

```
Management fee        = 1.5% × 100           = $1.5m
Gain after mgmt fee   = 15.0 − 1.5           = $13.5m
Hurdle                = 5% × 100             = $5.0m

HARD hurdle:  performance fee = 20% × (13.5 − 5.0) = $1.7m
              investor's net  = 13.5 − 1.7          = $11.8m  →  11.8%
              total fees      = 1.5 + 1.7 = $3.2m = 21.3% of the $15m gross gain

SOFT hurdle (or no hurdle): once the hurdle is cleared, fee on the whole gain
              performance fee = 20% × 13.5 = $2.7m
              investor's net  = 13.5 − 2.7 = $10.8m  →  10.8%
              total fees      = 1.5 + 2.7 = $4.2m = 28.0% of gross gain

Low year, gross 5%:  after mgmt fee 5.0 − 1.5 = $3.5m, below the $5m hurdle
              → no performance fee; investor nets 3.5%
```

### Fund-of-funds fee layering (practice numbers)

An underlying fund earns 12% gross and charges 1.5% + 20%. The fund of funds charges another 1% + 10% on top.

```
Underlying fund:  12.0 − 1.5 = 10.5;   10.5 − 20%(10.5) = 8.40%
Fund of funds:     8.40 − 1.0 = 7.40;   7.40 − 10%(7.40) = 6.66%

Fee drag: 12.0 − 6.66 = 5.34 points = 44.5% of the 12% gross return
```

> **"Additional fees" (slide 42) is not a footnote.** Two layers of fees can absorb close to half the gross return. The diversification and access a fund of funds provides have to be worth that.

## 9.8 Hedge fund performance (slides 43–49)

### Returns are highly variable (slides 43–44)

- Not all hedge funds are the same in **risk and return profile**.
- Strategy returns are **highly variable year to year**, both absolutely and relative to each other.
- Over **1990–2010**, hedge funds overall produced **positive alpha**.
- **Emerging markets** was the **worst** strategy in **1995 (−16.9%)** but the **best** in **1996 (+34.5%)**, a swing of 51.4 percentage points.
- **Long/short equity** performed well **2003–2007** but **lost money in 2002 and 2008**.

The table does confirm the long/short pattern for 2003–2008. The lesson is what counts: **no strategy is consistently best**, so last year's winner tells you little about next year.

### The measurement problems (slide 47)

The drawbacks of hedge funds include **performance measurement problems**:

- **lack of required disclosure**
- **lags in reporting**
- establishing **net-of-fees** return measures
- choosing an appropriate return measure when **derivatives make returns non-normal**
- **method adjustments are required, using lags of benchmarks**.

> **Why non-normality matters:** the Sharpe ratio, standard deviation and Jensen's alpha (Topic 10) all assume risk is fully described by mean and variance. Option-like payoffs (short volatility, merger arbitrage's small gains with occasional large losses) hide their risk in the tail, so standard deviation understates it. Lagged benchmarks are used because stale, smoothed prices make returns look less correlated with the market than they really are.

### The research verdict (slides 48–49)

- Research **casts doubt** on hedge funds' ability to earn superior returns.
- The **average hedge fund is less risky than the market, but not low risk**, and risk varies across strategies.
- Hedge funds **do not appear to earn superior risk-adjusted returns**.
- **But** hedge funds **do have lower correlations**. They **enhance a traditional portfolio in certain circumstances**, and **portfolio efficiency is improved**.
- A **10–20% allocation** to hedge funds is suggested.

**Claim against evidence:**

| Claimed benefit (slide 32) | Verdict (slides 47–49) |

| Low risk | **Less risky than the market but not low risk**, and risk varies by strategy |
| Diversifying power | **Yes**: lower correlations improve portfolio efficiency, so 10–20% is suggested |

## Summary

- **Alternatives** sit outside the traditional asset classes. They need large capital, have barriers to entry, are illiquid, and are restricted to professionals. They are usually structured as **limited partnerships**. The test for them is the usual one: risk-adjusted return and diversification.
- **Private equity** means ownership not tradable in a public market. It is illiquid and long-term. Its returns *should* be high (illiquidity, poor diversification, low survival) but are hard to measure, and entrepreneurs accept less (overconfidence, risk tolerance, non-pecuniary benefits, skewness preference). Australian private capital AUM grew from $29.8bn to $139.0bn between 2010 and 2023, and family offices are now the largest investor type.
- **The J-curve:** negative returns in the early years, then high returns later, with huge dispersion between managers. On Exhibit 17.12, PE and hedge funds plot above the SML.
- **Venture capital** is PE for start-ups, funded in stages from seed to pre-IPO, with an IPO exit. Raw returns of 30–45% p.a. carry betas of 1.4–1.7 and are **−7.1% risk-adjusted**.
- **Hedge funds** take levered long/short positions, use derivatives, target absolute returns, are lightly regulated and illiquid, and charge high fees. The strategy families are equity-based, arbitrage-based, opportunistic and multi-strategy (fund of funds). LTCM's lessons are capacity, leverage and exit options.
- **Fees:** a management fee of about 1.5% plus a performance fee charged only above the **high-water mark**. The deck's example labels the fee 20% but computes it at 25%.
- **Performance:** highly variable by strategy and year. There is no evidence of superior risk-adjusted returns, and measurement is hampered by disclosure, lags, fees and non-normal returns. **But** lower correlations mean a **10–20% allocation** can improve portfolio efficiency.

<details><summary>3. Why are a private equity fund's returns usually negative in its first few years?</summary>

The **J-curve effect**: the fund pays fees and set-up costs and has made no exits yet, so early returns are negative. Returns turn positive (about year 4 in Exhibit 17.22) and climb as investments are realised.

</details>

<details><summary>4. A fund's unit price goes $10.00 → $8.00 → $9.50 → $11.00 with a 20% performance fee and a high-water mark at $10.00. What fees are paid?</summary>

None at $8.00 or $9.50, because both are below the $10.00 mark. At $11.00: **20% × (11.00 − 10.00) = $0.20**, and the HWM resets to $11.00. Without a HWM the fee would be 20% × (1.50 + 1.50) = $0.60.

</details>

<details><summary>5. Distinguish long–short equity from equity market neutral.</summary>

**Long–short** buys undervalued and shorts overvalued stocks, and may keep a net market exposure. **Market neutral** sizes the longs and shorts so that their risk exposures offset, limiting the fund's overall volatility (market) exposure, so returns come from security selection.

</details>

<details><summary>6. A target's price implies a 60% chance the takeover completes; the manager thinks it is 40%. What trade?</summary>

**Short-sell the target's shares.** The manager believes the deal is less likely than the market does, so the price should fall towards the no-deal value (slide 39).

</details>

Any three of the following (slide 47): lack of required disclosure; lags in reporting; establishing net-of-fees returns; non-normal returns caused by derivatives, which make standard measures inappropriate; and the need to adjust methods using lagged benchmarks.
