## The One-Sentence Version

**Alternatives (hedge funds, private equity, venture capital) promise
better returns and diversification, but once you allow for fees, risk
and flattering data, the average one doesn't deliver.**

## The Picture

Hedge funds are the exclusive restaurant with no prices on the menu.
Only big spenders get in, the chef cooks whatever he likes, the bill is
large (a base fee plus a cut of the profits), and the reviews you read
are only from the restaurants that are still open, because the ones
that failed have closed and their reviews vanished. Some meals are
genuinely great. The *average* meal is not as good as the reviews say.

## The Words You Need

| Term | Plain meaning |
|---|---|
| **Alternative investments** | Anything outside shares, bonds and cash: hedge funds, private equity, real estate, commodities. Usually large minimums, illiquid, for professional investors |
| **Private equity (PE)** | Owning businesses that aren't listed on an exchange. Locked up for years |
| **Venture capital (VC)** | The part of PE that funds start-ups, stage by stage (seed → Series A → B/C → pre-IPO), aiming to exit through an IPO |
| **J-curve** | PE returns are **negative in the early years** (fees and set-up costs, no exits yet), then climb as investments are sold. Draw it as a J |
| **Hedge fund** | A lightly regulated private fund that can go long and short, use leverage and derivatives, and chases **absolute** returns (positive whatever the market does) |
| **Management fee + performance fee** | A yearly % of assets (about 1.5% in the slides) plus a share of profits |
| **High-water mark** | The performance fee is only paid on *new* highs. Past losses must be made back first |
| **Fund of funds** | A fund that invests in several hedge funds. More diversification and access, but **two layers of fees** |

## The J-Curve, Drawn in Words

Years 1–3: money goes in, fees are charged, nothing is sold yet, so
returns are **negative**. Years 4–10: companies are improved and sold
(exits), returns rise steeply. The line dips, then shoots up: a J.

## The Hedge Fund Strategies (slides 34–42)

| Family | Strategy | Plain meaning |
|---|---|---|
| **Equity-based** | Long–short equity | Buy the undervalued, short the overvalued |
| | Equity market neutral | Balance longs and shorts so the market's direction doesn't matter |
| **Arbitrage** | Fixed-income arbitrage | Exploit small mispricings between related bonds |
| | Convertible arbitrage | Exploit mispricing between a convertible bond and the company's shares |
| | Merger (risk) arbitrage | After a takeover is announced, buy the target if you think the deal is more likely to happen than the price implies |
| **Opportunistic** | Global macro | Bet on whole economies: rates, currencies, commodities |
| | Distressed / high yield | Buy the debt of struggling companies |
| | Managed futures | Long and short positions across futures markets |
| **Multiple** | Fund of funds | A spread of other hedge funds |

**LTCM's lessons (slide 41):** too much money is a burden, arbitrage
opportunities are limited, overconfidence plus leverage is dangerous,
have an exit, and "markets can stay irrational longer than you can stay
solvent".

## Worked Example: High-Water Mark

A fund starts at $100. Performance fee 20%.

| Year | Value | High-water mark | Performance fee charged on |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | $120 | $100 → $120 | the $20 gain → **$4** |
| 2 | $105 | stays $120 | nothing (below the mark) |
| 3 | $125 | $120 → $125 | only the **$5** above $120 → **$1** |

Without the mark, the manager would also charge on the $105 → $120
recovery, getting paid twice for the same ground.

## Worked Example: Adding a Hedge Fund to a Portfolio

$600,000 in shares (β = 1.0, expected 10%) plus $400,000 in a hedge fund
(β = 0.4, expected 6%).

```
Weights: 0.6 and 0.4
Portfolio beta   = 0.6(1.0) + 0.4(0.4) = 0.60 + 0.16 = 0.76
Expected return  = 0.6(10) + 0.4(6)    = 6.0 + 2.4   = 8.4%
```

Did it help? Compare **return per unit of beta**: shares 10 ÷ 1.0 = 10,
hedge fund 6 ÷ 0.4 = 15, portfolio 8.4 ÷ 0.76 = 11.1. Here the fund
gives more return per unit of market risk, so it improves the trade-off.
In [sample Q8](#/EFB335/18-sample-final-exam/question-8-adding-a-hedge-fund-to-a-portfolio-topic-9-4-marks)
the numbers go the other way (5 ÷ 0.8 = 6.25 is worse than the shares'
12 ÷ 1.1 = 10.9), so the answer is **no**. Always do the comparison;
don't assume.

## Do the Claimed Benefits Exist?

The three claims: **(1) high absolute returns, (2) low correlation
(diversification), (3) superior risk-adjusted returns.** The evidence
says: *partly, and less than advertised.*

- **Biased data.** Survivorship bias (dead funds drop out of databases) and backfill bias (funds report only after a good run) inflate average returns.
- **Fees** eat much of the gross outperformance.
- **Correlations rise in a crisis**, just when you need the diversification.
- **Illiquid, smoothed prices** understate volatility and flatter Sharpe ratios. Derivatives make returns non-normal, so standard risk measures mislead.
- **Lags and little disclosure** make performance hard to measure.
- **But** correlations *are* lower on average, and a **10–20% allocation** can improve a traditional portfolio's efficiency (slide 49).

Also: VC looks great on raw averages (30–45% a year in early studies),
but it's very risky (betas around 1.4–1.7) and **risk-adjusted, the
average return is negative** (slide 25).

## The Jargon to Learn

The plain English above is there to help you *understand* it. **The
marks go to the proper terms**, so learn every one of these: the term,
what it means, and a sentence that uses it the way a marker wants to
read it. Cover the right two columns and test yourself.

| Term | What it means | Say it like this in the exam |
|---|---|---|
| **Alternative investments** | Assets outside traditional shares, bonds and cash | "Alternatives tend to be illiquid, have high minimum investments and barriers to entry." |
| **Limited partnership (GP / LP)** | The usual structure: a manager (general partner) and investors (limited partners) | "Hedge funds and private equity funds are usually structured as limited partnerships." |
| **Private equity** | Ownership interests not traded on a public market | "Private equity commitments are long-term, highly illiquid positions." |
| **Venture capital and its funding stages** | PE for start-ups: seed, Series A, B/C, mezzanine, pre-IPO | "VC funds exit mainly through an IPO and take large ownership stakes and fees." |
| **J-curve effect** | Negative early returns, then strong later returns | "The J-curve reflects fees and costs incurred before portfolio companies are exited." |
| **Illiquidity premium** | Extra return for money you can't easily sell | "Part of private equity's return compensates investors for illiquidity and poor diversification." |
| **Absolute return** | Aiming for positive returns whatever the market does | "Hedge funds target absolute rather than relative returns." |
| **Leverage / short selling** | Borrowing to magnify positions / selling borrowed securities | "Hedge funds use leverage, short selling and derivatives, unlike most mutual funds." |
| **Long–short equity / equity market neutral** | Long underpriced, short overpriced / market exposure netted to zero | "A market-neutral fund aims for zero beta, so returns come from security selection." |
| **Convertible / fixed-income / merger (risk) arbitrage** | Exploiting relative mispricings | "Merger arbitrage profits if the manager judges the deal's completion probability better than the market." |
| **Global macro / managed futures / distressed** | Opportunistic strategies | "Global macro funds take directional bets on interest rates, currencies and commodities." |
| **Fund of funds** | A fund investing in several hedge funds | "Funds of funds offer diversification and access but add a second layer of fees." |
| **Management fee / performance (incentive) fee** | Annual % of assets / share of profits | "Hedge fund fees combine a management fee of around 1.5% with a performance fee." |
| **High-water mark** | Performance fee only on new highs | "The high-water mark requires past losses to be recouped before a performance fee is paid." |
| **Survivorship bias / backfill bias** | Dead funds drop out / funds report only after good runs | "Survivorship and backfill bias overstate average hedge fund returns." |
| **Non-normal returns** | Returns with skew and fat tails, often from derivatives | "Because hedge fund returns are non-normal, standard deviation understates their true risk." |

<details><summary>1. What is the J-curve and why does it happen?</summary>

When they think the takeover is **more likely** to go through than the
