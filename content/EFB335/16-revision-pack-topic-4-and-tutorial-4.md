## Part A — The concepts, taught from scratch

### A1. Where Topic 4 starts: three cracks in the CAPM

Topic 3 ended with the CAPM under strain, for three reasons you should be able to recite:

1. **Unrealistic assumptions** — eight of them, including quadratic utility and homogeneous expectations.
2. **The benchmark problem** — the true market portfolio is unobservable, and beta changes with the proxy chosen (Roll's critique; the JP Morgan example in doc 04, §3.7).
3. **Empirical misses** — characteristics beyond beta (size, book-to-market, P/E, leverage) predict returns in the cross-section, which a one-factor model says they shouldn't.

Stephen Ross's **Arbitrage Pricing Theory (1976)** answers all three at once: more factors, fewer assumptions, and **no market portfolio anywhere in the theory**.

### A2. APT's three assumptions (and the three it drops)

APT assumes only:

1. Capital markets are **perfectly competitive**;
2. Investors **prefer more wealth to less**, with certainty;
3. Returns are generated by a **linear function of K common factors**, with unsystematic risk diversifiable away.

It does **not** assume normally distributed returns, quadratic utility, or a mean–variance-efficient market portfolio — the three heaviest planks of the CAPM. "Name an assumption CAPM makes that APT doesn't" is a recurring one-marker; any of those three earns it.

### A3. The APT equation, term by term

```
E(Ri) = λ0 + λ1·bi1 + λ2·bi2 + … + λk·bik
```

| Symbol | Is | Think of it as |
|---|---|---|
| λ0 | Expected return on an asset with **zero systematic risk** | The RFR's seat in the equation |
| λj | **Risk premium** on common factor j | "The market pays this much per unit of factor-j exposure" |
| bij | Sensitivity of asset i to factor j | Asset i's beta *on that factor* |

Each term is (price of risk) × (quantity of risk), summed over K risks instead of CAPM's one. What the factors *are* — inflation, GNP growth, political upheaval, interest-rate changes — the theory deliberately does not say. That silence is A9's whole story.

### A4. CAPM vs APT — the comparison table

| | CAPM | APT |
|---|---|---|
| Equation | `E(Ri) = RFR + βi[E(RM) − RFR]` | `E(Ri) = λ0 + Σ λj·bij` |
| Form | Linear | Linear |
| # risk factors | 1 | K (> 1) |
| Factor premium | `E(RM) − RFR` | `{λj}` |
| Sensitivity | βi | `{bij}` |
| Zero-beta return | RFR | λ0 |
| Equilibrium enforced by | Everyone holding M | **Arbitrage** (law of one price) |
| Vulnerable to Roll's critique? | Yes | No — no benchmark needed |

The similarity to quote: both are **linear models pricing only systematic risk**. The differences to quote: **number of factors** and **how equilibrium is enforced**.

### A5. Using the APT — the two-factor example

Factors: unanticipated inflation (λ1 = 0.02) and unexpected real GDP growth (λ2 = 0.03); zero-beta return λ0 = 0.04.

```
Asset X:  b1 = 0.50, b2 = 1.50
Asset Y:  b1 = 2.00, b2 = 1.75

E(Rx) = 0.04 + 0.02(0.50) + 0.03(1.50) = 9.5%
E(Ry) = 0.04 + 0.02(2.00) + 0.03(1.75) = 13.25%
```

Y is more sensitive to both risks, so it must promise more. Mechanically this is CAPM arithmetic done twice — premium × sensitivity, then add λ0.

### A6. The three-stock arbitrage, slowly

The exam-grade worked example. Three stocks all priced at **$35** today, no dividends, two factors, λ0 = 0, λ1 = 4%, λ2 = 5%.

**Step 1 — what the model says each stock should earn and become:**

```
A: E(R) = 0.8(4) + 0.9(5)  = 7.7%  →  35 × 1.077 = $37.70
B: E(R) = −0.2(4) + 1.3(5) = 5.7%  →  35 × 1.057 = $37.00
C: E(R) = 1.8(4) + 0.5(5)  = 9.7%  →  35 × 1.097 = $38.40
```

**Step 2 — your own price forecasts differ** ($37.20 / $37.80 / $38.50). Discount *your* forecast at the *model's* required return to get today's fair value:

```
A: 37.20 / 1.077 = $34.54   <  $35  →  overpriced   →  SHORT
B: 37.80 / 1.057 = $35.76   >  $35  →  underpriced  →  BUY
C: 38.50 / 1.097 = $35.10   >  $35  →  underpriced  →  BUY
```

**Step 3 — the arbitrage portfolio** (short 1 A; long 0.5 B and 0.5 C) satisfies the three defining conditions:

```
No net wealth:      +35.00 − 17.50 − 17.50            = 0
No factor 1 risk:   (−1)(0.8) + 0.5(−0.2) + 0.5(1.8)  = 0
No factor 2 risk:   (−1)(0.9) + 0.5(1.3) + 0.5(0.5)   = 0
Positive payoff:    (−1)(2.20) + 0.5(2.80) + 0.5(3.50) = +$0.95
```

Free money with no capital and no systematic risk — so everyone does it, A is bid **down** toward $34.54, B and C bid **up** toward $35.76 / $35.10, and the opportunity closes. That closing mechanism — the **law of one price** — is what replaces "everyone holds M" as APT's equilibrium engine.

Three traps, all verified the hard way:

- Compare each **discounted forecast to $35**, not the raw forecast to the model's future price. Get the comparison backwards and every stock looks underpriced.
- The short leg *loses* $2.20 on its own. The $0.95 is a **portfolio** property. Don't "fix" the negative leg.
- Factor exposures cancel **per factor**, not just in total. Check each λ's column separately.

### A7. What "arbitrage" means precisely

Not "a good trade" — a portfolio with **zero net investment, zero systematic risk, and a strictly positive expected payoff**. All three at once. If any is missing (you had to fund it, it carries factor risk, or the payoff can be negative), it is speculation, not arbitrage, and the law of one price says nothing about it.

### A8. Testing the APT: the evidence in five lines

- **Roll & Ross (1980):** 1,260 stocks (42 alphabetical portfolios of 30), daily 1962–72; factor analysis found **≤ 5** factors; **3–4** priced with RFR = 6%, only **2** consistently significant with λ0 free; factor structure similar across groups. Verdict: general support, tests "not conclusive".
- **Extensions:** the factor count moves with portfolio size (Dhrymes–Friend–Gultekin 1984) — awkward; Roll & Ross reply the count is secondary to explanatory power; DFG (1985) find **unique risk predicts returns**, which the theory forbids; Connor & Korajczyk (1993) and Harding (2008) refine the machinery.
- **Anomalies:** APT beats CAPM on the small-firm effect per Chen (1983), loses per Reinganum (1981); neither model explains **January** (Gultekin² 1987), though Burmeister–McElroy (1988) still reject CAPM *in favour of* APT.

### A9. Shanken's challenge — the two-sided coin

**Shanken (1982):** because APT never names its factors, *any* return data conforms to *some* factor structure. A supportive test "supports APT"; a failed test only convicts the chosen factors, never the theory. Ergo APT is **effectively untestable** — its factor silence, which shields it from Roll's critique, is simultaneously an escape hatch that no experiment can close. Ross rebutted; Jobson (1982, multivariate regression), Brown–Weinstein (1983, bilinear) and Geweke–Zhou (1996, Bayesian) proposed alternative test designs. A discussion answer that states **both directions** — the silence is the strength *and* the weakness — is a full-marks answer.

### A10. Multifactor models: naming the factors — macro school

In a **multifactor model** the investor *chooses* the number and identity of factors:

```
Rit = ai + [bi1·F1t + bi2·F2t + … + biK·FKt] + eit
```

**Chen, Roll & Ross (1986)** — the canonical macro set: the market return plus **MP** (industrial-production growth), **DEI** (change in inflation), **UI** (unanticipated inflation), **UPR** (credit-spread surprise), **UTS** (term-structure surprise). Two findings worth quoting: the factors' significance **shifted dramatically over time**, and **the market factor was never significant** once the macro factors entered.

**Burmeister, Roll & Ross (1994)** — five investor-facing risks: **confidence, time horizon, inflation, business cycle, market timing** (the S&P 500 residual). Small firms: more business-cycle and confidence exposure, less horizon exposure.

The unit's `Topic 4 Factor Models.xlsx` is the hands-on version — quarterly ASX returns against ABS **CPI** and **GDP** using `SLOPE`/`INTERCEPT`, with its own footnote admitting the b's *"should be estimated using a multi-factor model"* (one multivariate regression, not two univariate ones — remember that caveat if you cite the workbook).

### A11. Fama–French three-factor — the micro school and its signs

```
(Rit − RFRt) = ai + bi1(Rmt − RFRt) + bi2·SMBt + bi3·HMLt + eit
```

- **SMB** = small minus big: return on small caps minus large caps.
- **HML** = high minus low: return on high book-to-market (**value**) minus low book-to-market (**growth**).
- Both sides are **excess returns** — subtract RFR from the stock's return before regressing. Forgetting the subtraction is the most common practical error.

**The sign-reading table — memorise it:**

| Loading | Positive means | Negative means |
|---|---|---|
| SMB | behaves small-cap | behaves large-cap |
| HML | behaves value | behaves growth |

Checkpoint form: SMB −0.22, HML 0.89 → **large-cap value stock**. And the factor "often at odds with high CAPM betas" is **HML**: value stocks earn high returns on *low* betas — the exact cross-sectional failure that motivated the model.

### A12. Five factors, and the momentum controversy

**Fama–French (2015)** add **RMW** (robust-minus-weak profitability) and **CMA** (conservative-minus-aggressive investment) to the original three. **MOM (momentum) is deliberately excluded** — FF regard it as a short-term market phenomenon, not a long-term priced risk. The **four-factor** model in Tutorial 4 (three factors + MOM) is Carhart's — and Problem 9's implausibly large momentum premia (up to 9.79%) are a live demonstration of why FF left it out.

### A13. Estimating expected returns — the recipe, and the Excel drill

The four steps:

```
1. Choose the K factors
2. Estimate each factor's premium λj
3. Regress the stock's EXCESS returns on the factor series  →  the bij
4. E(Ri) − RFR = Σ bij·λj
```

The briefing drill, from `Topic 4 Fama French Factors.xlsx` (CSL, CBA, BHP, WBC vs Ken French's Asia-Pacific-ex-Japan daily factors):

1. Build the stock's daily return (`price_t/price_{t−1} − 1`), subtract `RF`.
2. **Data → Data Analysis → Regression**: Y = excess return column, X = the three factor columns `Mkt-RF`, `SMB`, `HML` selected *as one block*.
3. Read off: coefficients (the loadings), **t-stats** (significant if |t| ≳ 2), **R²** (fit). CSL's in-sample R² ≈ 0.38 — daily single-stock regressions never explain much more.
4. Interpret in words: "market beta ≈ 1; SMB negative → large-cap; HML positive → value tilt; factors explain ~38% of daily variation."
5. The workbook then compares `CORREL(actual, expected)` **in-sample vs out-of-sample** — the same honesty discipline as Topic 2's strategy testing: never grade a model on the data that built it.

### B1. Chapter 7, Question 7 — CAPM vs APT for new analysts

Both model expected return as a linear function of systematic risk. CAPM: one named factor (the market), heavy assumptions, equilibrium via everyone holding M — exposed to Roll's critique. APT: K unnamed factors, three assumptions, equilibrium via arbitrage — no benchmark needed. Prefer APT when returns respond to several distinct systematic influences you want to isolate or hedge, when no credible market proxy exists, or when CAPM's distributional assumptions are plainly violated; the price is having to identify and estimate the factors yourself.

### B2. Chapter 7, Question 9 — XYZ's coefficients

Market 1.2 → amplifies the market (aggressive). SMB −0.3 → behaves **large-cap**. HML +1.4 → strongly **value**. Likely holdings: high-beta large-capitalisation value stocks.

### B3. Chapter 7, Problem 9 — premia sensitivity

With the 15-year premia the stated answers reproduce: 3F **5.24 / 9.08 / 11.85** and 4F **6.07 / 10.97 / 8.62** (%; the text's 11.86/10.98/8.63 are rounding). 30-year premia: 3F 4.79 / 9.30 / 11.89; 4F 5.88 / 11.78 / 7.67. 80-year: 3F 5.64 / 9.95 / 13.82; 4F 6.89 / 12.80 / 8.98. ### B5. Chapter 7, Problem 14(a–b) — the regressions

```
A:  0.54(t 2.73) + 0.99·F1(t 21.9) − 0.20·F2(t −4.5) − 0.13·F3(t −1.8)   R² = 0.970

**C2.** Two-factor APT: λ0 = 3%, λ1 = 6%, λ2 = 2%. Stock P has b1 = 0.9, b2 = 1.5; stock Q has b1 = 1.4, b2 = −0.5. Expected returns?

P: 3 + 6(0.9) + 2(1.5)  = 3 + 5.4 + 3.0 = 11.4%
Q: 3 + 6(1.4) + 2(−0.5) = 3 + 8.4 − 1.0 = 10.4%
```

**C3.** In the deck's three-stock example, why is stock A shorted even though its price is expected to rise from $35 to $37.20?

<details><summary>Answer C3</summary>

Because $37.20 is *less* than the $37.70 its factor exposures require: discounting your forecast at the required 7.7% values A at $34.54 today, below its $35 market price. Overpricing is relative to **risk-required** return, not to whether the price goes up.

</details>

**C4.** Write the three conditions an arbitrage portfolio must satisfy, and verify them for weights (−1, +0.5, +0.5) on the deck's stocks A, B, C.

<details><summary>Answer C4</summary>

Σwi = 0; Σwi·bij = 0 for every factor j; Σwi·Ri > 0.

```
Wealth:   −35 + 17.5 + 17.5 = 0 ✓
Factor 1: −0.8 − 0.1 + 0.9 = 0 ✓      Factor 2: −0.9 + 0.65 + 0.25 = 0 ✓
Payoff:   −2.20 + 1.40 + 1.75 = +$0.95 ✓
```

</details>

**C5.** What did Roll & Ross (1980) conclude, and what is Shanken's (1982) objection?

<details><summary>Answer C5</summary>

Roll–Ross: factor analysis of 1,260 stocks found at most ~5 factors, 3–4 priced, similar across portfolio groups — evidence "generally supports" APT but is not conclusive. Shanken: since the factors are unnamed, equivalent return sets fit different factor structures; failure convicts only the chosen factors, never the theory — so the APT is effectively untestable.

</details>

**C6.** A stock's FF3 regression gives market 1.05, SMB +0.6, HML −0.7. Classify it, and state what must be true of the returns used as the dependent variable.

<details><summary>Answer C6</summary>

A roughly market-beta **small-cap growth** stock (positive SMB, negative HML). The dependent variable must be the stock's **excess** return (return minus RF), matching the excess-return form of the model.

</details>

**C7.** Name the two factors added in the 2015 five-factor model and the famous factor left out — and why.

<details><summary>Answer C7</summary>

**RMW** (robust-minus-weak profitability) and **CMA** (conservative-minus-aggressive investment). **Momentum** is excluded: Fama and French consider it a short-term market phenomenon rather than a long-term priced risk factor.

</details>

**C8.** In Problem 14, Portfolio B's Factor 2 beta is 0.05 with t = 0.77 while R² = 0.91. Reconcile.

<details><summary>Answer C8</summary>

The model as a whole fits superbly (91% of variation explained — almost all by Factor 1), but Factor 2 individually adds nothing distinguishable from zero for B. Joint fit and individual significance answer different questions, and correlated factors make the gap wider.

</details>

**C9.** Why should factor correlations be zero in theory, and what goes wrong at r = −0.75?

<details><summary>Answer C9</summary>

Each factor is meant to be an independent source of systematic risk (orthogonal, as PCA constructs them). At −0.75 two factors substantially measure the same risk: multicollinearity makes the estimated betas unstable with inflated standard errors, and the "premium per unit of this factor" interpretation fails because the factors don't move independently.

</details>

**C10.** The Topic 4 Factor Models workbook estimates b1 and b2 with separate SLOPE() calls. What's wrong with that, and what does the workbook itself say?

<details><summary>Answer C10</summary>

Univariate slopes ignore the correlation between CPI and GDP — each slope absorbs part of the other factor's effect. The sensitivities should come from **one multivariate regression** on both factors simultaneously (Data Analysis → Regression with two X columns). The workbook's own footnote concedes this: "* should be estimated using a multi-factor model".

</details>
