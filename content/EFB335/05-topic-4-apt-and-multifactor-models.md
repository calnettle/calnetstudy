## 4.1 Why the CAPM needed a successor

The CAPM is criticised on three fronts:

1. **Unrealistic assumptions** (the eight from Topic 3, §3.1).
2. **The benchmark problem** — difficulty selecting a proxy for the true market portfolio (Roll's critique, Topic 3 §3.7).
3. **Empirical failure** — firm-specific characteristics *other than beta* (size, book-to-market, leverage, P/E) show explanatory power for cross-sectional stock returns.

Ross's APT (1976) is the alternative with **fewer assumptions**.

### The three APT assumptions

1. Capital markets are **perfectly competitive**.
2. Investors always **prefer more wealth to less** with certainty.
3. Asset returns are generated by a **linear function of K factors** (and unsystematic risk can be diversified away).

What APT does **not** assume (all required by CAPM):

- normally distributed security returns
- quadratic utility functions
- a mean–variance efficient market portfolio

> **"State an assumption CAPM needs that APT doesn't" is the classic one-marker.** Any of the three above works. The deeper point: APT never mentions the market portfolio at all, so it is immune to Roll's benchmark critique — that is its whole reason for existing.

## 4.2 The APT model

```
E(Ri) = λ0 + λ1·bi1 + λ2·bi2 + … + λk·bik

  λ0   expected return on an asset with ZERO systematic risk
  λj   risk premium on the j-th common factor
  bij  sensitivity of asset i to factor j
```

### CAPM vs APT, side by side

| | CAPM | APT |
|---|---|---|
| Equation | `E(Ri) = RFR + βi[E(RM) − RFR]` | `E(Ri) = λ0 + λ1bi1 + … + λkbik` |
| Form | Linear | Linear |
| Number of risk factors | 1 | K (> 1) |
| Factor risk premium | `[E(RM) − RFR]` | `{λj}` |
| Factor sensitivity | `βi` | `{bij}` |
| "Zero-beta" return | RFR | λ0 |

The similarity: both are **linear** models pricing only **systematic** risk. The difference: APT has many factors — and, crucially, **does not name them**. Candidates the deck lists: inflation, growth in GNP, major political upheavals, changes in interest rates.

> **The primary challenge in using APT is identifying the risk factors.** That single sentence is the pivot of the whole topic — it motivates the empirical-testing debate (§4.5) and the macro/micro factor models (§4.6–4.7) that exist precisely to fill the naming gap. Done well, factor identification enables risk isolation and mandate-customised portfolios — the foundations of an "alpha" fund.

## 4.3 Using the APT — the two-factor worked example

Two factors: unanticipated inflation (λ1 = 0.02 — a 2% premium per unit of sensitivity) and unexpected real-GDP growth (λ2 = 0.03). Zero-beta return λ0 = 0.04.

| | Asset X | Asset Y |
|---|---|---|
| b·1 (inflation sensitivity) | 0.50 | 2.00 |
| b·2 (GDP sensitivity) | 1.50 | 1.75 |

```
E(R) = 0.04 + 0.02·bi1 + 0.03·bi2

E(Rx) = 0.04 + 0.02(0.50) + 0.03(1.50) = 0.095  =  9.5%
E(Ry) = 0.04 + 0.02(2.00) + 0.03(1.75) = 0.1325 = 13.25%
```

Verified. Y is more exposed to both factors, so it must offer the higher expected return.

## 4.4 Security valuation with the APT — the three-stock arbitrage

The deck's centrepiece. Three stocks, two factors, **λ0 = 0**, λ1 = 4%, λ2 = 5%. All three currently trade at **$35** and pay no dividend.

**Step 1 — expected returns and prices from the factor model:**

```
E(RA) = (0.8)(4%) + (0.9)(5%)  = 7.7%   →  E(PA) = 35 × 1.077 = $37.70
E(RB) = (−0.2)(4%) + (1.3)(5%) = 5.7%   →  E(PB) = 35 × 1.057 = $37.00
E(RC) = (1.8)(4%) + (0.5)(5%)  = 9.7%   →  E(PC) = 35 × 1.097 = $38.40
```

**Step 2 — your research says the actual prices in a year will differ:** $37.20, $37.80, $38.50. Discount those beliefs back at the model returns to get what each stock is *worth* today:

```
PA = 37.20 / 1.077 = $34.54     market price $35.00  →  OVERPRICED
PB = 37.80 / 1.057 = $35.76     market price $35.00  →  underpriced
PC = 38.50 / 1.097 = $35.10     market price $35.00  →  underpriced
```

**Step 3 — build the arbitrage portfolio: short 1 share of A, buy 0.5 of B and 0.5 of C.** The three defining conditions, checked:

```
Σwi = 0        (no net wealth invested)
   short 1 A: +$35.00 in;  long 0.5 B: −$17.50;  long 0.5 C: −$17.50  →  $0

Σwi·bij = 0    (no systematic risk, per factor)
   factor 1:  (−1)(0.8) + (0.5)(−0.2) + (0.5)(1.8) = 0
   factor 2:  (−1)(0.9) + (0.5)(1.3) + (0.5)(0.5) = 0

Σwi·Ri > 0     (positive return anyway)
   A: (−1)(37.20 − 35.00) = −2.20
   B: (0.5)(37.80 − 35.00) = +1.40
   C: (0.5)(38.50 − 35.00) = +1.75
                         net  +$0.95 per share of A shorted
```

All verified — the factor exposures cancel *exactly*, and the position pays $0.95 with no money down and no systematic risk. Arbitrage trading like this continues until the mispricing disappears: A is bid down toward $34.54, B and C bid up toward $35.76 and $35.10. APT rests on the **law of one price** — the same risk exposures must sell for the same price, or a free lunch exists and gets eaten.

> **Keep the two price columns straight.** $37.70 is the *market's model-implied* future price for A; $37.20 is *your forecast*. The stock is overpriced because your forecast is **below** the model's — you expect it to earn less than its risk requires. If you accidentally compare each forecast to $35 instead of to the discounted value, every classification comes out "underpriced" and the arbitrage vanishes. And note the short leg **loses** $2.20 on its own — the profit is a portfolio property, not a per-leg one.

## 4.5 Empirical tests of the APT

**Roll & Ross (1980)** — the founding test. Two steps: estimate factor loadings from time-series returns, then test the cross-sectional pricing relation. Data: daily returns 1962–72, 1,260 stocks in 42 alphabetical portfolios of 30. Factor analysis found **at most 5 meaningful factors**; with a 6% risk-free rate, **at least 3 (probably no more than 4)** priced factors; when λ0 was left free, only **2** were consistently significant. The factor structure looked similar across the 42 groups. Conclusion: evidence *generally supports* APT — but the tests are not conclusive. (The unit's `Topic 4 PCA.xls` demonstrates the principal-component machinery on 20 ASX stocks.)

**Extensions:** Cho, Elton & Gruber (1984) — how many factors are *priced*; Dhrymes, Friend & Gultekin (1984) — the number of factors **varies with portfolio size**, an uncomfortable result; Roll & Ross (1984) reply — the factor count is secondary to explanatory power; Dhrymes, Friend & Gultekin (1985) — **unique risk appeared to predict returns**, contradicting the theory; Connor & Korajczyk (1993) — a test allowing correlated unsystematic risk; Harding (2008) — links systematic and unsystematic factors.

**APT vs the anomalies:**

| Anomaly | Study | Verdict |
|---|---|---|
| Small-firm effect | Reinganum (1981) | Inconsistent with APT — small firms earn *positive* risk-adjusted returns, large firms negative |
| Small-firm effect | Chen (1983) | Supports APT over CAPM — APT explains the residuals CAPM leaves |
| January anomaly | Gultekin & Gultekin (1987) | APT no better than CAPM |
| January anomaly | Burmeister & McElroy (1988) | APT doesn't capture January either — but their test still **rejects CAPM in favour of APT** |

**Shanken's challenge (1982):** because APT never names its factors, *any* set of returns can be made to fit *some* factor structure — so a failed test can always be blamed on wrong factors, which means the theory is effectively **untestable**. If factors work, APT is "supported"; if they fail, APT hasn't failed — the factors have. Ross rebutted, and others proposed alternative testing frameworks (Jobson 1982 — multivariate regression; Brown & Weinstein 1983 — bilinear; Geweke & Zhou 1996 — Bayesian).

> **Shanken is the discussion-question centrepiece.** The strength of APT (unnamed factors, no market portfolio, so no Roll critique) is *precisely* its weakness (unfalsifiable factor structure). An answer that states both directions of that trade-off is a full-marks answer.

## 4.6 Multifactor models: the investor names the factors

```
Rit = ai + [bi1·F1t + bi2·F2t + … + biK·FKt] + eit

  Fjt  period-t return to the j-th DESIGNATED risk factor
  Rit  security i's nominal or excess return
```

The difference from pure APT: in a multifactor model **the investor chooses the number and identity of the factors**. Two schools:

- **Macroeconomic-based** — factors are economy-wide series.
- **Microeconomic-based** — factors are characteristics of the securities themselves.

### Macroeconomic models

**Chen, Roll & Ross (1986):** returns driven by the market index plus **MP** (monthly industrial-production growth), **DEI** (change in inflation), **UI** (unanticipated inflation), **UPR** (unanticipated credit-spread change), **UTS** (unanticipated term-structure shift). Findings: the economic significance of the macro factors **changed dramatically over time**, and — remarkably — **the market factor was never significant** once the macro factors were in.

**Burmeister, Roll & Ross (1994):** five named risks — **confidence risk**, **time-horizon risk**, **inflation risk**, **business-cycle risk**, and **market-timing risk** (the S&P 500 return left over after the other four). Smaller firms are more exposed to business-cycle and confidence risk, less to horizon risk.

The unit's `Topic 4 Factor Models.xlsx` runs a two-factor macro version on ASX stocks: quarterly returns against **ABS CPI changes** (b1) and **GDP growth** (b2) via `SLOPE`/`INTERCEPT`, then `E(R) = intercept + b1·λ1 + b2·λ2`. The workbook's own footnote concedes the shortcut: the b's are estimated one factor at a time and *"should be estimated using a multi-factor model"* — i.e. one multivariate regression, not two univariate ones. Its `Arbitrage` sheet then ranks all 20 stocks by two-factor E(R) and goes long the top 10 / short the bottom 10 with offsetting exposures — the §4.4 arbitrage logic industrialised (with its own on-sheet warning that the forecast return is *"artificially high"*).

### Microeconomic models — Fama & French

**The three-factor model (1993):**

```
(Rit − RFRt) = ai + bi1(Rmt − RFRt) + bi2·SMBt + bi3·HMLt + eit

  SMB  "small minus big"  — return on small-cap stocks minus large-cap stocks
  HML  "high minus low"   — return on high book-to-market (VALUE) stocks
                            minus low book-to-market (GROWTH) stocks
```

Both sides are **excess returns** — subtract the risk-free rate from the stock before regressing. A positive b2 means the stock behaves like a small-cap; a positive b3 means it behaves like a value stock.

**The five-factor model (2015)** adds:

```
  RMW  "robust minus weak"          — high-profitability firms minus low
  CMA  "conservative minus aggressive" — steady capital investment minus low
```

Controversially, **momentum (MOM) is excluded** — Fama and French regard it as a short-term market phenomenon, not a long-term risk factor. (The four-factor model in the tutorial — Carhart's — is the three factors *plus* MOM.)

> **Reading FF coefficients is a standing exam question.** SMB loading negative → behaves like a **large**-cap; HML loading positive → behaves like a **value** stock. The deck's checkpoint: SMB = −0.22 and HML = 0.89 signifies a **large-cap value stock**. And the factor premium "often at odds with high CAPM betas" is **HML** — value stocks tend to carry *low* betas yet earn *high* returns, which is exactly the cross-sectional failure that motivated the model.

## 4.7 Estimating expected returns in a multifactor setting

Four steps (the deck's recipe, and the briefing's calc-13/14 workflow):

1. Identify a set of **K common risk factors**.
2. Estimate the **risk premia** (λj) for the factors.
3. Estimate the **sensitivities** (bij) of the stock to each factor — a multivariate regression of excess returns on the factor series.
4. Combine: `E(Ri) − RFR = Σ bij·λj`.

The unit's `Topic 4 Fama French Factors.xlsx` does exactly this for CSL, CBA, BHP and WBC against Ken French's **Asia-Pacific-ex-Japan** daily three-factor series: Data Analysis → Regression of the stock's daily excess return on `Mkt-RF`, `SMB`, `HML` (CSL: R² ≈ 0.38 in-sample), then an expected-return column from the fitted coefficients, then `CORREL(actual, expected)` split **in-sample vs out-of-sample** — the same honest-evaluation discipline as Topic 2's strategy testing.

> **This workbook is the briefing rehearsal.** The sample briefing's factor regression asks for precisely this: regress a stock's excess returns on the supplied `Mkt-RF`, `SMB`, `HML` columns with the Data Analysis ToolPak, read the coefficients and R², and interpret the loadings in words. Practise on this workbook until the output table reads like a sentence: "market beta ~1, small negative SMB (large-cap), positive HML (value tilt), R² = 0.38 — factors explain about 38% of daily variation."

## Summary

- APT (Ross, 1976): expected return is a **linear combination of K systematic factor premia**, `E(Ri) = λ0 + Σλj·bij`, resting on the **law of one price** — arbitrage removes mispricing. Only three assumptions; no normality, no quadratic utility, no market portfolio.
- Pure APT proves factors exist but **never names them** — its immunity to Roll's critique and the root of Shanken's untestability challenge.
- The three-stock arbitrage: zero net wealth, zero factor exposure, positive return ($0.95) — until prices adjust to $34.54 / $35.76 / $35.10.
- Empirics: Roll–Ross found 3–5 factors and general support; extensions muddied the count; APT beats CAPM on some anomalies (Chen 1983) but not January.
- Multifactor models name the factors: **macro** (Chen–Roll–Ross's MP/DEI/UI/UPR/UTS; Burmeister–Roll–Ross's five risks) or **micro** (Fama–French: market, **SMB**, **HML**; plus **RMW** and **CMA** in the 2015 five-factor model; MOM deliberately excluded).
- Estimating: pick factors → estimate premia → regress excess returns for sensitivities → combine. Regression of **excess** returns, multivariate, with statistical significance and R² read off the output.
